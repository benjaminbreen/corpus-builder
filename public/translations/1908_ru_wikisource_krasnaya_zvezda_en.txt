THE BREAK It was then, when that great upheaval in our country, which is still ongoing and, I think, is now approaching its inevitable terrible end, was just beginning. Its first, bloody days so profoundly shook the public consciousness that everyone expected a swift and bright outcome to the struggle: it seemed that the worst had already happened, that nothing worse could happen. No one could imagine to what extent the bony hands of a dead man, who was strangling and still continues to strangle the living in his convulsive embrace, are tenacious. Combat excitement rapidly spread among the masses. People's souls were selflessly opened towards the future; the present blurred in a rosy mist, the past receded somewhere into the distance, disappearing from view. All human relationships became unstable and fragile as never before. In those days, what happened that turned my life upside down and tore me away from the flow of the people's struggle. I was, despite my twenty-seven years, one of the "old" party workers. Six years of work were attributed to me, with only a one-year break for prison. I felt the approach of the storm earlier than many others and met it more calmly than they did. I had to work much more than before; but at the same time, I did not abandon my scientific pursuits—the question of the structure of matter particularly interested me—nor my literary ones: I wrote for children's magazines, and this provided me with a livelihood. At the same time, I loved... or I thought I loved. Her party name was Anna Nikolaevna. She belonged to a different, more moderate current of our party. I explained this by the softness of her nature and the general confusion of political relations in our country; despite the fact that she was older than me, I considered her still not a fully formed person. In this, I was mistaken. [...] And yet, I did not foresee or suppose the inevitability of the break—when an external influence penetrated our life, which hastened the denouement. Around this time, a young man arrived in the capital, who bore an unusual clandestine name for us: Manny. He brought some messages and assignments from the South, from which it was clear that he enjoyed the full trust of his comrades. Having completed his business, he decided to stay in the capital for some time and began to visit us often, showing a clear inclination to become closer to me. He was an original man in many ways, starting with his appearance. His eyes were so masked by very dark glasses that I didn't even know their color; his head was somewhat disproportionately large; the features of his face, beautiful but surprisingly immobile and lifeless, did not harmonize at all with his soft and expressive voice, just as they did not harmonize with his slender, youthful, flexible figure. His speech was free and fluent and always full of content. His scientific education was very one-sided; by specialty, he was apparently an engineer. In conversation, Manny had a tendency to constantly reduce specific and practical issues to general ideological foundations. When he visited us, it always turned out that the contradictions between my wife's and my natures and views quickly came to the fore so clearly and vividly that we began to painfully feel their hopelessness. Manny's worldview was apparently similar to mine; he always expressed himself very softly and cautiously in form, but just as sharply and deeply in essence. He managed to so skillfully connect our political disagreements with Anna Nikolaevna with the fundamental difference in our worldviews that these disagreements seemed psychologically inevitable, almost logical conclusions from them, and all hope of influencing each other, smoothing out contradictions, and arriving at something common disappeared. Anna Nikolaevna harbored something akin to hatred mixed with lively interest towards Manny. He inspired great respect and vague distrust in me: I felt that he was heading towards some goal, but I couldn't understand which one. On one of the January days—it was already the end of January—an discussion was to take place in the leading groups of both party currents on a project for a mass demonstration with a probable outcome in armed conflict. The evening before, Manny came to us and raised the issue of the party leaders themselves participating in this demonstration, if it were decided upon. A dispute ensued, which quickly took on a heated character. Anna Nikolaevna stated that anyone who votes for the demonstration is morally obliged to be in the front ranks. I believed that this was not at all necessary in general, and that one should go where he is needed or where he can be seriously useful, and I had myself in mind, as a person with some experience in such matters. Manny went further and argued that, in view of the apparently inevitable clash with the troops, street agitators and combat organizers should be present on the field of action, while political leaders have no place there at all, and physically weak and nervous people can even be very harmful. Anna Nikolaevna was directly offended by these arguments, which seemed directed specifically at her. She broke off the conversation and went to her room. Soon Manny also left. The next day I had to get up early and leave without saying goodbye to Anna Nikolaevna, and return only in the evening. The demonstration was rejected both in our committee and, as I learned, in the leading collective of the other current. I was pleased with this, because I knew how insufficient the preparation was for an armed conflict and considered such an action a fruitless waste of forces. It seemed to me that this decision would somewhat alleviate Anna Nikolaevna's irritation from yesterday's conversation. On my desk, I found a note from Anna Nikolaevna: "I am leaving. The more I understand myself and you, the clearer it becomes to me that we are going down different paths and that we both made a mistake. It is better for us not to meet again. Forgive me." I wandered the streets for a long time, weary, with a feeling of emptiness in my head and cold in my heart. When I returned home, I found an unexpected guest there: Manny was sitting at my table, writing a note. 2. INVITATION — I need to talk to you about something very serious and somewhat strange, — said Manny. It didn't matter to me; I sat down and prepared to listen. — I read your brochure about electrons and matter, — he began. — I myself have studied this issue for several years and believe that your brochure contains many correct ideas. I bowed silently. He continued: — In this work, you have one remark that is particularly interesting to me. You made a supposition that the electric theory of matter, necessarily representing the force of gravity as a derivative of electrical forces of attraction and repulsion, must lead to the discovery of gravity with a different sign, that is, to obtaining a type of matter that repels, rather than attracts, the Earth, the Sun, and other bodies familiar to us; you pointed to diamagnetic repulsion of bodies and repulsion of parallel currents in different directions for comparison. All this is said in passing, but I think you yourself attached more importance to it than you intended to reveal. — You are right, — I replied, — and I think it is precisely on this path that humanity will solve both the problem of completely free air travel, and then the problem of communication between planets. But whether this idea itself is correct or not, it is completely fruitless until there is an exact theory of matter and gravity. If another type of matter does exist, then it is obviously impossible to simply find it: it has long been removed from the entire solar system by the force of repulsion, and even more certainly—it did not enter its composition when it began to organize in the form of a nebula. Therefore, this type of matter must still be theoretically constructed and then practically reproduced. Now, there are no data for this, and in essence, one can only anticipate the task itself. — Nevertheless, this task has already been solved, — said Manny. I looked at him with surprise. His face was still immobile, but there was something in his tone that did not allow me to consider him a charlatan. "Perhaps he is mentally ill," I thought. — I have no need to deceive you, and I know well what I am saying, — he replied to my thought. — Listen to me patiently, and then, if necessary, I will present proof. — And he told the following: — The great discovery in question was not made by the efforts of an individual. It belongs to an entire scientific society, which has existed for quite some time and has worked in this direction for a long time. This society has been secret until now, and I am not authorized to introduce you closer to its origin and history until we can agree on the main point. Our society has significantly surpassed the academic world in many important scientific issues. Radiating elements and their distribution were known to us much earlier than Curie and Ramsay, and our comrades managed to conduct a much further and deeper analysis of the structure of matter. On this path, the possibility of the existence of elements repelled by terrestrial bodies was foreseen, and then the synthesis of this "minus-matter," as we briefly denote it, was carried out. After that, it was not difficult to develop and implement the technical applications of this discovery—first, flying machines for movement in the Earth's atmosphere, and then for communication with other planets. Despite Manny's calm and convincing tone, his story seemed too strange and implausible to me. — And you managed to accomplish all this and keep it a secret? — I remarked, interrupting his speech. — Yes, because we considered it extremely important. We believed that it would be very dangerous to publish our scientific discoveries while reactionary governments remain in most countries. And you, a Russian revolutionary, should agree with us more than anyone. Look how your Asian state uses European means of communication and methods of extermination to suppress and eradicate everything living and progressive within you. Is the government of that semi-feudal, semi-constitutional country, whose throne is occupied by a warlike, babbling fool, ruled by noble swindlers, much better? And what are even the two bourgeois republics of Europe worth? And yet, it is clear that if our flying machines became known, governments would first and foremost take care to monopolize them and use them to strengthen the power and might of the upper classes. We absolutely do not wish this and therefore retain the monopoly for ourselves, awaiting more suitable conditions. — And have you really managed to reach other planets? — I asked. — Yes, the two closest, telluric planets, Venus and Mars, not counting, of course, the dead Moon. We are currently engaged in their detailed study. We have all the necessary means; we need strong and reliable people. By the authority of my comrades, I offer you to join our ranks, — of course, with all the rights and obligations arising from this. He paused, awaiting an answer. I didn't know what to think. — Proof! — I said. — You promised to present proof. Manny took a glass vial from his pocket with some metallic liquid, which I took for mercury. But strangely, this liquid, which filled no more than a third of the vial, was not at the bottom, but in the upper part, near the neck, and in the neck all the way to the stopper. Manny turned the vial upside down, and the liquid poured to the bottom, that is, directly upwards. Manny let the vial out of his hands, and it hung in the air. It was incredible, but undeniable and obvious. — This vial is made of ordinary glass, — explained Manny, — and it is filled with a liquid that is repelled by the bodies of the solar system. There is exactly enough liquid to balance the weight of the vial; thus, both together have no weight. According to this method, we also construct all flying machines: they are made of ordinary materials but contain a reservoir filled with a sufficient amount of "matter of negative type." Then, it remains to give this entire weightless system the proper speed of movement. For terrestrial flying machines, simple electric motors with propellers are used; for interplanetary travel, this method is, of course, unsuitable, and here we use a completely different method, with which I can familiarize you later. There was no longer any room for doubt. — What limitations does your society impose on those who join it, besides, of course, the mandatory secrecy? — Generally, almost none. The personal lives and social activities of the comrades are not restricted in any way, as long as they do not harm the activities of the society as a whole. But each person must fulfill some important and responsible assignment of the society upon joining. In this way, on the one hand, their connection with society is strengthened, and on the other hand, the level of their abilities and energy is revealed in practice. — So, I will be offered such an assignment right away? — Yes. — What exactly? — You must participate in the expedition of the great eteronef [eteronef – spaceship (literally – ship for traveling through the ether); ether – ether (Greek)] to the planet Mars, which departs tomorrow. — How long will the expedition be? — It is unknown. The one-way trip there and back takes at least five months. One may not return at all. — I understand that; that's not the point. But what about my revolutionary work? You yourself are apparently a social democrat and will understand my difficulty. — Choose. We consider a break in work necessary to complete your preparation. The assignment cannot be postponed. Refusal of it is a refusal of everything. I pondered. With the broad masses coming onto the stage, the removal of one worker or another is an absolutely insignificant fact for the cause as a whole. Moreover, this removal is temporary, and upon returning to work, I will be much more useful to it with my new connections, knowledge, and means. I decided. — When should I leave? — Now, with me. — Will you give me two hours to notify my comrades? I need to be replaced in the district tomorrow. — This is almost done. Andrei, who fled from the South, arrived today. I warned him that you might leave, and he is ready to take your place. Waiting for you, I wrote him a letter with detailed instructions in case. We can take this letter to him on the way. There was nothing more to discuss. I quickly destroyed unnecessary papers, wrote a note to my landlady, and began to dress. Manny was already ready. — So, let's go. From this moment on, I am your prisoner. — You are my comrade, — replied Manny. 3. NIGHT Manny's apartment occupied the entire fifth floor of a large building, standing apart among the small houses on the outskirts of the capital. No one met us. The rooms we walked through were empty, and in the bright light of the electric lamps, this emptiness seemed particularly gloomy and unnatural. In the third room, Manny stopped. — Here, — he pointed to the door of the fourth room, — is the airboat in which we will now travel to the great eteronef. But first, I must undergo a small transformation. In this mask, it would be difficult for me to steer the gondola. He unbuttoned his collar and removed, along with his glasses, the amazingly made mask that I, like everyone else, had taken for his face until this moment. I was struck by what I saw. His eyes were monstrously huge, as human eyes never are. Their pupils were dilated even compared to the unnatural size of the eyes themselves, making their expression almost frightening. The upper part of his face and head was as wide as was necessary to accommodate such eyes; on the contrary, the lower part of his face, without any signs of beard and mustache, was relatively small. The whole produced an impression of extreme originality, perhaps ugliness, but not a caricature. — You see what appearance nature has given me, — said Manny. — You understand that I must hide it, if only for fear of frightening people, not to mention the demands of conspiracy. But you will have to get used to my ugliness; you will necessarily spend a lot of time with me. He opened the door to the next room and illuminated it. It was a spacious hall. In the middle lay a small, quite wide boat, made of metal and glass. In its front part, the sides and bottom were glass, with steel frames; this transparent wall, two centimeters thick, was obviously very strong. Above the bow sides, two flat crystal plates, joined at an acute angle, were intended to cut through the air and protect passengers from the wind at high speed. The engine occupied the middle part of the boat, and a three-bladed propeller half a meter wide was located at the stern. The front half of the boat, along with the engine, was covered from above by a thin, laminated canopy, attached to the metal trim of the glass sides and light steel columns. The whole thing was as elegant as a toy. Manny invited me to sit on the side bench of the gondola, turned off the electric light, and opened the huge window of the hall. He himself sat in front near the engine and threw out several ballast bags lying at the bottom of the boat. Then he put his hand on the engine lever. The boat vibrated, slowly rose, and quietly slipped out of the open window. — Thanks to minus-matter, our airplanes do not require fragile and clumsy wings, — noted Manny. I sat as if nailed, not daring to move. The noise of the propeller became louder, the cold winter air rushed under the canopy, pleasantly refreshing my burning face, but powerless to penetrate my clothes. Thousands of stars twinkled and shimmered above us, and below... I saw through the transparent bottom of the gondola how the black patches of houses diminished and the bright points of the city's electric lights receded into the distance, and the snowy plains glowed dimly below us with a dull bluish-white light. The dizziness, initially light and almost pleasant, grew stronger, and I closed my eyes to get rid of it. The air became sharper, the noise of the propeller and the whistling of the wind higher—obviously, the speed of movement was increasing. Soon, among these sounds, my ear began to distinguish a thin, continuous, and very even silvery ringing—the glass wall of the gondola trembled, cutting through the air. A strange music filled my consciousness, thoughts tangled and receded, leaving only a feeling of elemental lightness and freedom of movement, carrying me forward and forward, into infinite space. — Four kilometers per minute, — said Manny, and I opened my eyes. — Is it far yet? — I asked. — About an hour's journey, over the ice of one lake. We were at an altitude of several hundred meters, and the boat flew horizontally, without descending or ascending. My eyes were getting used to the darkness, and I saw more and more clearly. We entered the land of lakes and granite cliffs. These cliffs were black in places, free from snow. Villages huddled between them here and there. To our left, behind us, the snowy field of a frozen bay remained in the distance; to the right—the white plains of a vast lake. In this lifeless winter landscape, I was to break my ties with the old earth. And suddenly I felt—not doubt, no, but a real certainty—that this was a break forever... The gondola slowly descended between the rocks, into a small bay of a mountain lake, before a dark structure that rose from the snow. No windows or doors were visible. Part of the metal shell of the structure slowly slid aside, revealing a black opening into which our boat floated. Then the opening closed again, and the space where we were was illuminated by electric light. It was a large elongated room without furniture; a mass of ballast bags lay on its floor. Manny attached the gondola to specially designated columns and opened one of the side doors. It led into a long, dimly lit corridor. On its sides, apparently, were cabins. Manny led me into one of them and said: — Here is your cabin. Make yourself at home, and I will go to the engine room. See you in the morning. I was glad to be alone. Despite all the excitement caused by the strange events of the evening, fatigue made itself felt; I did not touch the supper prepared for me on the table and, having turned off the lamp, went to bed. My thoughts were absurdly mixed, shifting from one subject to another in the most unexpected way. I stubbornly forced myself to sleep, but I could not do so for a long time. Finally, my consciousness became vague; hazy, unstable images began to crowd before my eyes, the surroundings receded somewhere far away, and heavy dreams took possession of my brain. The chain of dreams ended in a terrible nightmare. I stood on the edge of a huge black abyss, at the bottom of which stars shone, and Manny, with irresistible force, was dragging me down, saying that there was no need to fear gravity and that after a few hundred thousand years of falling, we would reach the nearest star. I groaned in a tortuous last struggle and woke up. A soft blue light filled my room. Sitting on the bed beside me and leaning towards me was... Manny? Yes, he, but ghostly strange and as if different: it seemed to me that he had become much smaller, and his eyes did not protrude so sharply from his face; he had a soft, kind expression, not cold and unyielding, as at the edge of the abyss... — How good you are... — I uttered, dimly realizing this change. He smiled and placed his hand on my forehead. It was a small, soft hand. I closed my eyes again and, with the absurd thought that I had to kiss this hand, fell into a calm, blissful sleep. 4. EXPLANATION When I woke up and illuminated the room, the clock showed ten. After finishing my toilet, I pressed the bell button, and a minute later Manny entered the room. — Are we leaving soon? — I asked. — In an hour, — replied Manny. — Did you come to me last night, or did I just dream it? — No, it wasn't a dream; but it wasn't me who came, but our young doctor, Natty. You slept poorly and restlessly; he had to put you to sleep with blue light and suggestion. — Is he your brother? — No, — Manny said, smiling. — You still haven't told me what your nationality is... Do your other comrades belong to the same type as you? — Yes, — Manny replied. — So you deceived me, — I stated sharply. — This is not a scientific society, but something else? — Yes, — Manny said calmly. — We are all inhabitants of another planet, representatives of another humanity. We are Martians. — Why did you deceive me? — And would you have listened to me if I had told you the whole truth right away? I had too little time to convince you. I had to distort the truth for the sake of plausibility. Without this transitional stage, your consciousness would have been excessively shaken. But in essence, I told the truth—regarding the upcoming journey. — So, I am still your prisoner? — No, you are still free. You have an hour to decide. If you refuse during this time, we will take you back, and postpone the journey, because there is no point for us to return alone now. — Why do you need me? — To serve as a living link between our and earthly humanity, to familiarize myself with our way of life and to familiarize Martians more closely with earthly life, to be—as long as you wish—a representative of your planet in our world. — Is this the whole truth? — Yes, completely, if you prove capable of fulfilling this role. — In that case, I must try. I will stay with you. — Is this your final decision? — Manny asked. — Yes, if your latest explanation is not some... transitional stage. — So, we're going, — said Manny, ignoring my jab. — I will now go and give the engineer the final instructions, and then I will return to you, and we will go together to observe the departure of the eteronef. He left, and I surrendered myself to reflection. Our explanation, in essence, was not fully complete. There was still one question, and quite a serious one; but I did not dare to propose it to Manny. Did he consciously contribute to my break with Anna Nikolaevna? It seemed to me so. Probably, he saw an obstacle to his goal in her. Perhaps he was right. In any case, he could only hasten this break, not create it. Of course, this was also an audacious interference in my personal affairs. But now I was already connected with Manny and had to suppress my hostility towards him anyway. So, there was no point in dwelling on the past; it was best not to think about this question at all. On the whole, the new turn of events did not surprise me too much: sleep had strengthened my forces, and it was already quite difficult to surprise me after everything I had experienced the day before. I just had to develop a plan for further action. The task was, obviously, to orient myself in my new surroundings as quickly and fully as possible. The best thing was to start with the closest and move step by step to the more distant. The closest was the eteronef, its inhabitants, and the journey that was beginning. Mars was still far away: at least two months away, as one could infer from Manny's words yesterday. I had managed to notice the external form of the eteronef the day before: it was almost a sphere with a flattened segment at the bottom, like an egg standing on end—a shape calculated, of course, to provide the largest volume for the smallest surface area, i.e., minimum material consumption and minimum cooling area. As for the material, aluminum and glass apparently predominated. Manny was to show and explain the internal structure to me, and he was also to introduce me to all the other "monsters," as I mentally called my new companions. Returning, Manny led me to the other Martians. They had all gathered in a side hall with a huge crystal window occupying half the wall. The real sunlight was very pleasant after the ghostly light of the electric lamps. There were about twenty Martians, and they all looked alike, as it seemed to me at the time. The absence of beards, mustaches, and wrinkles on their faces almost erased even the difference in age. I involuntarily followed Manny with my eyes so as not to lose him among this company, which was alien to me. However, I soon began to distinguish my visitor, Natty, from among them, who stood out for his youth and liveliness, as well as the broad-shouldered giant Sterney, who struck me with a strangely cold, almost sinister expression. Besides Manny himself, only Natty spoke Russian with me; Sterney and three or four others spoke French; others spoke English and German; among themselves, they spoke in a language that was completely new to me—obviously, their native language. This language was resonant and beautiful, and, as I noticed with pleasure, it seemed to present no particular difficulties in pronunciation. 5. DEPARTURE As interesting as the "monsters" were, my main attention was involuntarily drawn to the approaching solemn moment of "departure." I looked intently at the snowy surface before us and at the sheer granite wall rising behind it. I expected to feel a sharp jolt any moment and for all this to quickly blur, receding from us. But nothing of the sort happened. A silent, slow, barely noticeable movement gradually began to separate us from the snowy area. For a few seconds, the ascent was barely noticeable. — Acceleration is two centimeters, — said Manny. I understood what this meant. In the first second, we would travel only one centimeter, in the second, three, in the third, five, in the fourth, seven centimeters; and the speed was to change all the time, continuously increasing according to the law of arithmetic progression. In a minute, we would reach the speed of a walking person, in 15 minutes—a courier train, and so on. We were moving according to the law of falling bodies, but we were falling upwards and 500 times slower than ordinary heavy bodies falling near the Earth's surface. The glass plate of the window started from the floor itself and formed an obtuse angle with it, corresponding to the direction of the eteronef's spherical surface, of which it was a part. Thanks to this, we could, leaning forward, see what was directly below us. The Earth receded faster and faster from beneath us, and the horizon expanded. The dark patches of rocks and villages diminished, the outlines of the lakes appeared as on a map. And the sky became darker and darker; and at the same time as a blue strip of unfrozen sea occupied the western side of the horizon, my eyes were already beginning to distinguish the brightest stars in the midday sunlight. A very slow rotational movement of the eteronef around its vertical axis allowed us to see the entire surrounding space. It seemed to us that the horizon was rising with us, and the earthly expanse below us appeared as a huge concave saucer with embossed decorations. Their outlines became smaller, the relief flatter, the entire landscape took on more and more the character of a geographical map, sharply drawn in the center, blurred and indistinct at its edges, where everything was obscured by a translucent bluish mist. And the sky became completely black, and countless stars, down to the smallest, shone on it with a calm, unwavering light, not fearing the bright sun, whose rays became painfully hot. — Tell me, Manny, will this acceleration of two centimeters, with which we are currently moving, continue throughout the journey? — Yes, — he replied, — only its direction will be about 50 kilometers per second, and the average—about 25 kilometers, but by the moment of arrival, it will become as small as it was at the very beginning of the journey, and we will descend to the surface of Mars without any jolt or vibration. Without these enormous variable speeds, we would not have been able to reach either the Earth or Venus, because even their closest distances—60 and 100 million kilometers—at the speed of your trains, for example, would only be possible to cover in centuries, not months, as we will do. As for the "cannon shot" method, which I read about in your fantasy novels, that is, of course, a simple joke, because according to the laws of mechanics, it is practically the same thing—whether to be inside the projectile during the shot or to receive the projectile inside. — And by what means do you achieve such uniform deceleration and acceleration? — The driving force of the eteronef is one of the radioactive substances that we manage to obtain in large quantities. We have found a way to accelerate the decomposition of its elements hundreds of thousands of times; this is done in our engines using fairly simple electrochemical methods. Thus, a huge amount of energy is released. The particles of decaying atoms fly apart, as you know, at a speed tens of thousands of times greater than that of artillery shells. When these particles can only fly out of the eteronef in one specific direction, that is, through one channel with walls impermeable to them, then the entire eteronef moves in the opposite direction, as is the case with the recoil of a rifle or cannon. According to the law of live forces that you know, you can easily calculate that a small fraction of a milligram of such particles per second is quite enough to give our eteronef its uniformly accelerated motion. During our conversation, all the Martians disappeared from the hall. Manny invited me to have breakfast in his cabin. I went with him. His cabin adjoined the wall of the eteronef and had a large crystal window. We continued our conversation. I knew that I would have new, untested sensations in the form of loss of my body's weight, and I asked Manny about it. — Yes, — said Manny, — although the Sun continues to attract us, here this effect is negligible. The influence of the Earth will also become unnoticeable tomorrow or the day after. Only thanks to the continuous acceleration of the eteronef will we retain 1/400—1/500 of our previous weight. It is not easy to get used to this for the first time, although the change happens very gradually. Gaining lightness, you will make many miscalculated movements that miss the target. The pleasure of flying through the air will seem very questionable to you. As for the inevitable heart palpitations, dizziness, and even nausea, Natty will help you get rid of them. It will also be difficult to handle water and other liquids, which will escape from containers at the slightest jolt and scatter everywhere in the form of huge spherical droplets. But we have everything carefully adapted to eliminate these inconvenconveniences: furniture and dishes are fixed in place, liquids are kept sealed, handles and straps are attached everywhere to stop involuntary flights during sharp movements. In general, you will get used to it; there will be enough time for this. About two hours had passed since departure, and the decrease in weight was already quite noticeable, although still very pleasant: the body became lighter, movements freer, and nothing more. We had completely passed through the atmosphere, but this did not bother us, as our hermetically sealed ship naturally had a sufficient supply of oxygen. The visible area of the Earth's surface had finally begun to resemble a geographical map, albeit with a confused scale: larger in the center, smaller towards the horizon; in places, it was covered by white clouds. To the south, beyond the Mediterranean Sea, North Africa and Arabia were quite clearly visible through the blue haze; to the north, beyond Scandinavia, the view was lost in a snowy and icy desert, only the rocks of Spitsbergen still stood out as dark patches. To the east, beyond the greenish-brown strip of the Urals, punctuated here and there by snow patches, the continuous realm of white began again, with a greenish tint in places—a faint reminder of the vast Siberian coniferous forests. To the west, beyond the clear outlines of Central Europe, the contours of the coasts of England and Northern France were lost in the clouds. I could not look at this giant picture for long, as the thought of the terrifying depth of the abyss over which we were located quickly evoked a feeling close to fainting. I resumed the conversation with Manny. — You are the captain of this ship, aren't you? Manny nodded affirmatively and remarked: — But this does not mean that I possess what you call the authority of a commander. It's just that I am the most experienced in operating an eteronef, and my instructions are accepted just as I accept the astronomical calculations performed by Sterney, or as we all accept Natty's medical advice to maintain our health and working capacity. — And how old is this Doctor Natty? He seems very young to me. — I don't remember, sixteen or seventeen, — Manny replied with a smile. That's roughly what I thought. But I couldn't help but be surprised by such early erudition. — And at this age, to be a doctor already! — I exclaimed involuntarily. — And add: a knowledgeable and experienced doctor, — Manny added. At that time, I didn't realize, and Manny intentionally didn't remind me, that Martian years are almost twice as long as ours: Mars' orbit around the Sun takes 686 of our days, and 16 Martian years are equivalent to 30 Earth years. 6. THE ETERONEF After breakfast, Manny took me on a tour of our "ship." First, we headed to the engine room. It occupied the lower floor of the eteronef, adjoining its flattened bottom, and was divided by partitions into five rooms—one central and four side ones. In the center of the central room stood the main engine, and around it, on all four sides, there were circular glass windows in the floor, one of pure crystal, three of colored glass of various colors; the glasses were three centimeters thick, remarkably transparent. At the moment, we could only see a part of the Earth's surface through them. The main part of the engine was a vertical metal cylinder three meters high and half a meter in diameter, made, as Manny explained to me, of osmium—a very refractory, noble metal related to platinum. In this cylinder, the decomposition of radioactive matter took place; the walls, heated red-hot to a thickness of 20 centimeters, clearly indicated the energy of this process. And yet, it was not too hot in the room: the entire cylinder was surrounded by a twice-as-wide casing of some transparent substance, perfectly protecting from heat; and at the top, this casing was connected to pipes through which the heated air was drawn off in all directions for uniform "heating" of the eteronef. The other parts of the engine, connected in various ways to the cylinder—electric coils, accumulators, indicators with dials, etc.—were arranged around in a beautiful order, and the duty engineer, thanks to a system of mirrors, could see them all at once without leaving his seat. Of the side rooms, one was the "astronomical," to its right and left were the "water" and "oxygen" rooms, and on the opposite side—the "computational." In the astronomical room, the floor and the outer wall were entirely crystal, made of geometrically polished glass of ideal purity. Their transparency was such that when I, following Manny across the air bridges, dared to look straight down, I saw nothing between myself and the abyss stretching below us—I had to close my eyes to stop the agonizing dizziness. I tried to look at the instruments arranged in the network of bridges, on complex tripods descending from the ceiling and inner walls of the room. The main telescope was about two meters long, but with a disproportionately large objective and obviously the same optical power. — We only use diamond eyepieces, — said Manny, — they provide the largest field of view. — How significant is the usual magnification of this telescope? — I asked. — Clear magnification is about 600 times, — Manny replied, — but when it is insufficient, we photograph the field of view and examine the photograph under a microscope. By this method, the magnification is effectively brought up to 60 thousand and more; and the ground-based photography takes less than a minute for us. Manny invited me to look at the departing Earth through the telescope immediately. He himself directed the tube. — The distance is now about two thousand kilometers, — he said. — Do you recognize what is before you? I immediately recognized the harbor of the Scandinavian capital, where I often passed through for party business. It was interesting for me to examine the steamships in the roadstead. Manny, with a turn of a side knob on the telescope, replaced the eyepiece with a photographic camera, and after a few seconds removed it from the telescope and transferred it entirely to a large apparatus standing nearby, which turned out to be a microscope. — We develop and fix the image right here in the microscope, without touching the plate with our hands, — he explained, and after a few minor operations, in about half a minute, he handed me the microscope eyepiece. I saw the steamship of the Northern Society, familiar to me, with amazing clarity, as if it were a few dozen steps away from me; the image in transmitted light seemed three-dimensional and had a completely natural color. The gray-haired captain, with whom I had spoken more than once during my trips, stood on the bridge. A sailor lowering a large box onto the deck seemed frozen in his pose, as did a passenger pointing something out to him with his hand. And all this was at a distance of two thousand kilometers... A young Martian, Sterney's assistant, entered the room. He needed to take an accurate measurement of the distance traveled by the eteronef. We did not want to disturb him in his work and moved on, into the "water" room. There was a huge reservoir of water and large apparatus for its purification. Numerous pipes carried this water from the reservoir throughout the eteronef. Next was the "computational" room. There stood incomprehensible machines with many dials and needles. Sterney was working behind the largest machine. A long tape extended from it, apparently containing the results of Sterney's calculations; but the symbols on it, like on all the dials, were unfamiliar to me. I did not want to disturb Sterney or talk to him at all. We quickly passed into the last side compartment. This was the "oxygen" room. It stored oxygen supplies in the form of 25 tons of bertol salt, from which 10 thousand cubic meters of oxygen could be extracted as needed: this amount is sufficient for several journeys like ours. There were also apparatus for decomposing bertol salt. Further, there were also supplies of barite and caustic potash for absorbing carbon dioxide from the air, as well as supplies of sulfur anhydride for absorbing excess moisture and volatile leucomain—that physiological poison that is released during respiration and is incomparably more harmful than carbon dioxide. Doctor Natty was in charge of this room. Then we returned to the central engine room and from there, in a small elevator, we went up to the very top floor of the eteronef. There, the central room was occupied by a second observatory, similar in all respects to the lower one, but only with a crystal shell on top, not below, and with larger instruments. From this observatory, the other half of the celestial sphere was visible along with the "planet of destination." Mars shone with its reddish light away from the zenith. Manny directed the telescope there, and I clearly saw the outlines of continents, seas, and the network of canals familiar to me from Schiaparelli's maps. Manny photographed the planet, and a detailed map appeared under the microscope. But I could not understand anything in it without Manny's explanation: the patches of cities, forests, and lakes differed from each other in subtle and incomprehensible details to me. — How great is the distance? — I asked. — Now it's relatively close—about one hundred million kilometers. — And why isn't Mars at the zenith of the dome? Are we not flying directly towards it, but sideways? — Yes, and we cannot do otherwise. When leaving Earth, due to inertia, we, among other things, retain its speed of movement around the Sun—30 kilometers per second. Mars' speed is only 24 kilometers, and if we were to fly perpendicular to both orbits, we would hit the surface of Mars with a residual lateral velocity of 6 kilometers per second. This is very inconvenient, and we must choose a curvilinear path on which the excess lateral velocity is balanced. — How long, in that case, is our entire path? — About 160 million kilometers, which will require at least two and a half months. If I were not a mathematician, these figures would not have said anything to my heart. But now they evoked in me a feeling close to a nightmare, and I hurried to leave the astronomical room. The six side compartments of the upper segment, encircling the observatory, were entirely without windows, and their ceiling, representing part of the spherical surface, sloped down to the floor. Large reservoirs of "minus-matter" were located near the ceiling, the repulsion of which was to paralyze the weight of the entire eteronef. The middle floors—third and second—were occupied by common halls, laboratories of individual expedition members, their cabins, baths, library, gymnasium, etc. Natty's cabin was next to mine. 7. PEOPLE The loss of gravity was increasingly making itself felt. The growing sense of lightness ceased to be pleasant. It was joined by an element of uncertainty and a kind of vague anxiety. I went to my room and lay down on the bunk. Two hours of calm position and intense reflection led me to fall asleep imperceptibly. When I woke up, Natty was sitting at the table in my room. I involuntarily rose from the bed with a sharp movement and, as if thrown upwards by something, hit my head on the ceiling. — When you weigh less than twenty pounds, you need to be careful, — Natty remarked in a good-naturedly philosophical tone. He came to me with the specific purpose of giving all necessary instructions in case of that "seasickness," which had already begun in me from the loss of weight. There was a special signal bell in the cabin to his room, with which I could always call him if his help was needed again. I took the opportunity to talk to the young doctor—I was somehow involuntarily drawn to this pleasant, very learned, but also very cheerful boy. I asked him why it happened that out of the entire company of Martians on the eteronef, he alone, besides Manny, knew my native language. — It's very simple, — he explained. — When we were looking for a person, Manny chose himself and me for your country, and we spent more than a year there until we managed to finish this business with you. — So, did others "look for a person" in other countries? — Of course, among all the major peoples of the Earth. But, as Manny predicted, it was most likely to be found in your country, where life is lived most energetically and brightly, where people are forced to look forward more. Having found a person, we informed the others; they gathered from all countries; and here we are, traveling. — What do you actually mean when you say: "were looking for a person," "found a person"? I understand that it was about a subject suitable for a certain role—Manny explained to me which one. I am very flattered to see that I was chosen, but I would like to know what I owe this to. — In the broadest terms, I can tell you. We needed a person in whose nature there would be as much health and flexibility as possible, as much capacity for rational labor as possible, as few purely personal attachments on Earth as possible, as little individualism as possible. Our physiologists and psychologists believed that the transition from the conditions of life of your society, sharply fragmented by eternal internal struggle, to the conditions of ours, organized, as you said, socially, — that this transition is very difficult and hard for an individual and requires a particularly favorable organization. Manny found that you were more suitable than others. — And Manny's opinion was enough for all of you? — Yes, we fully trust his assessment. He is a person of outstanding strength and clarity of mind and makes mistakes very rarely. He has more experience in communicating with earthly people than anyone else among us; he was the first to initiate these communications. — And who discovered the very method of communication between planets? — This is the work of many, not one. "Minus-matter" was obtained several decades ago. But initially, it could only be obtained in insignificant quantities, and it took the efforts of many factory colleges to find and develop methods for its production in large quantities. Then it became necessary to improve the technology for extracting and decomposing radioactive matter to have a suitable engine for eteronefs. This also required a lot of effort. Furthermore, many difficulties arose from the very conditions of the interplanetary environment, with its terrible cold and scorching sun rays, not softened by the atmospheric envelope. Calculating the path also turned out to be a difficult task and subject to errors that were not foreseen before. In short, previous expeditions to Earth ended in the death of all participants, until Manny managed to organize the first successful expedition. And now, using his methods, we have recently penetrated Venus. — But if so, then Manny is a truly great man, — I said. — Yes, if you like to call a person who has indeed worked a lot and well that way. — I didn't mean that: ordinary people, executors, can also work a lot and well. Manny, on the other hand, is obviously something completely different: he is a genius, a creator, creating the new and leading humanity forward. — This is all unclear and, it seems, incorrect. Every worker is a creator, but in every worker, humanity and nature create. Is it not true that in Manny's hands lies all the experience of previous generations and contemporary researchers, and did not each step of his work proceed from this experience? And did not nature provide him with all the elements and all the germs of his combinations? And did not all the living stimuli for these combinations arise from humanity's struggle with nature itself? A person is an individual, but his work is impersonal. Sooner or later, he dies with his joys and sufferings, and his work remains in the infinitely growing life. There is no difference between workers in this regard; only the magnitude of what they have experienced and what remains in life differs. — But the name of such a person as Manny does not die with him, but remains in the memory of humanity, while the countless names of others disappear without a trace. — The name of each is preserved as long as those who lived with him and know him are alive. But humanity does not need a dead symbol of a personality when it no longer exists. Our science and our art impersonally preserve what has been done by collective work. The ballast of past names is useless for the memory of humanity. — You may be right; but the feeling of our world rebels against this logic. For us, the names of the leaders of thought and action are living symbols, without which neither our science, nor our art, nor our entire social life can do without. Often, in the struggle of forces and ideas, a name on a banner says more than an abstract slogan. And the names of geniuses are not ballast for our memory. — This is because the common cause of humanity is still not a common cause for you; in the illusions generated by the struggle between people, it is fragmented and seems to be the cause of people, not of humanity. It was also difficult for me to understand your point of view, as you do ours. — So, whether it is good or bad, there are no immortals in our company. However, the mortals here are probably among the most select—aren't they?—among those who have "worked a lot and well," as you put it? — Generally yes. Manny selected comrades from among thousands who expressed a desire to go with him. — And the greatest after him—is it perhaps Sterney? — Yes, if you are determined to measure and compare people. Sterney is an outstanding scientist, although in a completely different way than Manny. He is a mathematician, of whom there are very few. He uncovered a whole series of errors in the calculations by which all previous expeditions to Earth were organized and showed that some of these errors by themselves were sufficient for the failure of the undertaking and its workers. He found new methods for such calculations, and so far, the results obtained by him have proven to be infallible. — I imagined him that way based on Manny's words and my first impressions. Meanwhile, I myself do not understand why his appearance evokes a sense of anxiety, a kind of indefinite unease, something like causeless antipathy. Do you happen to have any explanation for this, Doctor? — You see, Sterney has a very strong, but cold, primarily analytical mind. He breaks down everything, relentlessly and consistently, and his conclusions are often one-sided, sometimes excessively harsh, because the analysis of parts gives not the whole, but less than the whole: you know that wherever there is life, the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, just as a living human body is greater than a heap of its limbs. As a result, Sterney is less able than others to enter into the moods and thoughts of other people. He will always readily help you with what you yourself ask him for, but he will never guess for you what you need. This is also hindered, of course, by the fact that his attention is almost always absorbed by his work; his head is constantly full of some difficult task. In this, he is unlike Manny: he always sees everything around him and has more than once managed to explain even to me myself what I want, what bothers me, what my mind or feeling seeks. — If all this is so, then Sterney must be quite hostile towards us Earthlings, full of contradictions and flaws? — Hostile? No; he is devoid of this feeling. But he has more skepticism, I think, than he should. He spent only six months in France and telegraphed Manny: "Nothing to look for here." Perhaps he was partly right, because Letta, who was with him, also did not find a suitable person. But the characterizations he gives of the people he saw in that country are much harsher than those given by Letta, and, of course, much more one-sided, although they contain nothing directly incorrect. — And who is this Letta you are talking about? I somehow don't remember him. — A chemist, Manny's assistant, an older man, older than everyone on our eteronef. You will get along well with him, and it will be very useful for you. He has a gentle nature and a lot of understanding of another's soul, although he is not a psychologist like Manny. Come to his laboratory; he will be happy about it and will show you many interesting things. At that moment, I remembered that we had already flown far from Earth, and I wanted to look at it. We went together to one of the side halls with large windows. — Won't we have to fly close to the Moon? — I asked on the way. — No, the Moon remains far to the side, and that's a pity. I also wanted to look at the Moon up close. From Earth, it seemed so strange to me. Large, cold, slow, mysteriously calm, it is nothing like our two small moons, which run across the sky so quickly and change their faces so quickly, like lively, capricious children. True, your Moon is much brighter, and its light is so pleasant. Your Sun is also brighter; that's where you are much happier than us. Your world is twice as bright: that's why you don't need eyes like ours, with large pupils to collect the weak rays of our day and night. We sat by the window. The Earth shone in the distance like a giant sickle, on which one could distinguish only the outlines of the west of America, the northeast of Asia, a dim patch indicating part of the Great Ocean, and a white patch of the Arctic Ocean. The entire Atlantic Ocean and the Old World lay in darkness; beyond the blurred edge of the sickle, they could only be guessed at, precisely because the unseen part of the Earth obscured the stars in a vast expanse of black sky. Our indirect trajectory and the Earth's rotation around its axis led to such a change in the picture. I looked, and it made me sad that I couldn't see my native country, where there was so much life, struggle, and suffering, where just yesterday I stood in the ranks of my comrades, and now another had to take my place. And doubt arose in my soul. — Blood is being shed down there, — I said, — and here, yesterday's worker as a calm observer... — Blood is being shed there for a better future, — replied Natty, — but one must also know the better future for the struggle itself. And for the sake of this knowledge, you are here. With an involuntary impulse, I squeezed his small, almost childlike hand. 8. CLOSING IN The Earth moved further and further away and, as if emaciated by separation, turned into a crescent-shaped sickle, now accompanied by the very small sickle of the real Moon. In parallel with this, all of us inhabitants of the eteronef were becoming like fantastic acrobats, capable of flying without wings and comfortably positioning ourselves in any direction in space, head to the floor, or ceiling, or wall—almost indifferently... Gradually, I got closer to my new companions and began to feel more at ease with them. On the very next day after our departure (we kept this count of time, although for us, of course, there were no longer real days and nights), I changed into a Martian costume on my own initiative, so as not to stand out among everyone. True, the costume itself pleased me: simple, comfortable, without any useless, conventional parts like a tie or cuffs, it left the greatest possible freedom of movement. The individual parts of the costume were joined together by small fasteners in such a way that the whole costume became one whole, and at the same time, it was easy to unfasten and remove, for example, one sleeve or both, or the entire blouse, if necessary. And the manners of my companions were similar to their costumes: simplicity, absence of everything superfluous and conventional. They never greeted, never said goodbye, never thanked, never prolonged a conversation out of politeness if its direct thread was exhausted; and at the same time, they always gave explanations with great patience, carefully adapting to the interlocutor's level of understanding and entering into his psychology, however little it might suit their own. Of course, from the very first days, I began to study their native language, and all of them readily acted as my mentors, and most of all Natty. This language is very original; and despite the great simplicity of its grammar and word-formation rules, it has peculiarities that were not easy for me to cope with. Its rules generally have no exceptions, it has no such distinctions as masculine, feminine, and neuter genders; but at the same time, all nouns for objects and properties change according to tense. This did not fit into my head at all. — Tell me, what is the meaning of these forms? — I asked Natty. — Don't you understand? And yet, in your languages, when naming an object, you carefully indicate whether you consider it male or female, which is, in essence, very unimportant, and even rather strange in relation to inanimate objects. How much more important is the distinction between those objects that exist and those that no longer exist, or those that are yet to arise. For you, "house" is "male," and "boat" is "female"; for the French, it's the opposite—and the matter does not change from this at all. But when you talk about a house that has already burned down or one that you are still going to build, you use the word in the same form as when you talk about a house you live in. Is there a greater difference in nature than between a person who lives and a person who has died—between what is and what is not? You need whole words and phrases to denote this difference—isn't it better to express it by adding one letter to the word itself? In any case, Natty was pleased with my memory, and his teaching method was excellent, and progress was made quickly. This helped me to get closer to the Martians—I began to travel around the entire eteronef with increasing confidence, visiting the rooms and laboratories of my companions and asking them about everything that concerned me. The young astronomer Enno, Sterney's assistant, lively and cheerful, also almost a boy in age, showed me a lot of interesting things, clearly fascinated not so much by measurements and formulas, in which he was, however, a true master, as by the beauty of what he observed. I felt good with the young poet-astronomer; and the legitimate desire to orient myself in our position in nature gave me a constant reason to spend some time with Enno and his telescopes. Once, Enno showed me the tiny planet Eros through the strongest magnification, part of whose orbit passes between the paths of Earth and Mars, the rest lies beyond Mars, transitioning into the asteroid belt. And although Eros was 150 million kilometers away from us at that time, its small disk's photograph represented a whole geographical map in the microscope's field of view, similar to the maps of the Moon. Of course, it is a lifeless planet, just like the Moon. Another time, Enno photographed a swarm of meteorites passing just a few million kilometers from us. The image, of course, was only an indefinite nebula. On this occasion, Enno told me that in one of the previous expeditions to Earth, the eteronef was destroyed precisely when it was cutting through another similar swarm. Astronomers watching the eteronef with the largest telescopes saw its electric light go out, and the eteronef disappeared forever into space. — Probably, the eteronef collided with several of these small bodies, and at the tremendous difference in speeds, they must have pierced through all its walls. Then the air escaped from it into space, and the cold of the interplanetary environment froze the already dead bodies of the travelers. And now the eteronef is flying, continuing its journey along a cometary orbit; it is moving away from the Sun forever, and it is unknown where the end of this terrible ship, inhabited by corpses, is. At these words, the cold of the ethereal deserts seemed to penetrate my heart. I vividly imagined our tiny bright island amidst an endless dead ocean. Without any support in the dizzyingly fast movement, and black emptiness all around... Enno guessed my mood. — Manny is a reliable helmsman, — he said, — and Sterney makes no mistakes... And death... you have probably seen it up close in your life... it is only death, nothing more. Very soon came the hour when I had to recall these words in the struggle with agonizing heartache. The chemist Letta attracted me not only by his exceptional gentleness and sensitivity of nature, about which Natty had told me, but also by his vast knowledge in the most interesting scientific question for me—the structure of matter. Manny alone was more competent than him in this area, but I tried to turn to Manny as little as possible, understanding that his time was too precious both for the interests of science and for the interests of the expedition for me to have the right to distract him for myself. And the good-natured old man Letta was so inexhaustibly patient with my ignorance, so attentive and even with visible pleasure explained to me the very alphabet of the subject that I did not feel constrained with him at all. Letta began to give me a whole course on the theory of the structure of matter, illustrating it with a series of experiments on the decomposition and synthesis of elements. Many of the experiments related to this, however, he had to omit, limiting himself to verbal descriptions—precisely those in which the phenomena are particularly violent and proceed in the form of an explosion or can take such a form. Once, during a lecture, Manny entered the laboratory. Letta was finishing the description of a very interesting experiment and was about to proceed to its execution. — Be careful, — Manny told him, — I remember that this experiment once ended badly for me; a negligible impurity in the substance you are decomposing is enough, and then the weakest electrical discharge can cause an explosion during heating. Letta was about to refrain from performing the experiment, but Manny, always attentive and courteous towards me, offered to help him by carefully checking all the conditions of the experiment; and the reaction went excellently. The next day, new experiments with the same substance were planned. It seemed to me that this time Letta took it from a different jar than the one from yesterday. As he placed the retort on the electric bath, it occurred to me to tell him about it. Concerned, he immediately went to the reagent cabinet, leaving the bath and the retort on the table by the wall, which was simultaneously the outer wall of the eteronef. I went with him. Suddenly, a deafening crack rang out, and we were both thrown with great force against the cabinet doors. Then followed a deafening hiss and roar and a metallic rattling. I felt an irresistible force, like a hurricane, pulling me back towards the outer wall. I managed to instinctively grab onto a strong leather handle attached to the cabinet and hung horizontally, held in this position by a powerful stream of air. Letta did the same. — Hold on tight! — he shouted to me, and I could barely hear his voice amidst the noise of the storm. A sharp cold pierced my body. Letta quickly looked around. His face was terrifying in its pallor, but the expression of bewilderment suddenly gave way to an expression of clear thought and firm resolve. He said only two words—I could not hear them, but I guessed that it was a farewell forever—and his hands unclasped. A dull thud, and the roar of the hurricane stopped. I felt that I could release the handle and looked around. There was no trace of the table, and by the wall, pressing his back tightly against it, stood Letta motionless. His eyes were wide open, and his entire face seemed frozen. With one leap, I found myself at the door and opened it. A gust of warm wind pushed me back. A second later, Manny entered the room. He quickly approached Letta. A few seconds later, the room was filled with people. Natty pushed everyone out of the way and rushed to Letta. All the others surrounded us in anxious silence. — Letta is dead, — Manny's voice rang out. — The explosion during the chemical experiment broke through the wall of the eteronef, and Letta covered the breach with his body. The air pressure ruptured his lungs and paralyzed his heart. Death was instantaneous. Letta saved our guest, otherwise the death of both would have been inevitable. A muffled sob escaped from Natty. 9. THE PAST For several days after the catastrophe, Natty did not leave his room, and in Sterney's eyes, I began to notice a directly unfriendly expression at times. Undeniably, an outstanding scientist perished because of me, and Sterney's mathematical mind could not help but make a comparison between the value of the life that was lost and the one that was saved. Manny remained invariably even and calm and even doubled his attention and care for me; so did Enno, and all the others. I began to diligently continue my study of the Martian language and at the first convenient opportunity, I asked Manny to give me some book on the history of their humanity. Manny found my idea very successful and brought me a manual that popularly explained world history for Martian children. I began to read and translate the book with Natty's help. I was struck by the skill with which the unknown author brought to life and concretized even the most general, most abstract concepts and schemes with illustrations. This art allowed him to present the material in such a geometrically consistent system, in such a logically consistent sequence, as no Earth popularizer would dare to write for children. The first chapter was purely philosophical in nature and was dedicated to the idea of the universe as a single whole, encompassing everything and defining everything by itself. This chapter vividly reminded me of the works of that worker-thinker who, in a simple and naive form, first laid down the foundations of proletarian philosophy of nature. In the next chapter, the exposition returned to that immeasurably distant time when no forms familiar to us had yet formed in the universe, when chaos and uncertainty reigned in boundless space. The author told how the first formless accumulations of imperceptibly fine, chemically indeterminate matter separated in this environment; these accumulations served as the seeds of giant star worlds, such as nebulae, and among them, our Milky Way with 20 million suns, among which our Sun is one of the smallest. Then it was about how matter, concentrating and transitioning to more stable combinations, took the form of chemical elements, and alongside this, the primary, formless accumulations disintegrated, and among them, gaseous solar-planetary nebulae separated, of which thousands can still be found today with a telescope. The history of the development of these nebulae, the crystallization of suns and planets from them, was explained in the same way as our Kant-Laplace theory of the origin of worlds, but with greater certainty and more detail. — Tell me, Manny, — I asked, — do you consider it correct to give children these infinitely general and almost equally abstract ideas from the very beginning, these pale world pictures, so far from their immediate concrete environment? Does this not mean populating a child's brain with almost empty, almost purely verbal images? — The point is that we never begin education with books, — replied Manny. — A child draws his knowledge from direct observation of nature and live communication with other people. Before he picks up such a book, he has already made many trips, seen diverse natural scenes, knows many species of plants and animals, is familiar with the use of telescopes, microscopes, cameras, phonographs, and has heard many stories about the past and distant from older children, educators, and other adult friends. A book like this should only tie together and strengthen his knowledge, filling in accidental gaps along the way and outlining further avenues of study. It is understandable that in this case, the idea of the whole must first and foremost and constantly appear with full clarity, must be carried through from beginning to end, so that it is never lost in details. A whole person must be created in the child. All this was very unusual for me, but I did not ask Manny for details: in any case, I was to personally get acquainted with Martian children and their education system. I returned to my book. The subject of the following chapters was the geological history of Mars. Its exposition, although very concise, was full of comparisons with the history of Earth and Venus. With a significant parallelism of all three, the main difference was that Mars turned out to be twice as old as Earth and almost four times older than Venus. The ages of the planets were also established in figures, which I remember well, but I will not cite them here, so as not to irritate Earth scientists, for whom they would be quite unexpected. Then came the history of life from its very beginning. Descriptions were given of those primary compounds, complex cyano derivatives, which, not yet being actual living matter, possessed many of its properties, and descriptions of the geological conditions under which these compounds were chemically created. The reasons were explained why such substances were preserved and accumulated among other, more stable but less flexible, compounds. The gradual complication and differentiation of these chemical seeds of all life were traced, up to the formation of true living cells, from which the "kingdom of protists" begins. The picture of the further development of life reduced to a ladder of progress of living beings, or rather, to their common genealogical tree; from protists to higher plants, on the one hand, to humans, on the other—along with various lateral branches. When compared with the "terrestrial" line of development, it turned out that on the path from the primary cell to humans, a number of the first links in the chain are almost the same, and the differences in the last links are insignificant, while in the middle links, there are many more differences. This seemed extremely strange to me. — This question, — Natty told me, — as far as I know, has not yet been specifically studied. After all, twenty years ago, we did not know how the higher animals on Earth were structured, and we ourselves were very surprised to find such a similarity to our type. Obviously, the number of possible higher types that express the greatest fullness of life is not so great; and on planets as similar as ours, within very homogeneous conditions, nature could achieve this maximum of life only in one way. — And moreover, — Manny remarked, — the highest type that takes possession of its planet is the one that most completely expresses the entire sum of its conditions, while intermediate stages, capable of capturing only part of their environment, express these conditions partially and one-sidedly. Therefore, with a huge similarity in the total sum of conditions, the highest types must coincide to the greatest extent, and the intermediate ones, by virtue of their one-sidedness, offer more room for differences. I remembered that during my university studies, the same thought about a limited number of possible higher types came to my mind for a completely different reason: in octopuses, cephalopod mollusks, higher organisms of a whole branch of development, the eyes are unusually similar to the eyes of our branch—vertebrates; and yet, the origin and development of cephalopod eyes are completely different, so different that even the corresponding layers of tissues of the visual apparatus are arranged in them in the reverse order to ours... Be that as it may, the fact was undeniable: on another planet, there lived people similar to us, and it remained for me to diligently continue my acquaintance with their lives and history. As for prehistoric times and, in general, the initial phases of human life on Mars, the similarity to the terrestrial world was enormous here as well. The same forms of family life, the same isolated existence of individual communities, the same development of connections between them through exchange. But further, a divergence began, although not in the main direction of development, but rather in its style and character. The course of history on Mars was somehow softer and simpler than on Earth. There were, of course, wars of tribes and peoples, and there was class struggle; but wars played a relatively small role in historical life and ceased relatively early; and class struggle manifested itself much less and less often in the form of clashes of brute force. This, it is true, was not explicitly stated in the book I was reading, but it was obvious to me from the entire exposition. Martians did not know slavery at all; their feudalism had very little militarism; and their capitalism freed itself from national-state fragmentation very early and did not create anything similar to our modern armies. AT MANNY'S For the first time, I settled in with Manny, in a factory town, the center and foundation of which is a large chemical laboratory located deep underground. The above-ground part of the town is scattered among a park over an area of ten square kilometers: several hundred dwellings for laboratory workers, a large Assembly House. A consumer warehouse—something like a general store—and a communication station that connects the chemical town with the rest of the world. Manny was the head of all operations there and lived near the public buildings, next to the main descent into the laboratory. The first thing that struck me about the nature of Mars and that I found hardest to get used to was the red color of the plants. Their coloring substance, very close in composition to the chlorophyll of Earth plants, performs an analogous role in the economy of nature: it creates plant tissues from atmospheric carbon dioxide and solar energy. The attentive Natty offered me to wear protective glasses to get rid of the unfamiliar irritation of my eyes. I refused. — It is the color of our socialist banner, — I said. — I must get used to your socialist nature. — If so, then one must admit that there is socialism in terrestrial flora too, but in a hidden form, — noted Manny. — The leaves of terrestrial plants also have a reddish tint—it's just masked by the much stronger green. It is enough to wear glasses made of glass that completely absorb green rays and let red ones through, for your forests and fields to become red like ours. I cannot waste time and space describing the peculiar forms of plants and animals on Mars, or its atmosphere, clean and transparent, relatively rarefied, but rich in oxygen, or its sky, deep and dark, greenish in color, with a thinned sun and tiny moons, with two bright evening or morning stars—Venus and Earth. All this strange and alien then, beautiful and dear to me now, in the coloring of memories, is not so closely connected with the tasks of my narrative. People and their relationships—that is what is most important to me; and in all that fairy-tale setting, it was they who were the most fantastic, the most mysterious. Manny lived in a small two-story house, architecturally no different from the others. The most original feature of this architecture was the transparent roof made of several huge plates of blue glass. Directly under this roof were the bedroom and a room for conversations with friends. Martians invariably spend their rest hours in blue lighting, for its calming effect, and do not find the gloomy hue that this lighting gives to a human face unpleasant. All the workrooms—the study, the home laboratory, the communication room—were on the lower floor, whose large windows freely let in the waves of restless red light cast by the bright foliage of the park trees. This light, which initially caused me an anxious and scattered mood, is a habitual stimulus for Martians, useful for work. Manny's study contained many books and various writing instruments, from simple pencils to a printing phonograph. The latter apparatus is a complex mechanism in which the phonograph recording, when words are pronounced clearly, is immediately transmitted to the levers of a typewriter in such a way that an accurate translation of this recording into the ordinary alphabet is obtained. In this case, the phonogram is preserved intact, so it can be used equally with the printed translation, depending on which seems more convenient. Above Manny's desk hung a portrait of a middle-aged Martian. His facial features strongly resembled Manny's, but differed in an expression of stern energy and cold determination, an almost formidable expression alien to Manny, on whose face there was always only calm and firm will. Manny told me the story of this man. It was Manny's ancestor, a great engineer. He lived long before the social revolution, in the era of the digging of the Great Canals; these grandiose works were organized according to his plan and carried out under his leadership. His first assistant, envious of his glory and power, plotted against him. One of the main canals, which several hundred people were working on, began in a swampy, unhealthy area. Many thousands of workers died there from illness, and discontent grew among the rest. At the very time when the chief engineer was negotiating with the central Martian government about pensions for families of those who died at work and for those who lost their ability to work due to illness, the senior assistant secretly agitated against him among the discontented: he incited them to go on strike, demanding the relocation of work from this area to another, which was impossible according to the essence

It was important because it destroyed the entire plan of the Great Works, and the resignation of the chief engineer, which was, of course, entirely feasible… When he learned all this, he summoned his senior assistant for an explanation and killed him on the spot. In court, the engineer refused any defense, stating only that he considered his actions just and necessary. He was sentenced to many years of imprisonment. But it soon turned out that none of his successors were capable of managing the giant organization of work; misunderstandings, theft, and disorder began, the entire mechanism of the enterprise fell into disarray, expenses increased by hundreds of millions, and among the workers, sharp discontent threatened to turn into an uprising. The central government hastened to turn to the former engineer; he was offered a full pardon and reinstatement in his position. He resolutely refused the pardon but agreed to direct the work from prison. The inspectors he appointed quickly clarified the situation on the ground; thousands of engineers and contractors were dismissed and brought to trial. Wages were raised, the organization of food, clothing, and tools for the workers was rebuilt anew, and work plans were revised and corrected. Soon, order was fully restored, and the enormous mechanism began to work quickly and precisely, like an obedient tool in the hands of a true master. And the master not only directed the entire enterprise but also developed a plan for its continuation in the coming years; and at the same time, he was preparing a successor in the person of an energetic and talented engineer who had risen from the working class. By the day his prison sentence expired, everything was so well prepared that the great master found it possible to transfer the enterprise into other hands without fear for its fate; and at the very moment when the prime minister of the central government arrived at the prison to release the prisoner, the chief engineer took his own life. When Manni told me all this, his face changed strangely; he acquired an expression of inflexible severity, and he became completely like his ancestor. And I felt to what extent this man, who had died hundreds of years before his birth, was close and understandable to him. The communication room was the central room on the lower floor. It housed telephones and corresponding optical apparatuses that transmitted images of what was happening before them over any desired distance. Some of the devices connected Manni's dwelling to the Communication Station, and through it, to all other houses in the city and to all cities on the planet. Others served as a connection to the underground laboratory, which Manni managed. These latter operated continuously: on several finely latticed plates, moderate images of illuminated halls were visible, where large metal machines and glass apparatuses were located, and before them, tens and hundreds of working people. I asked Manni to take me to this laboratory. "It's inconvenient," he replied. Only the instruments and materials related to his current research were always kept in Manni's home laboratory. AT THE FACTORY Approximately 500 kilometers in two hours—a speed of the fastest falcon flight, not yet achieved even by our electric railways… Below, unfamiliar, strange landscapes unfolded in rapid succession; even faster, unfamiliar strange birds sometimes flew past us. Sunbeams flashed with blue light on the roofs of houses and with ordinary yellowish light on the huge domes of unfamiliar buildings. Rivers and canals flashed like steel ribbons; my eyes rested on them because they were the same as on Earth. Here, in the distance, a huge city became visible, spread around a small lake and bisected by a canal. The gondola slowed down and gently descended near a small, beautiful house—Netti's house. Netti was home and greeted us joyfully. He got into our gondola, and we set off further: the factory was still several kilometers away, on the other side of the lake. Five huge buildings, arranged in a cross shape, all of the same design; a clear glass dome, resting on several dozen dark columns forming a precise circle or a slightly stretched ellipse; similar glass plates, alternating transparent and opaque, between the columns in the form of walls. We stopped at the central, largest building, before a gate occupying an entire gap from column to column, about ten meters wide and twelve meters high. The ceiling of the first floor horizontally intersected the gate space in the middle; several pairs of rails entered the gate and disappeared inside the building. We floated up to the upper half of the gate and, deafened by the noise of the machines, immediately entered the second floor. However, this was not a separate floor in the strict sense of the word, but rather a network of aerial bridges that entwined the gigantic machines of unfamiliar design from all sides. Several meters above it was another similar network, even higher—a third, fourth, fifth; all of them were formed from a glass parquet, enclosed by iron grating bars, all connected by numerous lifts and stairs, and each subsequent network was smaller than the previous one. No smoke, no soot, no smell, no fine dust. Amidst the clean, fresh air, the machines, flooded with light, not too bright but penetrating everywhere, worked harmoniously and steadily. They cut, sawed, planed, drilled enormous pieces of iron, aluminum, nickel, copper. Levers, resembling colossal steel arms, moved smoothly and evenly; large platforms moved back and forth with elemental precision; wheels and drive belts seemed motionless. Not the brute force of fire and steam, but the subtle, yet even more powerful, force of electricity was the soul of this formidable mechanism. The very noise of the machines, as the ear gradually accustomed itself to it, began to seem almost melodious, except for those moments when the main hammer, weighing several thousand tons, fell, and everything shuddered with a thunderous blow. Hundreds of workers moved confidently among the machines, and neither their steps nor their voices were audible amidst the sea of sounds. In their facial expressions, there was no tense concern, only calm attention; they seemed like curious, scholarly observers who were, in fact, uninvolved in everything that was happening; they were simply interested in seeing how enormous pieces of metal, emerging on rail platforms under the transparent dome, fell into the iron embrace of dark monsters, how these monsters then gnawed at them with their strong jaws, crushed them with their heavy, solid paws, planed and drilled them with their shiny, sharp claws, and how, finally, the remnants of this cruel game were carried away from the other side of the building by light electric trains in the form of slender and elegant machine parts of mysterious purpose. It seemed perfectly natural that the other monsters did not touch the small, large-eyed contemplators walking trustingly among them: it was simply a disregard for weakness, an acknowledgment that the prey was too insignificant, unworthy of the formidable power of the giants. The threads that connected the delicate human brain with the unbreakable organs of the mechanism were elusive and invisible from the outside. When we finally left the building, the technician who guided us asked if we wished to inspect the other buildings and auxiliary structures immediately or intended to take a break for rest. I opted for a break. "I've seen the machines and the workers," I said, "but I can't quite grasp the organization of labor itself. I would like to ask you about that." Instead of answering, the technician led us to a small cubic building located between the central and one of the corner buildings. There were three more such buildings, all similarly located. Their black walls were covered with rows of shining white symbols—these were simply labor statistics tables. I already knew the Martian language well enough to decipher them. On one, marked with the number one, it stated: "Machine production has a surplus of 968,757 worker hours daily, of which 11,525 hours are skilled specialists' labor." "At this factory, there is a surplus of 753 hours, of which 29 hours are skilled specialists' labor." "There is no shortage of workers in agricultural, mining, earthworks, chemical... production..." etc. (a multitude of various labor industries were listed in alphabetical order). On table number two, it was written: "The clothing production has a deficit of 592,685 worker hours daily, of which 21,380 hours are for skilled mechanics for special machines and 7,852 hours are for specialist organizers." "Shoe production requires 79,360 hours; of which..." etc. "The calculation institute—3,078 hours"... etc. Tables number 5 and 4 contained similar information. The list of labor industries included such as childcare for younger children, childcare for older children, urban medicine, rural medicine, etc. "Why is the labor surplus specified precisely only in machine production, and the deficit noted everywhere in such detail?" I asked. "It is very understandable," replied Manni, "the tables are intended to influence the distribution of labor: for this, it is necessary that everyone can see where labor power is lacking and to what extent. Then, if one has an equal or approximately equal inclination for two activities, one will choose the one where the deficit is greater. As for the labor surplus, it is enough to know the exact data only where this surplus exists, so that each worker in such an industry can consciously take into account both the degree of surplus and the degree of their inclination to change occupations." While we were talking, I suddenly noticed that some numbers on the table disappeared, and then new ones appeared in their place. I asked what this meant. "The numbers change every hour," explained Manni, "within an hour, several thousand people have declared their desire to switch from one job to another. The central statistical mechanism continuously records this, and every hour the electrical transmission broadcasts its messages everywhere." "But how does the central statistics determine the figures for surplus and deficit?" "The calculation institute has agents everywhere who monitor the movement of products in warehouses, the productivity of all enterprises, and changes in the number of workers in them. In this way, it is precisely determined how much and what should be produced for a specific period and how many labor hours are required for this. Then, the institute only needs to calculate, for each labor industry, the difference between what exists and what should be, and report it everywhere. The flow of volunteers then restores equilibrium." "And is the consumption of products not limited in any way?" "Absolutely not: everyone takes what they need and as much as they want." "And does this not require anything similar to money, any certificates of the amount of labor performed or obligations to perform it, or anything of that sort?" "Nothing of the sort. In free labor, we never have a shortage of it anyway: labor is a natural need of a developed socialist person, and any form of disguised or overt compulsion to labor is completely unnecessary for us." "But if consumption is not limited in any way, are sharp fluctuations that could overturn all statistical calculations not possible?" "Of course not. An individual person might eat one dish or another twice, three times the usual amount, or want to change ten outfits in ten days, but a society of three billion people is not subject to such fluctuations. With such large numbers, deviations in one direction or the other balance out, and average values change very slowly, in strict continuity." "Thus, your statistics work almost automatically—simple calculations, and nothing more?" "Oh, no. The difficulties here are very great. The calculation institute must closely monitor new inventions and changes in natural production conditions to account for them accurately. A new machine is introduced—it immediately requires the relocation of labor both in the area where it is used and in machine production, and sometimes in the production of materials for one industry or another. Ore is depleted, new mineral wealth is discovered—again, labor is relocated in a whole series of rail lines, etc. All this must be calculated from the very beginning, if not entirely accurately, then with a sufficient degree of approximation, and this is not at all easy until data from direct observation is obtained." "With such difficulties," I remarked, "it is obvious that you must always have a certain labor surplus in reserve?" "Precisely so—this is the main support of our system. About two hundred years ago, when collective labor was just barely enough to satisfy all the needs of society, then complete accuracy in calculations was necessary, and the distribution of labor could not be fully free: there was a mandatory workday, and within its limits, it was not always and not fully possible to consider the calling of comrades. But every invention, while creating temporary difficulties for statistics, facilitated the main task—the transition to unlimited freedom of labor. First, the workday was shortened, then, when a surplus of labor appeared in all fields, all obligations were finally eliminated. Note how insignificant all the numbers expressing labor deficits in these tables are: thousands, tens, hundreds of thousands of labor hours, no more—this is with millions and tens of millions of hours of labor already expended in the same industries." "However, there is still a labor deficit," I objected. "True, it is probably covered by a subsequent surplus, isn't it?" "And not just by a subsequent surplus. In reality, the calculation of necessary labor is done in such a way that a certain amount is added to the basic figure. In the most important industries for society—food, clothing, construction, machinery—this addition reaches 6 percent, in less important ones—1-2 percent. Thus, the deficit figures in these tables generally express only a relative, not an absolute, shortage. If the tens and hundreds of thousands of hours indicated here were not compensated, it would not mean that society would suffer a shortage." "And how many hours does each person work daily, for example, at this factory?" "Mostly one and a half, two, two and a half hours," replied the technician, "but sometimes less, and sometimes more. For example, the comrade who manages the main hammer is so engrossed in his work that he doesn't let anyone replace him for the entire working time of the factory, which is six hours daily." I mentally translated all these figures into terrestrial terms from Martian, where a day, slightly longer than ours, contains 10 hours. It turned out: normal work 4.5, 5, 6 hours; maximum duration—15 hours, i.e., the same as in the most exploited enterprises on Earth. "And isn't it harmful for the comrade at the hammer to work so much?" I asked. "It's not harmful yet," replied Netti, "he can afford such a luxury for another six months. But I have, of course, warned him about the dangers this passion poses. One of them is the possibility of a convulsive mental fit, which will draw him irresistibly under the hammer. Last year, a similar incident occurred at this same factory with another mechanic, a similarly avid seeker of strong sensations. Only by a happy accident was it possible to stop the hammer, and involuntary suicide was not successful. The thirst for strong sensations in itself is not yet an illness, but it is easily perverted as soon as the nervous system is even slightly shaken by overwork, mental struggle, or any accidental illness. In general, of course, I do not lose sight of those comrades who indulge excessively in any monotonous work." "And shouldn't this comrade we are talking about have reduced his work in view of the fact that there is a labor surplus in machine production?" "Of course not," Manni laughed. "Why should he specifically restore balance at his own expense? Statistics do not obligate anyone to anything. Everyone takes it into account in their calculations but cannot be guided by it alone. If you wished to immediately join this factory, you would likely find work, and the central statistics would increase the surplus figure by one or two hours, that's all. The influence of statistics is continuously felt in mass labor shifts, but each individual is free." While talking, we managed to rest enough, and everyone except Manni went on to inspect the factory. Manni went home—he was called to the laboratory. In the evening, I decided to stay with Netti: he promised to take me to the "children's home" the next day, where his mother was one of the educators. 3. THE CHILDREN'S HOME The "Children's Home" occupied a whole significant and, moreover, the best part of the city with a population of 15-20 thousand people. This population consisted almost entirely of children with their educators. Such institutions exist in all major cities on the planet, and in many cases form independent cities; only in small settlements, like Manni's "chemical town," are they mostly absent. Large two-story houses with ordinary blue roofs, scattered among gardens with streams, ponds, playgrounds for games and gymnastics, flowerbeds and useful herbs… Crowds of large-eyed children of unknown sex—thanks to the uniform costume for boys and girls… True, it is also difficult to distinguish men and women by their costumes among adult Martians—in their basic features, they are the same, with only a slight difference in style: men's clothing more accurately conveys body shapes, while women's clothing masks them more. In any case, the elderly lady who met us as we exited the gondola in front of the doors of one of the largest houses was undoubtedly a woman, because Netti, hugging her, called her "Mom." In further conversation, he often referred to her, like any other comrade, simply by name—Nella. The Martian woman already knew the purpose of our visit and led us directly into her "children's home," through all its departments, starting from the department for the youngest, which she herself managed, to the department for older children, bordering on adolescence. The little monsters joined us along the way and followed us, observing with interest with their huge eyes the person from another planet—they knew well who I was; and when we toured the last departments, we were already accompanied by a whole crowd, although most of the children had scattered into the gardens since morning. About three hundred children of various ages lived in this house. I asked Nella why all ages were combined in the "children's homes" instead of being separated into individual houses, which would significantly simplify the division of labor among educators and simplify all their work. "Because then there would be no real upbringing," Nella answered me. "To receive an education for society, a child must live in society. Children learn the most life experience and knowledge from each other. Isolating one age from another would mean creating a one-sided and narrow living environment for them, in which the development of the future person would proceed slowly, sluggishly, and monotonously. And for direct activity, the difference in ages provides the greatest scope. Older children are our best helpers in caring for the younger ones. No, we not only consciously combine all children's ages but also try to select educators of the most varied ages and practical specialties for each children's home." "However, in this house, the children are divided into departments according to age, which seems inconsistent with what you are saying?" "The children gather in departments only to sleep, have breakfast, and have dinner; of course, there is no need to mix different ages there. But for games and activities, they constantly group themselves as they please. Even when there are lectures, fictional or scientific, for children of one department, a mass of children from all other departments fills the auditorium. Children choose their own company and like to socialize with children of other ages, especially with adults." "Nella," said a little one, stepping out from the crowd at that moment, "Esta took my boat, which I made myself; take the boat from her and give it back to me." "And where is she?" asked Nella. "She ran to the pond to launch the boat," explained the child. "Well, I don't have time to go there now; let one of the older children go with you and convince Esta not to offend you. Or, even better, go there alone and help her launch the boat; there's nothing surprising that she liked the boat if it's made well." The child left, and Nella turned to the others: "And you, children, would do well to leave us alone. A foreigner is hardly pleased to be stared at by a hundred children's eyes. Imagine, Elvi, that a whole crowd of foreigners is looking at you attentively. What would you do then?" "I would run away," bravely declared the nearest one in the crowd, whom she had named. And all the children at that very moment scattered with laughter. We went out into the garden. "Yes, look at the power of the past," said the educator with a smile. "It would seem that communism is complete here, and children are rarely refused anything—where does a sense of personal property come from? And the child comes and says: 'my' boat, 'I' made it myself. And this happens quite often: sometimes it even comes to fights. Nothing can be done about it—it's a general law of life: the development of the organism briefly repeats the development of the species, and the development of the personality similarly repeats the development of society. The self-determination of children of middle and older age in most cases has such a vague individualistic character. The approach of sexual maturity initially further strengthens this nuance. Only in adolescence does the social environment of the present finally conquer the remnants of the past." "And do you introduce children to this past?" I asked. "Of course, we do; and they love conversations and stories about old times very much. At first, for them, these are fairy tales, beautiful, slightly scary stories about another world, distant and strange, but which awakens unclear echoes in the atavistic depths of children's instincts with its pictures of struggle and violence. Only later, overcoming the living remnants of the past in their own souls, does the child learn to perceive the connection of times more clearly, and the fairy tale pictures become the reality of history for them, transforming into living links of living continuity. We walked along the alleys of the vast garden. From time to time, we encountered groups of children engaged in games, digging trenches, working with craft tools, building gazebos, or simply engaged in lively conversation. They all turned to me with interest, but none followed us; apparently, they were all warned. Most of the encountered groups were of mixed ages; many had one or two adults. "There are quite a few educators in your house," I remarked. "Yes, especially if you count all the older children among them, as you justly should. But we only have three specialist educators here; the other adults you see are mostly mothers and fathers temporarily residing with us near their children, or young people who wish to learn the art of education." "So, can all parents who wish to do so live here to be with their children?" "Yes, of course; and some mothers live here for several years. But most of them come from time to time for a week, two weeks, a month. Fathers live here less often. Our house has 60 private rooms for parents and for those children who seek solitude, and I don't recall a case where these rooms were insufficient." "So, do children sometimes refuse to live in communal spaces?" "Yes, older children often prefer to live separately. This is partly due to the vague individualism I told you about, and partly, especially for children prone to deep scientific study, it is simply a desire to set aside everything that distracts and diverts attention. Among adults, too, those who are most engaged in scientific research or artistic creation prefer to live completely separately." At that moment, we noticed a child in front of us in a small clearing, who, in my opinion, was six or seven years old, chasing an animal with a stick. We quickened our steps; the child paid no attention to us. The moment we approached, he caught his prey—it turned out to be something like a large frog—and hit it hard with his stick. The animal crawled slowly along the grass with a broken leg. "Why did you do that, Aldo?" Nella asked calmly. "I couldn't catch it, it kept running away," explained the boy. "And do you know what you did? You caused the frog pain and broke its leg. Give me the stick, and I'll explain it to you." The boy handed the stick to Nella, and with a swift movement, she hit his hand hard. The boy cried out. "Does it hurt, Aldo?" the educator asked just as calmly. "It hurts a lot, wicked Nella!" he answered. "And you hit the frog harder than this. I only bruised your hand, and you broke its leg. It not only hurts much more than you, but it can no longer run and jump, it won't be able to find food, and it will die of hunger or be eaten by evil animals from which it cannot escape. What do you think about that, Aldo?" The child stood with tears of pain in his eyes, holding his bruised hand with the other. But he thought. A minute later, he said: "We need to fix its leg." "That's right," said Netti. "Let me show you how to do it." They immediately caught the injured animal, which had only managed to crawl a few steps. Netti took out his handkerchief and tore it into strips, and Aldo, at his direction, brought him some thin twigs. Then both, with the seriousness of true children engaged in very important work, began to apply a firm strengthening bandage to the frog's broken leg. Soon, I and Netti prepared to go home. "Yes, by the way," Nella recalled, "this evening you could catch your old friend Enno here. He will be reading to the older children about the planet Venus." "So, he lives in this same city?" I asked. "No, the observatory where he works is three hours away from here. But he loves children very much and doesn't forget me, his old educator. That's why he often visits here and tells the children something interesting each time." In the evening, at the appointed time, we, of course, appeared again at the "children's home," in the large auditorium where all the children, except the very youngest, and several dozen adults had already gathered. Enno greeted me joyfully. "I chose the topic as if for you," he said jokingly. "You are saddened by the backwardness of your planet and the wicked morals of your humanity. I will talk about a planet where the highest representatives of life are currently only dinosaurs and flying lizards, and their customs are worse than those of your bourgeoisie. Your coal does not burn in the fire of capitalism, but is still growing in the form of giant forests. Shall we go hunting for ichthyosaurs there together sometime? They are the Rothschilds and Rockefellers of that place, true, much more moderate than your earthly ones, but also much less cultured. There reigns the most primitive accumulation, forgotten in your Marx's 'Capital'... Well, Nella is already frowning at my frivolous chatter. I'm starting now." He captivatingly described a distant planet with its deep stormy oceans and mountains of enormous height, with its burning sun and dense white clouds, with its terrible hurricanes and thunderstorms, with its ugly monsters and majestic giant plants. He illustrated all this with live photographs on a screen that occupied an entire wall of the hall. Only Enno's voice was heard in the darkness; deep attention reigned in the hall. When, describing the adventures of the first travelers in this world, he told how one of them killed a giant lizard with a hand grenade, a strange little scene occurred, unnoticed by most of the audience. Aldo, who had been staying near Nella all this time, suddenly began to cry quietly. "What's wrong with you?" Nella asked, leaning towards him. "I feel sorry for the monster. It hurt a lot, and it died completely," the boy answered quietly. Nella hugged the child and began to explain something to him in a whisper, but he did not calm down for a long time. Meanwhile, Enno spoke about the innumerable natural riches of the beautiful planet, about its giant waterfalls of hundreds of millions of horsepower, about precious metals found directly on the surface of its mountains, about rich radium deposits at a depth of several hundred meters, about energy reserves for hundreds of thousands of years. I did not yet command the language well enough to feel the beauty of the narration, but the pictures themselves held my attention as completely as the children's. When Enno finished and the hall lit up, I even felt a little sad, as children feel sad when a beautiful fairy tale is over. After the lecture, questions and objections from the listeners began. The questions were as varied as the listeners themselves; they concerned either the details of the natural scenery or the methods of combating it. One question was: after how long should people appear on Venus from its own nature, and what should their physical structure be? The objections, fortunately naive, but sometimes quite witty, were directed mainly against Enno's conclusion that Venus is currently a very inconvenient planet for humans and that it will be difficult to utilize its great riches significantly anytime soon. Young optimists energetically protested this position, which expressed the views of most researchers. Enno pointed out that the scorching sun and humid air with a mass of bacteria pose a danger of many diseases to humans, as all travelers who have visited Venus have experienced; that hurricanes and thunderstorms impede work and threaten human lives, and much more. The children found it strange to retreat before such obstacles when it was necessary to conquer such a beautiful planet. To combat bacteria and diseases, a thousand doctors should be sent there as soon as possible; to combat hurricanes and thunderstorms—hundreds of thousands of builders who would build high walls where needed and install lightning rods. "Let nine-tenths perish," said a fervent boy of about twelve, "it's worth dying for, as long as victory is achieved!" And it was clear from his burning eyes that he himself, of course, would not hesitate to be among these nine-tenths. Enno gently and calmly dismantled the card houses of his opponents; but it was evident that deep down he sympathized with them and that his fiery young imagination harbored equally decisive plans, of course, more considered, but perhaps no less self-sacrificing. He himself had not yet been to Venus, and it was clear from his enthusiasm that its beauty and dangers strongly attracted him. When the conversation ended, Enno went with me and Netti. He decided to stay another day in this city and proposed that we go to the art museum together the next day. Netti was busy—he was called to another city for a large meeting of doctors. 4. ART MUSEUM "I never would have thought that you had special museums of artistic works," I said to Enno on the way to the museum. "I thought that sculptural and painting galleries were a peculiarity of capitalism with its ostentatious luxury and desire to crudely accumulate wealth. In a socialist society, I supposed, art would be scattered everywhere alongside the life it adorns." "In this, you were not mistaken," answered Enno. "Most works of art are always intended for public buildings—those where we discuss our common affairs, those where we learn and research, those where we rest… We adorn our factories and plants much less: the aesthetics of powerful machines and their harmonious movement are pleasant to us in their pure form, and there are very few works of art that would harmonize perfectly with it, without distracting or weakening its impression. We adorn our homes the least, where we spend most of our time. And our art museums are scientific-aesthetic institutions, schools for studying how arts develop, or rather, how humanity develops in its artistic activity. The museum was located on a small island in the lake, connected to the shore by a narrow bridge. The building itself, an elongated rectangle surrounding a garden with tall fountains and many blue, white, black, green flowers, was elegantly decorated on the outside and full of light inside. There was indeed no such chaotic accumulation of statues and paintings as in the large museums of Earth. Before me, the chain of development of plastic arts passed in several hundred examples, from the primitive crude works of prehistoric times to the technically perfect works of the last century. And from beginning to end, the imprint of that living internal integrity that people call 'genius' was felt everywhere. Obviously, these were the best works of all eras. To fully understand the beauty of another world, one must deeply know its life, and to give others an idea of this beauty, one must oneself be organically connected to it… That is why it is impossible for me to describe what I saw there; I can only give hints, only fragmentary indications of what struck me most. The main motif of Martian, like our, sculpture is the beautiful human body. The differences in the physical build of Martians from earthly humans are generally not great; if we disregard the sharp difference in eye size and, partly, in skull structure, these differences do not exceed those that exist between earthly races. I would not be able to explain them precisely—for this, I know anatomy too poorly—but my eye easily accustomed itself to them and perceived them almost immediately not as ugliness, but as originality. I noticed that male and female builds are more similar than in most earthly tribes: the relatively broad shoulders of women, the less sharply defined musculature of men due to some fullness, and their narrower pelvis smooth out the difference. This, however, mainly applies to the last era—the era of free human development: in statues of the capitalist period, sexual differences are expressed more strongly. Clearly, the domestic slavery of women and the feverish struggle for existence of men distort their bodies in two dissimilar directions. Not for a moment did the clear, then vague, consciousness that I was looking at images of an alien world disappear within me; it gave all impressions a strange, semi-phantom hue. And even the beautiful female body in these statues and paintings evoked in me an incomprehensible feeling, as if completely unlike the familiar loving-aesthetic attraction, but rather like those vague presentiments that stirred me long ago, on the border of childhood and youth. Statues of early periods were monochrome, like ours, later ones—natural colors. This did not surprise me. I always thought that deviation from reality cannot be a necessary element of art, that it is even anti-artistic when it reduces the richness of perception, like the monochrome of sculpture, that in this case it does not help but hinders artistic idealization, which concentrates life. In statues and paintings of ancient epochs, as in our ancient sculpture, images of serene harmony, free from any tension, predominated. In the middle, transitional epochs, a different character emerges: impulse, passion, exciting striving, sometimes softened to the point of wandering dreams, erotic or religious, sometimes sharply breaking through in the extreme tension of unbalanced forces of soul and body. In the socialist epoch, the main character changes again: it is harmonious movement, calm-confident manifestation of strength, action devoid of the painfulness of effort, striving free from excitement, living activity imbued with the consciousness of its harmonious unity and its invincible rationality. If the ideal female beauty of ancient art expressed the boundless possibility of love, and the ideal beauty of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance—an insatiable thirst for love, mystical or sensual, then here, in the ideal beauty of another world moving ahead of us, love itself was embodied in its calm and proud self-awareness, love itself—clear, bright, all-conquering… For later artistic works, as for ancient ones, extreme simplicity and unity of motif are characteristic. Very complex human beings with rich and harmonious life content are depicted, and at the same time, moments of their lives are chosen when their entire being is concentrated in one feeling, one striving… Favorite themes of the latest artists are the ecstasy of creative thought, the ecstasy of love, the ecstasy of enjoying nature, the peace of voluntary death—subjects that deeply outline the essence of a great tribe that knows how to live with full intensity and tension, to die consciously and with dignity. The painting and sculpture section constituted one half of the museum; the other was entirely dedicated to architecture. By architecture, Martians understand not only the aesthetics of buildings and large engineering structures but also the aesthetics of furniture, tools, machines, generally the aesthetics of everything material and useful. How important this art was in their lives could be judged by the special completeness and meticulousness of this collection. From primitive cave dwellings with their crudely decorated utensils to luxurious public houses of glass and aluminum with their interiors made by the best artists, to giant factories with their formidable and beautiful machines, to the greatest canals with their granite embankments and aerial bridges—all typical forms were represented here in the form of pictures, drawings, models, and especially stereograms in large stereoscopes, where everything was reproduced with full illusion of identity. A special place was occupied by the aesthetics of gardens, fields, and parks; and however unfamiliar the nature of the planet was to me, even I often understood the beauty of the combinations of colors and forms that were created from this nature by the collective genius of the tribe with large eyes. In works of previous epochs, very often, as with us, elegance was achieved at the expense of convenience, decorations harmed durability, art committed violence against the direct useful purpose of objects. I detected nothing of the sort in the works of the latest epoch—neither in its furniture, nor in its tools, nor in its structures. I asked Enno if their modern architecture allows for deviations from the practical perfection of objects for the sake of their beauty. "Never," answered Enno, "that would be false beauty, artificiality, not art. In pre-socialist times, Martians erected monuments to their great people; now they erect monuments only to great events; such as the first attempt to reach Earth, which ended in the death of the explorers, such as the eradication of a deadly epidemic disease, such as the discovery of the decomposition and synthesis of all chemical elements. A series of monuments was presented in stereograms in the same department where the tombs and temples were located (Martians formerly had religions too). One of the latest monuments to great people was the monument to that engineer whom Manni told me about. The artist managed to clearly portray the strength of the soul of a person who victoriously led the army of labor in the struggle against nature and proudly rejected the cowardly judgment of morality over his actions. As I paused before the panorama of the monument in involuntary thought, Enno quietly recited a few verses expressing the essence of the hero's spiritual tragedy. "Whose verses are these?" I asked. "Mine," answered Enno, "I wrote them for Manni." I could not fully judge the inner beauty of the verses in a language still foreign to me, but undoubtedly, their thought was clear, the rhythm very harmonious, and the rhyme sonorous and rich. This gave a new direction to my thoughts. "So, in your poetry, strict rhythm and rhyme still flourish?" "Of course," said Enno with a hint of surprise. "Does this seem not beautiful to you?" "No, not at all," I explained, "but there is a common opinion among us that this form was born from the tastes of the ruling classes of our society, as an expression of their lustfulness and fondness for conventions that restrict the freedom of artistic speech. From this, the conclusion is drawn that the poetry of the future, the poetry of the socialist era, must reject and forget these restrictive laws." "That is completely unfair," Enno argued heatedly. "We find rhythmic correctness beautiful not out of fondness for the conventional, but because it deeply harmonizes with the rhythmic correctness of the processes of our life and consciousness. And rhyme, which completes a series of diversities in identical final chords, is it not in such a deep kinship with that vital connection of people which their inner diversity increases with the unity of enjoyment in art? Without rhythm, there is no artistic form at all. Where there is no rhythm of sounds, there must be, and the stricter, the rhythm of ideas… And if rhyme is indeed of feudal origin, then this can be said about many other good and beautiful things." "But doesn't rhyme actually restrict and complicate the expression of a poetic idea?" "So what? This restriction arises from the goal that the artist freely sets for himself. It not only complicates but also perfects the expression of the poetic idea, and it exists only for this reason. The more complex the goal, the more difficult the path to it, and consequently, the more restrictions on this path. If you want to build a beautiful building, how many rules of engineering and harmony will define and, therefore, 'restrict' your work! You are free to choose your goals—this is the only human freedom. But once you desire a goal, you thereby desire the means by which it is achieved. We descended into the garden to rest from the multitude of impressions. It was already evening, a clear and soft spring evening. The flowers began to fold their cups and leaves to close them for the night—this general characteristic of Martian plants, born from its cold nights. I resumed the interrupted conversation. "Tell me, what genres of fiction are most prevalent among you now?" "Drama, especially tragedy, and poetry of nature scenes," answered Enno. "What is the content of your tragedy? Where is the material for it in your happy peaceful coexistence?" "Happy? Peaceful? Where did you get that from? Peace reigns among people, that's true, but there is no peace with the elemental forces of nature, and there cannot be. And this is such an enemy, in whose defeat there is always a new threat. In the last period of our history, we have increased the exploitation of our planet tenfold, our population is growing, and our needs are growing even faster. The danger of depletion of natural forces and resources has already arisen before us more than once in one area or another of labor. Until now, we have managed to overcome it without resorting to the hateful reduction of life—in ourselves and in our descendants; but it is precisely now that the struggle is taking on a particularly serious character." "I never thought that with our technical and scientific power, such dangers were possible. You say this has already happened in your history?" "Seventy years ago, when coal reserves were depleted, and the transition to water and electrical energy was far from complete, we had to destroy a significant portion of our planet's precious forests to complete the enormous retooling of machines, which disfigured it for decades and worsened the climate. Then, when we recovered from this crisis, about twenty years ago, it turned out that iron ores were running out. Urgent study of aluminum alloys began, and a huge portion of the technical forces at our disposal was directed towards the electrical extraction of aluminum from the soil. Now, according to statisticians' calculations, we are threatened by a food shortage in thirty years if the synthesis of protein substances from elements is not completed by then." "And other planets?" I objected. "Can't you find something there to supplement the shortage?" "Where? Venus is apparently still inaccessible. Earth? It has its own humanity, and in general, it is not yet clear how much we will be able to use its forces. It requires a huge expenditure of energy each time to travel there; and the reserves of radioactive matter necessary for this, according to Manni, who recently told me about his latest research, are very small on our planet. No, the difficulties are significant everywhere; and the closer our humanity closes ranks to conquer nature, the closer the elements close ranks to take revenge for the victories." "But surely it is enough, for example, to reduce reproduction to fix things?" "Reduce reproduction? But this is the victory of the elements. This is a refusal of unlimited growth of life, it is its inevitable stop at one of the nearest stages. We win as long as we attack. When we refuse to grow our army, it will mean that we are already besieged by the elements from all sides. Then faith in our collective strength, in our great common life, will begin to weaken. And along with this faith, the meaning of each of our lives will also be lost, because within each of us, the small cells of the great organism, the whole lives, and each lives by this whole. No, reducing reproduction is the last thing we would decide on; and when it happens against our will, it will be the beginning of the end." "Well, I understand that the tragedy of the whole always exists for you, at least as a threatening possibility. But as long as victory still belongs to humanity, the individual is sufficiently protected from this tragedy by collectivity; even when direct danger arises, the gigantic efforts and suffering of intense struggle are distributed so evenly among countless individuals that they cannot seriously disrupt their calm happiness. And for such happiness, you seem to have everything you need." "Calm happiness! But can an individual not feel strongly and deeply the upheavals of the life of the whole, in which its beginning and end lie? And do not deep contradictions of life arise from the very limitation of an individual being compared to its whole, from the very powerlessness to fully merge with this whole, to fully dissolve one's consciousness in it and embrace it with one's consciousness? You do not understand these contradictions? This is because they are obscured in your world by others, closer and cruder. The struggle of classes, groups, individuals takes away the idea of the whole from you, and with it, the happiness and the suffering that it brings. I have seen your world; I could not bear even a tenth of the madness in which your brothers live. But precisely because of this, I would not venture to decide: who among us is closer to calm happiness: the more harmonious and orderly life, the more agonizing its inevitable dissonances. "But tell me, Enno, aren't you a happy person? Youth, science, poetry, and probably love… What could you have experienced so difficult to speak so passionately about the tragedy of life?" "That's very fortunate," Enno laughed, and his laughter sounded strange. "You don't know that cheerful Enno once decided to die. And if Manni had been only one day late in writing me six words that ruined all his calculations: 'Wouldn't you like to go to Earth?'—then you wouldn't have your cheerful companion. But now I couldn't explain all this to you. You will see for yourself later that if we have happiness, it is not the peaceful and calm happiness you spoke of. I did not dare to go further into questions. We stood up and returned to the museum. But I could no longer systematically examine the collections: my attention was scattered, my thoughts drifted. I stopped in the sculpture section in front of one of the newest statues depicting a beautiful boy. The features of his face resembled Netti's; but what struck me most was the art with which the artist managed to embody nascent genius in an unformed body, in unfinished features, in the anxious, inquisitive eyes of a child. I stood motionless for a long time before the statue, and everything else ceased to exist in my consciousness when Enno's voice startled me. "It's you," he said, pointing to the boy, "it's your world. It will be a wonderful world, but it is still in its infancy; and look, what vague dreams, what disturbing images stir its consciousness… It is half-asleep, but it will wake up, I feel it, I believe in it deeply!" A strange regret mixed with the joyful feeling these words evoked in me: "Why didn't Netti say this!" 5. AT THE CLINIC I returned home very tired, and after two sleepless nights and a whole day of complete inability to work, I decided to go to Netti again, as I did not want to consult an unfamiliar doctor in the chemical town. Netti had been working at the clinic since morning; there I found him seeing outpatients. When Netti saw me in the reception room, he immediately came up to me, looked intently at my face, took my hand, and led me into a small separate room, where a light, pleasant scent of unfamiliar perfume mixed with soft blue light, and the silence was unbroken. There, he seated me comfortably in a deep armchair and said: "Don't think about anything, don't worry about anything. For today, I'll take care of all that. Rest, I'll come later." He left, and I didn't think about anything, didn't worry about anything, as he took all the thoughts and worries upon himself. It was very pleasant, and after a few minutes, I fell asleep. When I woke up, Netti was standing before me again, looking at me with a smile. "Are you feeling better now?" he asked. "I am completely healthy, and you are a brilliant doctor," I replied. "Go to your patients and don't worry about me." "My work for today is already finished. If you wish, I will show you our clinic," Netti offered. I was very interested, and we went on a tour of the entire extensive, beautiful building. Among the patients, surgical and nervous cases predominated. The majority of surgical cases were victims of accidents with machines. "Don't you have enough safety guards at your factories and plants?" I asked Netti. "Absolute safety guards, making accidents impossible, almost don't exist. But these patients are gathered from a district with a population of over two million people—a few dozen victims are not that many for such a district. Most often, they are newcomers, not yet accustomed to the workings of the machines they operate: after all, we all like to switch from one production area to another. Specialists, scientists, and artists are particularly prone to becoming victims of their absent-mindedness: their attention often fails them, they get lost in thought or contemplation." "And nervous patients—these are, of course, mainly from overwork?" "Yes, quite a few. But no less are illnesses caused by emotional turmoil and crises of sexual life, as well as other emotional shocks, such as the death of loved ones." "And are there mentally ill people here with obscured or confused consciousness?" "No, there are none here; there are separate clinics for them. There, special arrangements are needed for cases where the patient might harm themselves or others." "In these cases, do you resort to violence against patients too?" "As much as is absolutely necessary, of course." "This is the second time I've encountered violence in your world. The first time was in the 'children's home.' Tell me: so, you cannot completely eliminate these elements from your life, you are forced to consciously allow them?" "Yes, just as we allow illness and death, or perhaps, as a bitter medicine. What reasonable being would refuse violence, for example, for self-defense?" "You know, for me, this significantly reduces the gap between our worlds." "But their main difference is not that you have a lot of violence and coercion, and we have little. The main difference is that with you, both are dressed in laws, external and internal, in norms of law and morality, which dominate people and constantly weigh upon them. With us, however, violence exists either as a manifestation of illness or as a rational act of a rational being. In either case, no social laws or norms, no personal or impersonal commands are created from it or for it." "But aren't there rules established here by which you limit the freedom of your mentally ill or your children?" "Yes, purely scientific rules for patient care and pedagogy. But, of course, even in these technical rules, not all cases of necessary violence, nor all methods of its application, nor its degree are foreseen—all this depends on the totality of actual conditions." "But if so, then real arbitrariness by educators or caregivers is possible here?" "What does this word mean—'arbitrariness'? If it means unnecessary, excessive violence, then it is possible only from a sick person who is himself subject to treatment. But a rational and conscious person is, of course, incapable of this." We passed the patients' rooms, operating rooms, medicine rooms, the living quarters of the caregivers, and, ascending to the upper floor, entered a large, beautiful hall, through whose transparent walls a view of the lake, forest, and distant mountains opened. The room was decorated with highly artistic statues and paintings, the furniture was luxurious and elegant. "This is the room for the dying," said Netti. "Do you bring all the dying here?" I asked. "Yes, or they come here themselves," replied Netti. "But can your dying people still walk?" I wondered. "Those who are physically healthy, of course, can." I understood that the conversation was about suicides. "Do you provide this room for suicides to carry out their deed?" "Yes, and all means for a calm, painless death." "And no obstacles?" "If the patient's consciousness is clear and their decision is firm, then what obstacles can there be? The doctor, of course, first suggests that the patient consult with him. Some agree to this, others do not." "And are suicides very frequent among you?" "Yes, especially among the elderly. When the sense of life weakens and dulls, many prefer not to wait for the natural end." "But you must also encounter suicides of young people, full of strength and health?" "Yes, this also happens, but not often. In my memory, there were two such cases in this clinic; in a third case, the attempt was stopped." "Who were these unfortunate people and what led them to their demise?" "The first was my teacher, a famous doctor who contributed much new to science. He had an excessively developed ability to feel the suffering of other people. This directed his mind and energy towards medicine, but it also destroyed him. He could not bear it. He concealed his mental state from everyone so well that the collapse occurred completely unexpectedly. This happened after a severe epidemic that arose during the draining of a sea bay, due to the decomposition of several hundred million kilograms of fish that died there. The disease was agonizing, like your cholera, but even more dangerous and ended in death in nine out of ten cases. Due to this slight possibility of recovery, doctors could not even fulfill their patients' requests for a quick and easy death: after all, a person seized by acute feverish illness cannot be considered fully conscious. My teacher worked madly during the epidemic, and his research helped to end it quite quickly. But when it was done, he refused to live." "How old was he then?" "By our count—about fifty. That is still a very young age for us." "And the second case?" "It was a woman whose husband and child died simultaneously." "And finally, the third case?" "Only the comrade who survived it could tell you about that." "That's true," I said. "But explain something else to me: why do you Martians retain youth for so long? Is it a peculiarity of your race, or a result of better living conditions, or something else?" "The race has nothing to do with it: about two hundred years ago, we were half as long-lived. Better living conditions? Yes, to a significant extent, precisely. But not only that. The main role is played by the renewal of life that we practice." "What is that?" "It's a thing, in essence, very simple, but it will probably seem strange to you. Yet, your science already has all the data for this method. You know that nature, in order to increase the viability of cells or organisms, constantly supplements one individual with another. For this purpose, unicellular organisms, when their viability decreases in a monotonous environment, merge in pairs into one, and only in this way does their reproductive capacity return in full—their protoplasm becomes 'immortal.' The same meaning is carried by sexual crossing of higher plants and animals: here too, the life elements of two different beings are combined to produce a more perfect embryo of a third. Finally, you already know the use of blood serums to transfer elements of vitality from one being to another, so to speak, in parts—in the form, for example, of increased resistance to one disease or another. We go further and arrange an exchange of blood between two human beings, each of whom can transfer to the other a mass of conditions for increasing life. This is simply simultaneous transfusion of blood from one person to another and vice versa, by doubly connecting their blood vessels with appropriate instruments. With all precautions observed, this is completely safe; the blood of one person continues to live in the body of another, mixing there with his blood and bringing deep renewal to all his tissues." "And in this way, can youth be restored to the elderly by infusing their veins with young blood?" "Partly yes, but not entirely, of course, because blood is not everything in the body, and it is, in turn, processed by it. Therefore, for example, a young person does not age from the blood of an older person: what is weak, senile in it, is quickly overcome by the young organism, but at the same time, much is assimilated from it that this organism lacks; its energy and flexibility of life functions also increase." "But if it is so simple, why then does our Earth medicine not use this method? After all, it has known blood transfusions for several hundred years, if I am not mistaken." "I don't know, perhaps there are some specific organic conditions that deprive this method of its significance in your world. Or perhaps it is simply a result of the prevailing individualism psychology, which so deeply separates one person from another in your world that the idea of their vital merging is almost inaccessible to your scientists. Moreover, a multitude of diseases that poison the blood are widespread among you, diseases that the sick themselves often do not know about, and sometimes simply hide. Blood transfusions practiced in your medicine—now very rarely—have a philanthropic character: he who has a lot gives to another who has an acute need for it, due to, for example, severe bleeding from a wound. We also have this, of course; but another practice is constantly applied—what corresponds to our entire system: a friendly exchange of life not only in an ideological but also in a physiological existence…" 6. WORK AND PHANTOMS The impressions of the first days, which flooded my consciousness in a stormy torrent, gave me an idea of the immense scope of the work that lay before me. I had to first comprehend this world, immeasurably rich and unique in its vital harmony. Then I had to enter it not as an interesting museum exhibit, but as a person among people, a worker among workers. Only then could my mission be fulfilled, only then could I serve as the beginning of a real mutual connection between the two worlds, between which I, a socialist, stood on the border as an infinitely small moment of the present between the past and the future. When I was leaving the clinic, Netti told me: "Don't hurry too much!" It seemed to me that he was wrong. It was necessary to hurry, to put all my strength, all my energy into motion, because the responsibility was terrifyingly great! What colossal benefit to our old, exhausted humanity, what gigantic acceleration of its development, its flourishing, the living, energetic influence of higher culture, powerful and harmonious, should bring! And every moment of delay in my work could postpone this influence… No, there was no time to wait, to rest. And I worked a lot. I became acquainted with the science and technology of the new world, I intensely observed its social life, I studied its literature. Yes, there was much that was difficult here. Their scientific methods baffled me: I mechanically assimilated them, convinced by experience that their application was easy, simple, and infallible, yet I did not understand them, did not understand why they led to the goal, where their connection with living phenomena was, what their essence was. I was exactly like those old mathematicians of the 17th century, whose rigid thought could not organically assimilate the living dynamics of infinitesimals. The social gatherings of Martians struck me with their intensely business-like character. Whether they were dedicated to scientific issues, or work organization issues, or even art issues—the reports and speeches were terribly concise and brief, the argumentation definite and precise, no one ever repeated themselves or others. The decisions of the meetings, most often unanimous, were carried out with fabulous speed. A meeting of scientists of one specialty decided that such-and-such a scientific institution should be organized; a meeting of labor statisticians, that such-and-such an enterprise should be established; a meeting of city residents, that it should be decorated with such-and-such a building—immediately new figures of necessary labor appeared, published by the central bureau, hundreds and thousands of new workers arrived by air, and within a few days or weeks, everything was done, and the new workers disappeared without a trace. All this made an impression on me as a kind of magic, strange magic, calm and cold, without incantations and mystical ornaments, but all the more mysterious in its superhuman power. The literature of the new world, even purely artistic, was not a rest or a consolation for me either. Its images seemed simple and clear, but somehow internally alien to me. I wanted to penetrate them more deeply, to make them close and understandable, but my efforts led to a completely unexpected result: the images became phantoms and were shrouded in mist. When I went to the theater, the same feeling of incomprehensibility pursued me here. The plots were simple, the acting superb, yet life remained distant. The heroes' speeches were so restrained and soft, their behavior so calm and cautious, their feelings emphasized so little, as if they did not want to impose any moods on the viewer, as if they were complete philosophers, and moreover, as it seemed to me, strongly idealized. Only historical plays from the distant past gave me somewhat familiar impressions, and the actors' performances there were as energetic and the expression of personal feelings as frank as I was used to seeing in our theaters. There was one circumstance that, despite everything, attracted me to the theater of our small town with particular force. It was precisely that there were no actors at all. The plays I saw there were either transmitted by optical and acoustic transmission devices from distant large cities, or even—and this was most often the case—were reproductions of performances that had taken place long ago, sometimes so long ago that the actors themselves were already dead. Martians, knowing the methods of instant color photography, used them to photograph life in motion, as is done for our cinematographs. But they not only combined the cinematograph with the photographer, as is beginning to be done on Earth—still very unsuccessfully—but they used the idea of the stereoscope and turned the cinematograph images into relief. The screen simultaneously displayed two images—two halves of a stereogram, and in front of each seat in the auditorium was attached a corresponding stereoscopic binocular, which merged the two flat images into one, but in all three dimensions. It was strange to see live people clearly and distinctly moving, acting, expressing their thoughts and feelings, and at the same time realizing that there was nothing there, but a matte plate and behind it—a phonograph and an electric lantern with a clockwork mechanism. It was almost mystically strange and gave rise to a vague doubt about all reality. All this, however, did not make it easier for me to fulfill my task—to understand the alien world. 2. SEPARATION

And still, that day arrived, a day I cannot recall without a curse, a day when a dark shadow of hateful and inevitable... separation fell between me and Netty. With a calm and clear expression, as always, Netty told me that she had to leave soon with a giant expedition being fitted out for Venus under Manni's command. Seeing how stunned I was by this news, she added: "It won't be long; if successful, which I have no doubt about, part of the expedition will return very soon, and I will be among them."

Then she began to explain the situation. On Mars, the supply of radiomatter, necessary as a propellant for interplanetary communication and as a tool for the decomposition and synthesis of all elements, was running out: it was only being consumed, and there were no means to replenish it. On Venus, a young planet that had existed for almost a quarter of the time Mars had, the presence of colossal deposits of radiating substances, which had not yet had time to decompose naturally, had been established by undeniable signs. On one island, located in the middle of Venus's main ocean and bearing the Martian name "Island of Hot Storms," lay the richest ore of radiomatter; and there, it was decided to begin its immediate exploitation. But first, it was necessary to build very high and strong walls to protect the workers from the deadly effects of the humid hot wind, which, with its ferocity, far surpassed the storms of our sandy deserts. Therefore, an expedition of ten etheronefs and one and a half to two thousand people was required, of whom only one-twentieth were for chemical work, and almost all the rest for construction. The best scientific minds were involved, including the most experienced doctors: health dangers threatened both from the climate and from the deadly rays and emanations of the radiating substance. Netty, she said, could not escape participation in the expedition; but it was expected that if the work went well, one etheronef would depart within three months with news and a supply of extracted substance. Netty was to return with this etheronef, meaning 10-11 months after departure.

I could not understand why Netty had to go. She told me that the undertaking was too serious to be refused; that it was of great importance for my mission as well, since its success would for the first time enable frequent and extensive communication with Earth; that any mistake in providing medical assistance from the very beginning could lead to the ruin of the entire endeavor. All this was convincing – I had already learned that Netty was considered the best doctor for all cases that went beyond the scope of old medical experience – and yet, it seemed to me that this was not all. I felt there was something unspoken. In one thing I had no doubt – in Netty herself and in her love. If she said it was necessary to go, then it was necessary; if she didn't say why, then I shouldn't question her. I saw fear and pain in her beautiful eyes when she thought I wasn't looking at her.

"Enno will be a good and kind friend to you," she said with a sad smile, "and don't forget Nellu; she loves you for me, she has a lot of experience and wisdom, her support in difficult moments is invaluable. And about me, just think one thing: that I will return as soon as possible."
"I believe in you, Netty," I said, "and therefore I believe in myself, in the person whom you have loved."
"You are right, Lanny. And I am convinced that from under any burden of fate, from any ruin, you will emerge true to yourself, stronger and purer than before."

3. THE CLOTHING FACTORY

In those short months, with Netty's help, I had largely prepared to fulfill my main plan – to become a useful worker in Martian society. I consciously rejected all offers to lecture about Earth and its people: it would have been unwise to make it my specialty, as it would have meant artificially arresting my knowledge on the images of my past, which could not leave me anyway, instead of the future that had to be conquered. I decided to simply get a job at a factory and chose, for the first time, after thorough comparison and discussion, the clothing factory. I chose, of course, almost the easiest. But even here, considerable and serious preparatory work was required. I had to study the scientifically developed principles of factory organization in general, familiarize myself specifically with the organization of the factory where I was to work, its architecture, its labor organization, and I especially had to deal with – of course, in all the machines used there, and the machine with which I was specifically supposed to deal – of course, in all its details. In addition, it became necessary to preliminarily master some departments of general and applied mechanics and technology, and even mathematical analysis. The main difficulties arose for me not from the content of what I had to study, but from the form. Textbooks and manuals were not designed for a person of lower culture. I recalled how, as a child, I was tormented by a French mathematics textbook that I happened to get my hands on. I had a serious inclination for this subject and, seemingly, considerable aptitude for it: ideas of "limit" and "derivative," difficult for most beginners, came to me somehow imperceptibly, as if I had always been familiar with them. But I lacked the logical discipline and practice of scientific thinking that the French professor, very clear and precise in his expressions but very stingy with explanations, presumed in the reader-student. He constantly skipped the logical bridges that might have been implied for a person of higher scientific culture, but not for a young Asian. And more than once, I spent hours pondering some magical transformation following the words: "whence, taking into account the previous equations, we derive..."

It was the same with me now, only even more so, when I read Martian scientific books; the illusion that possessed me at the beginning of my illness, when everything seemed easy and understandable, had vanished without a trace. But Netty's patient help was constantly with me and smoothed the difficult path.

Soon after Netty's departure, I made up my mind and joined the factory. It was a gigantic and very complex enterprise, entirely unlike our usual conception of a clothing factory. It combined spinning, weaving, cutting, sewing, and dyeing of clothes, and the material worked with was not linen, not cotton, and generally not plant fibers, nor wool, nor silk, but something entirely different. In former times, Martians prepared fabrics for clothing in approximately the same way as it is done with us: they cultivated fibrous plants, sheared wool from suitable animals and skinned them, bred special breeds of spiders, from whose webs a silk-like substance was obtained, and so on. The impetus for changing the technology was given by the necessity to increase the production of bread more and more. Fibrous plants began to be replaced by fibrous minerals like asbestos. Then chemists directed their efforts to the study of spider silk fabrics and the synthesis of new substances with similar properties. When they succeeded, a complete revolution occurred in this entire branch of industry in a short time, and now old-type fabrics are found only in historical museums. Our factory was the true embodiment of this revolution.

Several times a month, "material" for yarn was delivered from nearby chemical plants via rail in the form of a semi-liquid transparent substance in large tanks. From these tanks, the material, using special apparatus that excluded air, was poured into a huge, high-hung metal reservoir, the flat bottom of which had hundreds of thousands of microscopic holes. Through these holes, the viscous liquid was pressed under high pressure in the thinnest streams, which, under the action of air, solidified within a few centimeters and turned into transparent silk-like fibers. Tens of thousands of mechanical spindles picked up these fibers, twisted them into dozens to form threads of various thicknesses and densities, and pulled them further, passing the finished "yarn" to the next weaving department. There, on looms, the threads were interlaced into various fabrics, from the most delicate, like voile and batiste, to the densest, like broadcloth and felt, which stretched further in endless wide ribbons to the cutting workshop. Here, new machines picked them up, carefully folded them into many layers, and cut out thousands of pre-determined and measured patterns for individual costume parts from them according to drawings. In the sewing workshop, the cut pieces were sewn into ready-made garments, but without any needles, threads, or sewing machines. The neatly folded edges of the pieces were softened by a special chemical solvent, returning to their semi-liquid state, and when the very volatile dissolving substance evaporated a minute later, the pieces of fabric were firmly joined, better than any seam could have done. Simultaneously, fasteners were soldered wherever required, so that finished costume parts were produced – several thousand samples, varying in shape and size. For each age, there were several hundred samples, from which almost anyone could almost always choose a perfectly suitable one, especially since Martian clothing is usually very loose. If a suitable one could not be found, for example, due to not entirely normal proportions, a special measurement was taken immediately, a cutting machine was set up according to new patterns, and it was "sewn" specifically for that person, which took about an hour. As for the color of the costume, most Martians are satisfied with the usual shades, dark and soft, in which the fabric itself is prepared. If a color other than the one available is required, the costume is sent to the dyeing department, where, in a few minutes, using electrochemical methods, it acquires the desired color, perfectly even and perfectly durable.

From the same fabrics, only much denser and stronger, and using similar methods, footwear and warm winter clothing are prepared. Our factory did not engage in this, but other, even larger ones, produced absolutely everything needed to dress a person from head to toe.

I worked successively in all departments of the factory and was initially very absorbed in my work. It was especially interesting to work in the cutting department, where I had to apply new methods of mathematical analysis to practice. The task was to cut out all the parts of a costume from a given piece of fabric with the least material loss. A very prosaic, but very serious task, because even a minimal error, repeated millions of times, would result in enormous waste. And I managed to achieve a successful solution "no worse" than others. To work "no worse" than others – this I strove for with all my might and, in general, not without success. But I could not help noticing that it cost me much greater effort than the other workers. After the usual 4-6 (by Earth reckoning) hours of labor, I was very tired and needed immediate rest, while others went to museums, libraries, laboratories, or other factories to observe production, and sometimes even to work there... I hoped that habit to new types of labor would come and make me equal to all workers. But this did not happen. I became increasingly convinced that I lacked the culture of attention. Very little physical movement was required, and in their speed and dexterity, I did not yield, even surpassed many. But it required such continuous and intense attention to observe the machines and the material, which was very difficult for my brain: evidently, only over several generations could this ability develop to the degree that was ordinary and average here. When – usually by the end of my workday – fatigue began to manifest, and my attention began to fail me, I would make a mistake or slow down the execution of some work act for a second, then the hand of one of my neighbors would invariably and unerringly correct the matter. Not only was I surprised, but sometimes outright indignant at their strange ability to notice everything around them without taking their eyes off their work. Their solicitousness did not so much touch me as provoke annoyance and irritation in me; I felt as if they were constantly watching my actions… This restless mood further increased my absentmindedness and spoiled my work.

Now, after a long time, when I recall all the circumstances carefully and impartially, I find that all this was perceived incorrectly. With exactly the same solicitousness and in exactly the same way – perhaps only less often – my comrades at the factory helped each other as well. I was not the subject of any exceptional supervision and control, as it seemed to me then. I myself – a person from an individualistic world – involuntarily and unconsciously singled myself out from the rest and painfully perceived their kindness and comradely services, for which, it seemed to me, a person from a commodity world, I had nothing to repay.

4. ENNO

That long autumn passed; winter, little snowy but cold, reigned in our region – in the middle latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere. The small sun did not warm at all and shone less than before. Netty's verdict was a terrible blow to Enno, in whom love for a brilliant person and a deep maternal instinct had merged into one passionate aspiration, which had suddenly proven hopeless.

But that was not all; the investigation led Netty to another result. It turned out that for Manni's immense intellectual work, for the full development of his brilliant abilities, as much physical restraint as possible was needed, as little of amorous caresses as possible. Enno could not help but follow this advice and quickly became convinced of its fairness and wisdom. Manni became livelier, worked more energetically than ever, new plans rapidly arose in his head and were executed with particular success, and he seemed to feel no deprivation. Then Enno, for whom her love was dearer than life, but the genius of her beloved was dearer than love, drew all the conclusions from what she had learned. She separated from Manni; he was saddened by this at first, but then quickly reconciled himself to the fact. The true reason for the breakup may have even remained unknown to him: Enno and Netty kept it a secret; although, of course, one could not be sure whether Manni's perceptive mind had not guessed the hidden undercurrent of events. And for Enno, life turned out to be so empty, the suppressed feeling created such suffering that after a short time the young woman decided to die. To prevent suicide, Netty, to whose assistance Enno had turned, delayed its execution for a day under various pretexts, and informed Manni herself. He was organizing an expedition to Earth at that time and immediately sent Enno an invitation to participate in this important and dangerous undertaking. It was difficult to refuse. Enno accepted the offer. A mass of new impressions helped her cope with her heartache; and by the time she returned to Mars, she had already managed to control herself to such an extent that she could adopt the guise of the cheerful poet boy whom I knew on the etheronef. Enno did not go on the new expedition because she was afraid of becoming too accustomed to Manni's presence again. But anxiety for his fate did not leave her as long as she remained alone: she knew the dangers of the undertaking too well.

On long winter evenings, our thoughts and conversations constantly returned to the same point in the universe: to where, under the rays of a huge sun, under the breath of a scorching wind, the beings dearest to both of us were feverishly carrying out their titanic, bold work. This unity of thought and mood brought us deeply together. Enno was more than a sister to me. Somehow, naturally, without impulse or struggle, our closeness led us to a loving relationship. Always meek and kind, Enno did not shy away from this intimacy, though she did not seek it herself. She had only decided not to have children with me... There was a shade of gentle sadness in her caresses – caresses of tender friendship that permits everything... And winter still spread its cold, pale wings over us – a long Martian winter without thaws, storms, or blizzards, calm and motionless as death. And neither of us had any desire to fly south, where the sun was warming at that time and nature, full of life, was unfolding its bright attire. Enno did not want that kind of nature, too little harmonious with her mood; as for me, I avoided new people and new surroundings, because getting acquainted with and accustomed to them required new, extra effort and fatigue, and I was already progressing too slowly toward my goal. Our friendship was phantasmagorically strange – love in the kingdom of winter, worry, waiting...

5. AT NELLA'S

Even in her early youth, Enno was Netty's closest friend and told me a lot about her. During one of our conversations, a combination of Netty's and Sterni's names struck me as strange. When I asked a direct question, Enno first thought and seemed even embarrassed, and then replied: "Netty was Sterni's wife before. If she didn't tell you, then I shouldn't have either. I obviously made a mistake, and don't ask me any more about it."

I was strangely shaken by what I heard... As if there was nothing new... I had never assumed that I was Netty's first husband. It would have been absurd to think that a woman full of life and health, beautiful in body and soul, a child of a free, highly cultured race, could have lived without love until our meeting. What then caused my incomprehensibly stunned state? I could not reason about it; I felt only one thing: that I needed to know everything, to know it clearly and precisely. But it was obviously impossible to interrogate Enno. I remembered Nellu. Netty, when she was leaving, said: "Don't forget Nellu, go to her in a difficult moment!" And I had already thought more than once about how to see her; but partly work, and partly some vague fear of the hundreds of curious childish eyes that surrounded her, prevented me. But now all indecision disappeared, and that very day I was at the "children's house," in the Great City of Machines. Nellu immediately dropped the work she was engaged in, and, asking another caregiver to substitute for her, led me to her room, where the children could not disturb us. I decided not to tell her the purpose of my visit right away, especially since the purpose itself did not seem particularly reasonable or particularly noble to me. It was most natural that I started a conversation about the person closest to both of us; and then it remained to wait for the right moment for my question.

Nellu spoke at length and with enthusiasm about Netty, about her childhood and youth. Netty spent her first years of life with her mother, as is the case with most Martians. Then, when it was time to send Netty to the "children's house" so as not to deprive her of the educational influence of child society, Nellu could no longer part with her and at first temporarily settled in the same house, and then permanently remained there as a caregiver. This also suited her scientific specialty: she was mainly involved in psychology. Netty was a lively, energetic, impulsive child, with a great thirst for knowledge and activity. The mysterious astronomical world beyond the planet especially interested and attracted her. Earth, which they had not yet managed to reach, and its invisible people were Netty's favorite dream, her favorite topic of conversation with other children and caregivers. When the report of Manni's first successful expedition to Earth was published, the girl was almost beside herself with joy and admiration. She remembered Manni's report word for word and therefore tormented Nellu and the caregivers with requests to explain every incomprehensible term in this report. She fell in love with Manni from afar and wrote him an enthusiastic letter; in this letter, among other things, she begged him to bring a child from Earth, whom no one was raising; she promised to raise him in the best possible way. She decorated her entire room with Earth landscapes and portraits of Earth people and began to study dictionaries of Earth languages as soon as they appeared in print. She was indignant at the violence that Manni and his companions used against the first Earthling they met; they took him captive so that he could help them learn Earth languages; and at the same time, she deeply regretted that, upon returning, they released him instead of bringing him to Mars. She firmly decided to go to Earth someday and, in response to her mother's joke that she would marry an Earthling there, after thinking, declared: "Very possibly!"

Netty herself never told me all this; in her conversations, she generally avoided touching upon the past. And of course, no one, not even she herself, could have told it better than Nellu. How brightly maternal love shone in Nellu's descriptions! At times, I completely forgot myself and saw before me the wonderful girl with burning large eyes and a mysterious attraction to the distant, distant world... But this quickly passed: consciousness of my surroundings returned, and the memory of the purpose of the conversation, and my soul became cold again.

Finally, when the conversation turned to Netty's later years, I decided to ask, with a calm and unforced air, how Netty's and Sterni's relationship had begun. Nellu thought for a minute.
"Ah, that's it!" she said. "So you came to me because of this... Why didn't you tell me that directly? An unusual strictness sounded in her voice. I remained silent.
"Of course, I can tell you this," she continued. "It's a very simple story. Sterni was one of Netty's teachers; he gave lectures on mathematics and astronomy to the youth. When he returned from his first trip to Earth – it was, I think, Manni's second expedition – he gave a series of lectures about this planet and its inhabitants. Netty was his constant listener. Their relationship especially deepened due to the patience and attention with which he treated her endless questions. The closeness led to marriage. It was a kind of polar attraction of two very dissimilar, in many ways opposite natures. Later, the same dissimilarity, manifesting itself more constantly and in its entirety in their joint life, led to a cooling and a breakup. That's all."
"Tell me, when did this breakup occur?"
"Finally – after Letta's death. Actually, Netty's and Letta's rapprochement was already the beginning of this breakup. Netty felt uncomfortable with Sterni's analytically cold mind; he too systematically and persistently destroyed all the castles in the air, all the fantasies of mind and feeling, which she lived on so much and so intensely. She involuntarily began to seek a person who would relate to all this differently. And old Letta had a remarkably responsive heart and a lot of childlike enthusiasm. Netty found in him the companion she needed: he not only knew how to patiently accept the impulses of her imagination but often got carried away with her. With him, her soul rested from Sterni's harsh, all-freezing criticism. He, like her, loved Earth with dreams and fantasy, believed in a future union of two worlds that would bring great flourishing and great poetry of life. And when she learned that a person with such a treasure of feeling in his soul had never known a woman's love and tenderness, she could not reconcile herself to it. Thus arose her second relationship."

... Why didn't Netty tell me all this? And how many more secrets and deceptions are around me? And how many more await in the future? Again, untruth! A secret – yes, that's true. But there was no deceit here. And isn't a secret a deceit in this case?... These thoughts whirled in my head when the door opened and Nellu entered the room again. She had obviously read on my face how difficult it was for me, because there was no longer the former dryness and sternness in her tone when she addressed me.
"Of course," she said, "it's not easy to get used to completely foreign life relationships and the customs of another world with which you have no blood ties. You have already overcome many obstacles, you will overcome this one too. Netty believes in you, and I think she is right. Has your faith in her wavered?"
"Why did she hide all this from me? Where is her faith in this? I can't understand her."
"Why she acted this way, I don't know. But I know that she must have had serious and good, not petty, motives for it. Perhaps this letter will explain it to you. She left it for you in case of a conversation like the one we just had. The letter was written in my native language, which my Netty had learned so well. Here is what I read there.

'My Lanny! I have never spoken to you about my previous personal relationships, but it was not because I wanted to hide anything from you about my life. I deeply trust your clear mind and noble heart; I have no doubt that, however foreign and unusual some of our life relationships may be to you, you will ultimately always be able to correctly understand and fairly evaluate them. But I feared one thing... After your illness, you quickly regained strength for work, but that mental equilibrium, upon which self-control in words and actions depends at every moment and under every impression, has not yet fully returned to you. If, under the influence of the moment and the elemental forces of the past, which always lie deep within the human soul, you were to reveal, even for a second, to me, as a woman, that negative attitude born of violence and slavery that prevails in the old world, you would never forgive yourself for it. Yes, my dear, I know you are strict, often even cruel to yourself – you have carried this trait from your harsh school of eternal struggle in the earthly world, and one second of a bad, painful impulse would forever remain a dark spot on our love. My Lanny, I want and can reassure you. Let the evil feeling that connects anxiety for living property with love for a person sleep and never awaken in your soul. I will have no other personal relationships. I can easily and confidently promise you this, because before my love for you, before the passionate desire to help you in your great life task, everything else becomes so petty and insignificant. I love you not only as a wife, I love you as a mother who leads her child into a new and alien life, full of effort and danger. This love is stronger and deeper than any other that a person can have for another. And therefore, there is no sacrifice in my promise. Goodbye, my dear, beloved child. Your Netty.'"

When I finished reading the letter, Nellu looked at me questioningly.
"You were right," I said and kissed her hand.

6. IN SEARCH

From this episode, a feeling of deep humiliation remained in my soul. Even more painfully than before, I began to perceive the superiority of those around me, both at the factory and in all other interactions with Martians. Undoubtedly, I even exaggerated this superiority and my own weakness. In their goodwill and concern for me, I began to see a hint of condescending indulgence, in their careful reserve – a hidden aversion to an inferior being. The accuracy of perception and the correctness of evaluation were increasingly disrupted in this direction. In all other respects, my thoughts remained clear, and now they worked particularly strongly to fill the gaps related to Netty's departure. More than before, I was convinced that there were still unknown motives for Netty's participation in this expedition, stronger and more important than those presented to me. Netty's new proof of love and the immense importance she attached to my mission in bringing the two worlds closer was new confirmation that without exceptional reasons, she would not have dared to leave me for a long time amidst the depths and shallows and hidden reefs of an alien ocean, understanding better and clearer than I myself what dangers threatened me there. There was something I didn't know, but I was convinced it had some serious relation to me; and it was necessary to find out this something at all costs. I decided to get to the truth through systematic research. Recalling some accidental and involuntary hints from Netty, the restless expression I had long ago caught on her face when colonial expeditions were mentioned in my presence, I concluded that Netty decided on this separation not when she told me about it, but a very long time ago, no later than the first days of our marriage. Therefore, the causes must be sought around that time. But where to look for them? They could be connected either with Netty's personal affairs or with the origin, character, and significance of the expedition itself. The former, after Netty's letter, seemed least likely. Therefore, first of all, I had to direct my search in the second direction and start by fully clarifying the history of the expedition's origin.

It goes without saying that the expedition was decided by the "colonial group" – this was the name given to the gathering of workers actively participating in the organization of interplanetary travel, along with representatives from central statistics and from the factories that produce etheronefs and generally provide the necessary means for these travels. I knew that the last congress of this "colonial group" took place precisely during my illness. Manni and Netty participated there. Since I was already recovering then and was bored without Netty, I wanted to be at this congress too, but Netty said it was dangerous for my health. Did the "danger" depend on something I should not have known? It was obviously necessary to obtain the exact minutes of the congress and read everything that could relate to the question. But here I encountered difficulties. In the colonial library, I was given only a collection of the congress's decisions. The decisions perfectly outlined, almost to the smallest detail, the entire organization of the grandiose undertaking on Venus, but there was nothing that specifically interested me now. This, in no way, exhausted the question for me. The decisions, despite their thoroughness, were presented without any reasoning, without any indication of the entire discussion that preceded them. When I told the librarian that I needed the minutes themselves, he explained that the minutes were not published and that such minutes were generally not kept, as is usually done at technical-business meetings. At first glance, this seemed plausible. Martians indeed most often publish only the decisions of their technical congresses, believing that any reasonable and useful opinion expressed there would either find reflection in the adopted resolution or would be explained more thoroughly and circumstantially by the author in a separate article, brochure, or book, if the author himself found it important. Martians generally dislike over-multiplying literature, and one cannot find anything like our multi-volume "commission works" among them: everything is compressed to the smallest possible volume. But in this case, I did not believe the librarian. Too important and significant matters were decided at the congress to be treated as ordinary debates on some ordinary technical issue. However, I tried to hide my disbelief and, to divert any suspicion from myself, meekly delved into the study of what I had been given, while actually considering a plan of further action.

It was obvious that I would not get what I needed in the book library: the minutes were either truly nonexistent, or the librarian, warned by my question, would skillfully hide them from me. There was another option – the phonographic department of the library. There, minutes might be found even if they were not published in print. The phonograph often replaces shorthand for Martians, and their archives store many unpublished phonograms of various public meetings. I chose the moment when the book librarian was deeply engrossed in his work and, unnoticed by him, went to the phonographic department. There, I asked the duty comrade for a large catalog of phonograms. He gave it to me. According to the catalog, I quickly found the numbers of the phonograms of the congress that interested me and, pretending not to want to bother the duty comrade by asking him to fetch them, went to find them myself. This also succeeded easily. There were fifteen phonograms in total, corresponding to the number of congress sessions. Each, as Martians usually do, had a written table of contents attached. I quickly reviewed them. The first five sessions were entirely devoted to reports on expeditions conducted after the previous congress and new improvements in etheronef technology. The heading of the sixth phonogram read: "Proposal by Central Statistics on the transition to mass colonization. Choice of planet – Earth and Venus. Speeches and proposals by Sterni, Netty, Manni, and others. Preliminary decision in favor of Venus." I felt that this was what I was looking for. I inserted the phonogram into the apparatus. What I heard was forever etched in my soul. Here is what was there.

The sixth session was opened by Manni, the chairman of the congress. The first to speak with a report was a representative of central statistics. Using a series of precise figures, he proved that with the current population growth and the progress of its needs, if Martians were to limit themselves to exploiting the entire planet, then within thirty years a shortage of food resources would begin. This could be prevented by discovering the technical synthesis of proteins from inorganic matter, but there was no guarantee that this could be achieved within thirty years. Therefore, it was absolutely necessary for the colonial group to move from simple scientific excursions to other planets to organizing real mass resettlement of Martians there. There are two planets available to Martians with vast natural resources. It is necessary to immediately decide which one to make the center of colonization first, and then proceed to develop a plan. Manni asks if anyone wishes to object in substance to the proposal of central statistics or its justification. No one wishes to object. Then Manni puts to discussion the question of which planet to choose first in the matter of mass colonization. Sterni takes the floor.

7. STERNI

"The first question posed to us by the representative of central statistics," Sterni began in his usual, mathematically business-like tone, "the question of choosing a planet for colonization, in my opinion, needs no decision, because it has long been decided, decided by reality itself. There is nothing to choose from. Of the two planets now available to us, only one is generally suitable for mass colonization. That is Earth. There is extensive literature about Venus, which you are all, of course, familiar with. The conclusion from all the data collected in it can only be one: we cannot conquer Venus now. Its scorching sun will exhaust and weaken our colonists, its terrible thunderstorms and storms will destroy our buildings, scatter our aeroplanes into space and smash them against colossal mountains. We could still cope with its monsters, albeit at the cost of considerable sacrifices; but its bacterial world, terrifyingly rich in forms, is known to us only to a negligible extent – and how many new diseases does it hide for us? Its volcanic forces are still in restless fermentation; how many unexpected earthquakes, lava eruptions, oceanic floods do they promise us. Reasonable beings should not undertake the impossible. An attempt to colonize Venus would give us innumerable, but also useless sacrifices, not sacrifices of science and general happiness, but sacrifices of madness and dreams. This question, it seems to me, is clear, and the report of the last expedition to Venus alone could hardly have left any doubts in anyone. Thus, if it is about mass resettlement at all, then, of course, it is only about resettlement on Earth. Here, the obstacles from nature are negligible, its riches are incalculable – they exceed what our planet provides by eight times. The very act of colonization is well prepared by the existing, albeit not high, culture on Earth. All this, of course, is also known to central statistics. If it proposes the question of choice to us, and we find it necessary to discuss it, it is solely because Earth presents us with one very serious obstacle. That is its humanity. The people of Earth own it, and under no circumstances will they voluntarily give it up, not even a significant portion of its surface. This follows from the entire character of their culture. Its foundation is property, guarded by an organized population. Although even the most civilized tribes of Earth practically exploit only a negligible part of the forces of nature available to them, the desire to seize new territories never weakens in them. The systematic plunder of lands and property of less civilized tribes is called colonial policy among them and is considered one of the main tasks of their state life. One can imagine how they will react to a natural and reasonable proposal from us – to cede part of their continents to us, in exchange for which we would teach them and help them use the remaining part incomparably better... For them, colonization is a matter of brute force and violence; and whether we want it or not, they will force us to adopt this point of view in relation to them. If it were only a matter of proving our strength to them once, it would be relatively simple and would require no more sacrifices than any of their usual senseless and useless wars. The large herds of people trained for murder, called armies, would serve as the most suitable material for such necessary violence. Any of our etheronefs could, through a stream of destructive rays generated by the accelerated decomposition of radium, destroy one or two such herds in a few minutes, and this would be more beneficial than harmful even to their culture. But, unfortunately, the matter is not so simple, and the main difficulties would only begin from this moment. In the eternal struggle between the tribes of Earth, they have developed a psychological peculiarity called patriotism. This vague, but strong and deep feeling encompasses both malicious distrust of all foreign peoples and races, and a spontaneous habit to their common living environment, especially to the territory with which Earth tribes grow together like a turtle with its shell, and a kind of collective self-importance, and, often it seems, a simple thirst for extermination, violence, and seizure. The patriotic state of mind is greatly intensified and sharpened after military defeats, especially when the victors take territory from the vanquished; then the patriotism of the defeated acquires the character of long-lasting and cruel hatred towards the victors, and revenge becomes the life ideal of the entire tribe, not only its worst elements – the "higher," or ruling, classes, but also the best – its working masses. And so, if we were to take part of the Earth's surface by necessary force, it would undoubtedly lead to the unification of all Earth's humanity in a single feeling of Earthly patriotism, in a merciless racial hatred and malice against our colonists; exterminating the invaders by any means, even the most treacherous, would become a sacred and noble feat in the eyes of people, granting immortal glory. The existence of our colonists would become completely unbearable. You know that the destruction of life is generally very easy, even for our culture; we are immeasurably stronger than Earth people in open combat, but in unexpected attacks, they can kill us just as successfully as they usually do to each other. It should also be noted that the art of extermination is developed in them incomparably higher than all other aspects of their peculiar culture. Living together with them and among them would, of course, be simply impossible; it would mean eternal conspiracies and terror on their part, constant awareness of an inescapable danger, and countless sacrifices for our comrades. We would have to evict them from all the areas we occupied – evict tens, perhaps hundreds of millions at once. With their social structure, which does not recognize comradely mutual support, with their social relations that stipulate services and assistance by payment of money, and finally, with their clumsy and inflexible production methods, which do not allow for a sufficiently rapid increase in productivity and redistribution of labor products, – these millions of evicted people would, in the vast majority, be doomed to a torturous death by starvation. And the surviving minority would form the cadres of embittered, fanatical agitators against us among the rest of humanity on Earth. Then the struggle would have to continue. Our entire Earthly territory would have to turn into a continuously guarded military camp. The fear of further seizures by us and great racial hatred would direct all the forces of the Earth's tribes towards preparing and organizing wars against us. If even now their weapons are far superior to their tools, then the progress of extermination technology will go even incomparably faster. At the same time, they will search for and lie in wait for opportunities for a sudden outbreak of war, and if they succeed, they will undoubtedly inflict great irreparable losses on us, even if the matter ends in our victory. Moreover, it is not impossible that they will somehow learn the design of our main weapon. Radiating matter is already known to them, and the method of its accelerated decomposition can either be discovered by them in some way from us, or even independently discovered by their scientists. But you know that with such a weapon, whoever preempts the enemy by a few minutes inevitably destroys them; and it is as easy to destroy higher life in this case as the most elementary. What kind of existence would our comrades have among these dangers and this eternal waiting? Not only all the joys of life would be poisoned, but life itself would soon be perverted and debased. Suspicion, vindictiveness, selfish self-preservation, and the inseparable cruelty would gradually penetrate it. This colony would cease to be our colony, turning into a military republic among conquered, perpetually hostile tribes. Repeated attacks with their victims would not only generate feelings of revenge and malice, distorting the image of man dear to us, but would also objectively force a transition from self-defense to merciless offense. And in the end, after long hesitations and a fruitless, torturous waste of strength, the matter would inevitably lead to the formulation of the question that we, conscious beings who foresee the course of events, must adopt from the very beginning: colonization of Earth requires the complete extermination of Earthly humanity. (Among hundreds of listeners, a murmur of horror passes, from which a loud, indignant exclamation from Netty stands out.)

When silence is restored, Sterni calmly continues: "We must understand necessity and look it squarely in the face, no matter how harsh it may be. We face one of two things: either a halt in the development of our life, or the destruction of alien life on Earth. There is nothing else before us." (Netty's voice: "Untrue!" I know what Netty means by protesting my words, and I will now discuss the third possibility she offers. - "This is an attempt at immediate socialist re-education of Earthly humanity, a plan to which we all recently inclined and from which, in my opinion, we must now inevitably abandon. We know enough about the people of Earth to understand the complete infeasibility of this idea. The level of culture of the advanced peoples of Earth approximately corresponds to the level on which our ancestors stood in the era of digging the Great Canals. Capitalism also reigns there, and there is a proletariat fighting for socialism. Based on this, one could think that the moment of a revolution that would eliminate the system of organized violence and create the possibility for the free and rapid development of human life is not far off. But Earthly capitalism has important peculiarities that greatly change the whole matter. On the one hand, the Earthly world is terribly fragmented by political and national divisions, so that the struggle for socialism is not carried out as a single, coherent process in one vast society, but as a whole series of independent and peculiar processes in separate societies, separated by state organization, language, and sometimes race. On the other hand, the forms of social struggle there are much cruder and more mechanical than they were with us, and direct material violence, embodied in constant armies and armed uprisings, plays an incomparably larger role in them. Because of all this, the issue of social revolution becomes very uncertain: not one, but many social revolutions are foreseen, in different countries at different times, and even, in many ways, probably of different character, and most importantly – with a doubtful and unstable outcome. The ruling classes, relying on the army and high military technology, in some cases can inflict such a devastating defeat on the rebellious proletariat that in entire vast states the struggle for socialism will be pushed back for decades; and examples of this kind have already occurred in the annals of Earth. Then, individual advanced countries where socialism triumphs will be like islands amidst a hostile capitalist, and partly even pre-capitalist, world. Fighting for their own dominance, the higher classes of non-socialist countries will direct all their efforts to destroy these islands, will constantly organize military attacks on them, and will find enough allies among the socialist nations, ready for any government, from among the former owners, large and small. The result of these conflicts is difficult to predict. But even where socialism prevails and emerges victorious, its character will be deeply and for a long time distorted by many years of siege conditions, necessary terror, and militarism, with the inevitable consequence – barbarous patriotism. This will be far from our socialism. The task of our intervention, according to our previous plans, was to accelerate and assist the triumph of socialism. In what way is this possible for us? Firstly, we can transfer our technology, our science, our skill in mastering the forces of nature to the people of Earth, and thereby raise their culture so abruptly that the backward forms of economic and political life will be in too sharp contradiction with it and will fall due to their unsuitability. Secondly, we can directly support the socialist proletariat in its revolutionary struggle and help it break the resistance of the two classes. There are no other ways. But do these two achieve their goal? We know enough now to answer definitively: no! What will be the result of communicating our technical knowledge and methods to Earthlings? The ruling classes of all countries will first seize them for their own benefit and increase their power with them. This is inevitable, because they have all the material means of labor in their hands, and ninety-nine percent of all scientists and engineers serve them – which means they will have the entire possibility of applying new technology. And they will use it exactly to the extent that it is beneficial to them and increases their power over the masses. Moreover: they will immediately try to use the new and powerful means of extermination and destruction that will fall into their hands to suppress the socialist proletariat. They will tenfold their persecution and organize a broad provocation to quickly force it into open combat and crush it in this combat, its conscious and best forces, ideologically decapitate it, before it has time, in turn, to master new, better methods of military violence. Thus, our intervention will serve as a trigger for reaction from above and at the same time give it a weapon of unprecedented power. In the final analysis, it will delay the struggle of socialism for decades. And what would we achieve by trying to provide direct assistance to the socialist proletariat against its enemies? Let's assume – this is not yet certain – that it will accept an alliance with us. The first victories will then be won easily. But further? The inevitable development among all other classes of society of the most embittered and rabid patriotism directed against us and against the socialists of Earth... The proletariat still represents a minority in almost all, even the most advanced countries of Earth; the majority are formed by the remnants of the petty bourgeoisie, which have not yet decomposed, masses of the most ignorant and dark. To fully incite all of them against the proletariat will then be very easy for the large landowners and their closest servants – officials and scientists, because these masses, conservative in their essence and even partly reactionary, perceive any rapid progress very painfully. The advanced proletariat, surrounded on all sides by terribly embittered, merciless enemies – to whom large segments of proletarians lagging in development will join – will find itself in a no less unbearable situation than our colonists would find themselves among the defeated Earth tribes. There will be countless treacherous attacks, pogroms, massacres, and, most importantly, the entire position of the proletariat in society will be as unfavorable as possible for transforming society. And again, our intervention will not bring the social revolution closer, but will slow it down. The time for this revolution, therefore, remains indefinite, and it is not up to us to accelerate it. In any case, we would have to wait for it much longer than is possible for us. In 30 years, we will have 15-20 million surplus population, and then it will increase by another 20-25 million each year. Significant colonization must be carried out in advance; otherwise, we will not have enough strength and means to immediately carry it out to the necessary extent. Moreover, it is more than doubtful that we would be able to peacefully come to terms even with the socialist societies of Earth, if they suddenly formed quickly. As I have already said, it will be our socialism in many ways. Centuries of national fragmentation, mutual misunderstanding, crude and bloody struggle could not pass without a trace – they will leave deep traces in the psychology of liberated Earthly humanity for a long time; and we do not know how much barbarism and narrow-mindedness the socialists of Earth will bring with them into their new society. Before us is the experience that allows us to judge how far the psychology of Earth is from us, even in its best representatives. From the last expedition, we brought one Earthly socialist with us, a man who was outstanding in his environment for his spiritual strength and physical health. And what then? Our entire life turned out to be so alien to him, so contradictory to his entire organization, that after a very short time, he became ill with a deep mental disorder. This is one of the best, whom Manni himself chose from many. What can we expect from the rest?

Thus, the same dilemma remains: either the cessation of our own reproduction, and with it the weakening of the entire development of our life, or the colonization of Earth, based on the extermination of its entire humanity. I speak of the extermination of its entire humanity because we cannot even make an exception for its socialist vanguard. No, firstly, there is no technical possibility amidst universal destruction to single out this vanguard from the remaining masses, of which it represents an insignificant fraction. And secondly, even if we managed to preserve the socialists, they themselves would then start a fierce, merciless war with us, sacrificing themselves to complete extermination, because they could never reconcile themselves to the murder of hundreds of millions of people, similar to them and connected to them by many, often very close, life ties. In the collisions of two worlds, there is no compromise here. We must choose. And I say: we can only choose one thing. Higher life cannot be sacrificed for lower life. Among Earthlings, there will not be even a few million who consciously strive for a truly human type of life. For the sake of these embryonic people, we cannot abandon the possibility of the birth and development of tens, perhaps hundreds of millions of beings of our world – people in a incomparably fuller sense of the word. And there will be no cruelty in our actions, because we will be able to carry out this extermination with far fewer sufferings for them than they themselves constantly inflict on each other. World life is one. And for it, it will not be a loss, but a gain, if on Earth, instead of its still distant semi-barbaric socialism, our socialism unfolds now – a life incomparably more harmonious in its continuous, boundless development. (After Sterni's speech, there is first a deep silence. It is interrupted by Manni, who suggests that those who hold the opposite view express themselves. Netty takes the floor.)

8. NETTY

"World life is one" – Sterni said this. And what did he propose to us? To destroy, to exterminate forever a whole peculiar type of this life, a type that we can never restore or replace later. For hundreds of millions of years, a beautiful planet lived its own, special life, not like others... And then, from its mighty elements, consciousness began to organize; rising through cruel and difficult struggles from lower stages to higher ones, it finally took on close, familiar human forms. But these forms are not like ours: they reflect and concentrate the history of a different nature, a different struggle; under them lies a different spontaneity, they contain different contradictions, different possibilities of development. An era has arrived when the union of two great lines of life can be realized for the first time. How much new diversity, what higher harmony should arise from this combination! And we are told: world life is one, therefore we should not unite, but... destroy it. When Sterni pointed out how much the humanity of Earth, its history, its morals, its psychology are unlike ours, he refuted his idea almost better than I can. If they were completely similar to us in everything, except the stage of development, if they were what our ancestors were in the epoch of our capitalism, then one could agree with Sterni: the lower stage should be sacrificed for the higher, the weak for the strong. But Earthlings are not like that; they are not only lower and weaker than us in culture – they are also different from us. Therefore, by eliminating them, we will not replace them in world development; we will only mechanically fill the void we create in the realm of life forms. It is not in the barbarism, not in the cruelty of Earthly culture that its real difference from ours lies. Barbarism and cruelty are only transient manifestations of that general prodigality in the process of development that characterizes all life on Earth. The struggle for existence there is more energetic and intense, nature continuously creates far more forms, but far more of them also perish as victims of development. And this cannot be otherwise, because from the source of life – the Sun – Earth as a whole receives eight times more radiant energy than our planet. That is why so much life is scattered and dispersed there, why so many contradictions arise in the diversity of its forms, and why the entire path of their reconciliation is so painfully complex and full of failures. In the plant and animal kingdoms, millions of species fought fiercely and quickly displaced each other, participating through their life and death in the formation of new, more complete and harmonious, more synthetic types. So it was in the human kingdom. Our history, when compared to the history of Earthly humanity, seems surprisingly simple, free from deviations, and correct to the point of schematic regularity. The accumulation of elements of socialism proceeded calmly and continuously – small property owners disappeared, the proletariat rose from stage to stage; all this happened without fluctuations and jolts, throughout the planet, united into a coherent political whole. There was struggle, but people somehow understood each other; the proletariat did not look far ahead, but the bourgeoisie was not utopian in its reactionism either; different eras and social formations did not mix to such an extent as happens on Earth, where in a highly capitalist country feudal reaction is sometimes possible, and a numerous peasantry, lagging in its culture by a whole historical period, often serves the upper classes as a tool for suppressing the proletariat. By a smooth and steady path, we arrived a few generations ago at a social structure that liberates and unites all forces of social development. The road taken by our Earthly brothers was not like that – thorny, with many twists and interruptions. Few of us know, and none of us can clearly imagine, to what madness the arts of torturing people were driven by the most cultured peoples of Earth in the ideological and political organizations of the domination of the upper classes – in the church and the state. And what is the result? Has development slowed down? No, we have no reason to assert this, because the first stages of capitalism, before the emergence of proletarian socialist consciousness, passed amidst confusion and cruel struggle of various formations not more slowly, but faster than with us – in gradual and calmer transitions. But the very severity and ruthlessness of the struggle generated such an upsurge of energy and passion in the fighters, such a force of heroism and martyrdom, which our ancestors' more moderate and less tragic struggle did not know. And in this, the Earthly type of human life is not lower, but higher than ours, although we, older in culture, stand at a much higher level. Earthly humanity is fragmented, its individual races and nations are deeply rooted in their territories, they speak different languages, and a deep misunderstanding of each other permeates all their life relationships... All this is true, and it is true that the universal human union, which is forcing its way through all these borders with great difficulty, will be achieved by our Earthly brothers comparatively much later than by us. This fragmentation arose from the vastness of the Earthly world, the richness and diversity of its nature. It leads to the emergence of many different points of view and nuances in understanding the universe. Does all this place Earth and its people lower, rather than higher than our world in analogous epochs of its history? Even the mechanical difference in languages they speak has, in many ways, helped the development of their thinking, freeing concepts from the crude power of the words by which they express themselves. Compare the philosophy of Earthlings with the philosophy of our capitalist ancestors. Earth's philosophy is not only more diverse but also more subtle, not only originates from more complex material but, in its best schools, analyzes it more deeply, more correctly, establishing the connection between facts and concepts. Of course, all philosophy is an expression of weakness and fragmentation of knowledge, inadequacy of scientific development; it is an attempt to give a unified picture of being, filling in gaps in scientific experience with propositions; therefore, philosophy will be eliminated on Earth, just as it has already been eliminated with us, by the monism of science. But look, how many propositions of philosophy created by their advanced thinkers and fighters, warn in rough outlines of the discoveries of our science – such is almost all the social philosophy of the socialists. It is clear that peoples who have surpassed our ancestors in philosophical creativity can subsequently surpass us ourselves in scientific creativity. And Sterni wants to measure this humanity by the count of the righteous – conscious socialists, whom it now contains – wants to judge it by its current contradictions, and not by the forces that have generated and will resolve these contradictions in due time. He wants to drain this turbulent but beautiful ocean of life forever! We must firmly and decisively answer him: never! We must prepare our future union with the humanity of Earth. We cannot significantly accelerate its transition to a free order: but the little that we can do for this, we must do. And if we failed to protect the first emissary of Earth in our midst from the unnecessary suffering of illness, it does not honor us, but the Yuls. Fortunately, he will soon recover, and even if in the end he is killed by this too rapid rapprochement with a life alien to him, he will manage to do much for the future union of the two worlds. And we must overcome our own difficulties and dangers through other paths. New scientific forces must be directed to the chemistry of protein substances, and colonization of Venus must be prepared as much as possible. If we do not manage to solve these tasks in the short time we have left, we must temporarily reduce reproduction. What reasonable midwife would not sacrifice the life of a newborn infant to save the life of the woman? We must also, if necessary, sacrifice a particle of our life that does not yet exist for that life that exists and is developing, which is still alien. The union of worlds will infinitely repay this sacrifice. The unity of life is the highest goal, and love is the highest reason! (Deep silence. Then Manni takes the floor.)

9. MANNI

"I have carefully observed the mood of my comrades and see that a significant majority are on Netty's side. I am very happy about this, because my point of view is approximately the same. I will add only one practical consideration that seems very important to me. There is a serious danger that at the present time we may not even have sufficient technical means if we attempt mass colonization of other planets. We can build tens of thousands of large etheronefs, and it may turn out that there will be nothing to power them with. The radiomatter that serves as the necessary propellant will have to be consumed hundreds of times more than before. Meanwhile, all the deposits known to us are being depleted, and new ones are being discovered less and less often. We must not forget that radiomatter is constantly needed not only to give etheronefs their immense speed. You know that all our technical chemistry is now built on these substances. We spend them in the production of "minus-matter," without which these same etheronefs and our countless aeroplanes turn into unusable heavy boxes. We do not have to sacrifice this necessary application of active matter. But the worst thing is that the only possible substitute for colonization – protein synthesis – may prove infeasible due to the same lack of radioactive substances. Technically easy and convenient for factory production, protein synthesis, given their immense complexity, is unimaginable by old synthesis methods, by methods of gradual complication. By that path, as you know, artificial proteins were obtained several years ago, but in a negligible quantity and with great expenditure of energy and time, so that the entire work has only theoretical significance. Mass production of proteins from inorganic material is possible only through those rapid and sharp changes in chemical compositions that are achieved with us by the action of unstable elements on ordinary stable matter. To achieve success in this direction, tens of thousands of workers will have to switch specifically to research on protein synthesis and conduct millions of diverse new experiments. For this, and then, in case of success, for mass production of proteins, enormous quantities of active matter, which we do not now have at our disposal, will again be required. Thus, from whatever point of view we look, we can ensure a successful resolution of the issue that concerns us only if we find new sources of radioelements. But where to look for them? Obviously, on other planets, that is, either on Earth or on Venus; and it is undoubtedly for me that the first attempt should be made on Venus. Regarding Earth, it can be assumed that there are rich reserves of active elements there. Regarding Venus, this is fully established. Earth deposits are unknown to us, because those found by Earth scientists are, unfortunately, worthless. Deposits on Venus have already been discovered by us from the first steps of our expedition. On Earth, the main deposits are apparently located, as on our planet, deep beneath the surface. On Venus, some of them are so close to the surface that their radiation was immediately detected photographically. If we search for radium on Earth, we will have to dig up its continents as we did on our planet; this may take tens of years, and there is also a risk of being disappointed in our expectations. On Venus, all that remains is to extract what has already been found, and this can be done without any delay. Therefore, no matter how we decide the question of mass colonization later, now, to guarantee the possibility of this solution, it is my firm conviction that a small, perhaps temporary, colonization of Venus must be undertaken immediately, with the sole purpose of extracting active matter. The natural obstacles are, of course, enormous, but we will not have to overcome them completely at all. We only have to master a small patch of this planet. In essence, the matter comes down to a large expedition that will have to stay there for years, not months, as our previous expeditions, engaged in radium extraction. It will, of course, be necessary to wage an energetic struggle with natural conditions simultaneously, protecting ourselves from the deadly climate, unknown diseases, and other dangers. There will be great sacrifices; it is possible that only a small part of the expedition will return. But the attempt must be made. The most suitable place to start, according to many data, is the Island of Hot Storms. I have thoroughly studied its nature and compiled a detailed plan for organizing the entire undertaking. If you, comrades, consider it possible to discuss it now, I will immediately present it to you. (No one spoke against, and Manni proceeded to present his plan, thoroughly examining all the technical details. After his speech, new speakers came forward, but they all spoke exclusively about his plan, analyzing the details. Some expressed doubt about the success of the expedition, but all agreed that an attempt should be made. In conclusion, a resolution proposed by Manni was adopted.)

10. MURDER

The profound bewilderment in which I found myself precluded even an attempt to gather my thoughts. I only felt a cold pain tightening my heart like an iron ring, and before my consciousness, with the fury of a hallucination, the immense figure of Sterni with his implacable, calm face appeared. Everything else merged and was lost in a heavy, dark chaos. Like an automaton, I left the library and got into my gondola. The cold wind from the rapid flight made me wrap myself tightly in my cloak, and this seemed to inspire a new thought, which immediately solidified in my consciousness and became indisputable: I needed to be alone. When I arrived home, I immediately put it into effect – all mechanically, as if it were not me acting, but someone else. I wrote to the factory's governing board that I was taking a leave of absence from work. To Enno, I said that we needed to part for now. She looked at me anxiously and peered, and turned pale, but said nothing. Only later, at the very moment of departure, she asked if I wished to see Nellu. I answered: "No" – and kissed Enno for the last time. Then I plunged into a dead stupor. There was cold pain, and there were fragments of thoughts. From the speeches of Netty and Manni, there remained a pale, indifferent memory, as if it were all unimportant and uninteresting. Only once did a consideration flash through: "Yes, that's why Netty left: everything depends on the expedition." Sterni's individual expressions and entire phrases stood out sharply and distinctly: "We must understand necessity... a few million human embryos... the complete extermination of Earthly humanity... he is ill with a severe mental disorder..." But there was no connection, no conclusions. Sometimes I imagined the extermination of humanity as a fait accompli, but in a vague, abstract form. The pain in my heart intensified, and the thought arose that I was guilty of this extermination. For a short time, the consciousness broke through that none of this had happened yet and perhaps would not happen. The pain, however, did not cease, and the thought slowly stated again: "Everyone will die... and Anna Nikolaevna... and the worker Vanya... and Netty, no, Netty will remain, she is Martian... but everyone will die... and there will be no cruelty, because there will be no suffering... yes, that's what Sterni said... and everyone will die because I was sick... so I am guilty..." Fragments of heavy thoughts grew numb and solidified, remaining in consciousness, cold, motionless. And time seemed to have stopped with them. It was delirium, agonizing, continuous, hopeless. There were no phantoms outside of me. There was one black phantom in my soul, but it was – everything. And there could be no end to it, because time had stopped. The thought of suicide arose and slowly stretched out, but did not fill my consciousness. Suicide seemed useless and boring: could it stop this black pain that was everything? There was no faith in suicide, because there was no faith in my own existence. There was longing, cold, a hateful everything, but my "I" was lost in it as something imperceptible, insignificant, infinitely small. "I" did not exist. At times, my consciousness became so unbearable that an irresistible desire arose to rush at everything around, living and dead, to beat, to destroy, to annihilate without a trace. But I still realized that it was senseless and childlike; I clenched my teeth and held back. The thought of Sterni constantly returned and immovably settled in my consciousness. It was, as it were, the center of all my longing and pain. Little by little, very slowly, but continuously, around this center, an intention began to form, which then turned into a clear, unyielding decision: "I must see Sterni." Why, for what motives, I could not say. It was only certain that I would do it. And at the same time, it was agonizingly difficult to break out of my immobility to carry out the decision. Finally, the day came when I had enough energy to overcome this inner resistance. I got into the gondola and drove to the observatory that Sterni managed. On the way, I tried to think about what I would talk to him about; but the cold in my heart and the cold around paralyzed my thought. After three hours, I arrived. Entering the large hall of the observatory, I said to one of the comrades working there: "I need to see Sterni." The comrade went for Sterni and, returning a minute later, informed me that Sterni was busy checking instruments and would be free in a quarter of an hour, and in the meantime, it would be more convenient for me to wait in his office. I was led to the office, I sat in an armchair in front of the desk and began to wait. The office was filled with various instruments and machines, some familiar to me, some unknown. To the right of my armchair stood some small instrument on a heavy metal stand with three legs, on the table lay an open book about Earth and its inhabitants. I mechanically began to read it, but stopped at the very first phrases and fell into a state close to my former stupor. Only in my chest, along with the usual longing, was there some indefinite convulsive agitation.

I don't know how much time passed like this. Heavy footsteps were heard in the corridor, and Sterni entered the room with his usual calm, business-like air; he sat down in an armchair on the other side of the table and looked at me questioningly. I remained silent. He waited for a minute and addressed me with a direct question:
"How can I be of service?"
I continued to remain silent and stared at him motionlessly as if he were an inanimate object. He shrugged slightly and settled into his chair expectantly.
"Netty's husband..." I finally uttered with effort and semi-consciously, not really addressing him.
"I was Netty's husband," he corrected calmly: "We separated long ago."
"...Extermination... will not be... cruelty..." I continued, repeating the thought that had petrified in my brain, just as slowly and semi-consciously.
"Ah, that's what you're talking about," he said calmly. "But that's no longer a question. The preliminary decision, as you know, has been made entirely differently."
"Preliminary decision..." I repeated mechanically.
"As for my plan at the time," Sterni added, "while I have not fully abandoned it, I must say that I could not defend it as confidently now."
"Not fully..." I repeated.
"Your recovery and participation in our common work have partly destroyed my argument..."
"Extermination... partly," I interrupted, and, no doubt, all my longing and torment were too clearly reflected in my unconscious irony. Sterni turned pale and looked at me anxiously. Silence ensued. And suddenly, the cold ring of pain tightened my heart with an unprecedented, inexpressible force. I leaned back in my armchair to restrain myself from a mad scream. The fingers of my hand convulsively grasped something hard and cold. I felt a cold weapon in my hand, and spontaneous, irresistible pain turned into furious despair. I jumped up from the chair, delivering a terrible blow to Sterni. One of the tripod's legs hit him in the temple, and he slumped to the side without a cry, without a moan, like an inert body. I threw aside my weapon; it clattered and rattled against the machines. It was all over. I went into the corridor and said to the first comrade I met: "I killed Sterni." He turned pale and quickly went into the office, but there, obviously, he immediately realized that help was no longer needed and returned to me at once. He led me to his room and, entrusting another comrade there to call a doctor by telephone, and to go to Sterni himself, remained alone with me. He did not dare to speak to me. I myself asked him:
"Is Enno here?"
"No," he answered, "she left for a few days to see Nellu."
Then silence again, until the doctor arrived. He tried to question me about what had happened; I said that I did not want to talk. Then he took me to the nearest psychiatric clinic. There, I was given a large, comfortable room and left alone for a long time. This was all I could have wished for. The situation seemed clear to me. I had killed Sterni and thereby ruined everything. The Martians see in practice what they can expect from rapprochement with Earthlings. They see that even the one whom they considered most capable of entering their life can give them nothing but violence and death. Sterni is killed – his idea is resurrected. The last hope disappears, the earthly world is doomed. And I am guilty of all this. These ideas quickly arose in my head after the murder and immovably settled there along with the memory of it. At first, there was some relief in their cold certainty. And then longing and pain began to intensify again, it seemed, to infinity. Deep self-disgust joined this. I felt like a traitor to all humanity. A vague hope flickered that the Martians would kill me, but immediately the thought arose that I was too disgusting for them and their contempt would prevent them from doing so. They did, indeed, hide their disgust for me, but I clearly saw it, despite their efforts. How much time passed like this, I do not know. Finally, the doctor came to me and said that I needed a change of scenery, that I was being sent to Earth. I thought that this hid my impending death sentence, but I had no objection. I only asked that my body be thrown as far as possible from all planets: it could defile them. The impressions of the return journey are very vague in my memories. There were no familiar faces around me; I did not talk to anyone. My consciousness was not confused, but I hardly noticed anything around me. It was all the same to me.

PART FOUR

1. AT WERNER'S

I don't remember how I woke up in Doctor Werner's clinic, my senior comrade. It was an Earth hospital in one of the northern provinces, familiar to me earlier from Werner's letters; it was located a few versts from the provincial town, was very poorly organized and always terribly overcrowded, with an unusually clever steward and an insufficient, overworked medical staff. Dr. Werner waged a persistent war with the very liberal district council over the steward, over the extra barracks that she built very reluctantly, over the church that she was completing at all costs, over the salaries of employees, and so on. Patients safely progressed to final imbecility instead of recovery, and also died of tuberculosis due to lack of air and nutrition. Werner himself, of course, would have left long ago if he had not been compelled to remain by completely special circumstances related to his revolutionary past. But all the charms of the earthly clinic did not touch me at all. Werner was a good comrade and did not hesitate to sacrifice his own convenience for me. In his large apartment, allotted to him as chief physician, he provided me with two rooms, housed a young paramedic in a third adjacent room, and in a fourth, under the guise of an attendant for the care of the sick, a hidden comrade. I had, of course, no former comfort, and the supervision over me, with all the delicacy of the young comrades, was much coarser and more noticeable than with the Martians, but for me all this was completely indifferent. Doctor Werner, like the Martian doctors, hardly treated me, only occasionally gave me soporifics, and mainly concerned himself with my comfort and peace. Every morning and every evening he visited me after the bath, which caring comrades arranged for me; but he only visited for a minute and limited himself to asking if I needed anything. For many months of illness, I had completely unlearned to talk and answered him only "no" or not at all. But his attention touched me, and at the same time, I felt that I did not deserve such treatment at all and that I should inform him of this. Finally, I managed to gather enough strength to tell him that I was a murderer and a traitor and that all humanity would perish because of me. He said nothing in response, only smiled, and after that began to visit me more often. Gradually, the change of scenery had its beneficial effect. The pain tightened my heart less, the longing faded, my thoughts became more mobile, their coloring became brighter. I began to leave my rooms, walked in the garden and grove. Someone from the comrades was constantly nearby; it was unpleasant, but I understood that one could not let a murderer walk alone; sometimes I even talked to them myself, of course, on indifferent topics. It was early spring, and the revival of life around no longer exacerbated my painful memories; listening to the chirping of birds, I even found some sad peace in the thought of

that they would remain and live, but only that people are doomed to destruction. Once, near a grove, I was met by a feeble-minded patient, who was going to work in the field with a spade. He hastened to introduce himself to me, with extraordinary pride – he had a megalomania – passing himself off as a district officer, obviously the highest authority he knew during his life in freedom. For the first time in my entire illness, I involuntarily laughed. I felt the Fatherland around me and, like Antaeus, was gaining, albeit very slowly, new strength from my native land.

2. ONCE UPON A TIME?

When I began to think more about those around me, I wanted to know if Werner and the other two comrades knew what had happened to me and what I had done. I asked Werner who had brought me to the clinic. He replied that I had arrived with two young men unknown to him, who could not give him any interesting information about my illness. They said that they had accidentally met me in the capital completely ill, that they knew me before the revolution, and that at that time they had heard from me about Dr. Werner, and therefore decided to turn to him. They left the same day. To Werner, they seemed like reliable people, whom there was no reason not to believe. He himself had lost sight of me several years before and could not get any news about me from anyone... I wanted to tell Werner the story of the murder I had committed, but it seemed terribly difficult to me due to its complexity and the multitude of circumstances that would have seemed very strange to any impartial person. I explained my difficulty to Werner and received an unexpected answer from him:
"It's best if you don't tell me anything now at all. It is not beneficial for your recovery. I will not argue with you, of course, but I will not believe your story anyway. You are ill with melancholy, a disease with which people sincerely attribute to themselves unprecedented crimes, and their memory, adapting to their delusions, creates false memories. But you will not believe me either until you recover; and therefore it is better to postpone your story until then."
If this conversation had taken place a few months earlier, I would undoubtedly have seen in Werner's words the greatest distrust and contempt for me. But now, when my soul was already seeking rest and solace, I approached the matter completely differently. It was pleasant for me to think that my crime was unknown to my comrades and that the very fact of it could still be legitimately questioned. I began to think about it less and less. Recovery proceeded faster; only occasionally did attacks of the former anguish return, and always for a short time. Werner was clearly pleased with me and almost even removed me from medical supervision. Once, remembering his opinion about my "delirium," I asked him to give me a typical history of a similar illness to mine, from those he had observed and recorded in the clinic. With great hesitation and evident reluctance, he nevertheless granted my request. From a large pile of medical histories, he chose one before my eyes and handed it to me. It spoke of a peasant from a remote, secluded village, whom necessity had brought to the capital for earnings, to one of its largest factories. The life of the big city had apparently overwhelmed him, and, according to his wife, he walked around for a long time "as if not himself." Then this passed, and he lived and worked like everyone else. When a strike broke out at the factory, he was with his comrades. The strike was long and stubborn; he, his wife, and his child had to starve greatly. He suddenly "became sad," began to reproach himself for getting married and having a child, and for generally living "ungodly." Then he began to "ramble," and he was taken to the hospital, and from the hospital sent to the clinic of the province from which he came. He claimed that he had broken the strike and betrayed his comrades, as well as the "kind engineer" who secretly supported the strike and was hanged by the government. By chance, I was closely acquainted with the entire history of the strike – I was working in the capital at the time; in reality, no betrayal occurred, and the "kind engineer" was not only not executed but not even arrested. The worker's illness ended in recovery. This story lent a new shade to my thoughts. Doubt began to arise whether I had actually committed murder or, perhaps, as Werner said, it was "an adaptation of my memory to the delirium of melancholy." At that time, all my memories of life among the Martians were strangely vague and pale, in many ways even fragmentary and incomplete; and although the picture of the crime was remembered most clearly, even it somehow became confused and faded before the simple and clear impressions of the present. At times I cast aside cowardly, comforting doubts and clearly realized that everything had happened and nothing could change it. But then the doubts and sophisms returned; they helped me to get rid of the thought of the past. People so readily believe what is pleasant for them... And although somewhere deep down there remained the consciousness that it was a lie, I stubbornly indulged in it, as one indulges in joyful dreams. Now I think that without this deceptive auto-suggestion, my recovery would not have been so rapid or so complete.

3. THE LIFE OF THE HOMELAND

Werner carefully removed from me any impressions that might be "unbeneficial" for my health. He did not allow me to enter the clinic itself, and of all the mentally ill people who were there, I could only observe those incurably feeble-minded and degenerates who walked freely and engaged in various works in the field, grove, and garden; and, to tell the truth, this was uninteresting to me: I very much dislike everything hopeless, everything unnecessary and doomed. I wanted to see acute cases and precisely those who could recover, especially melancholics and cheerful maniacs. Werner promised to show them to me himself when my recovery had advanced sufficiently, but he kept postponing it. It never came to that. Even more, Werner tried to isolate me from all the political life of my homeland. Apparently, he believed that the illness itself had arisen from the severe impressions of the revolution; he did not suspect that all this time I had been separated from my homeland and could not even know what was happening there. He considered this complete ignorance simply as forgetfulness caused by my illness, and found it very beneficial for me; and he not only told me nothing himself from this area, but also forbade my bodyguards; and in his entire apartment, there was not a single newspaper, not a single book or journal from recent years: all this was kept in his office at the clinic. I had to live on a politically uninhabited island. At first, when I only wanted peace and quiet, such a situation pleased me. But then, as my strength accumulated, I began to feel increasingly confined in this shell; I started pestering my companions with questions, and they, loyal to the doctor's orders, refused to answer me. It was annoying and boring. I began to look for ways to escape my political quarantine and tried to convince Werner that I was healthy enough to read newspapers. But it was all in vain: Werner explained that it was still premature and that he would decide when I could change my mental diet. I had to resort to cunning. I had to find a like-minded accomplice among those around me. It would have been very difficult to win over the paramedic: he had too high an opinion of his professional duty. I directed my efforts towards another bodyguard, Comrade Vladimir. There was not much resistance here. Vladimir had been a worker before. Uneducated and almost a boy in age, he was a rank-and-file revolutionary, but an experienced soldier. During a famous pogrom, where many comrades perished from bullets and in the flames of fire, he fought his way through the crowd of pogromists, shooting several people and by some chance not receiving a single wound. Then he wandered illegally for a long time in various cities and villages, performing the modest and dangerous role of a transporter of weapons and literature. Finally, the ground became too hot under his feet, and he was forced to hide with Werner for a while. All this, of course, I learned later. But from the very beginning, I noticed that the young man was very oppressed by his lack of education and the difficulty of independent study in the absence of preliminary scientific discipline. I began to study with him; things went well, and very soon I won his heart forever. And the rest was easy; medical considerations were generally not well understood by Vladimir, and we formed a small conspiracy that paralyzed Werner's strictness. Vladimir's stories, newspapers, magazines, political brochures, which he secretly brought me, quickly revealed to me the life of the homeland during the years of my absence. The revolution was uneven and painfully dragging on. The working class, which came out first, initially achieved great victories thanks to the speed of its attack; but then, not supported by the peasant masses at a decisive moment, it suffered a brutal defeat from the combined forces of reaction. While it was gathering energy for a new fight and awaiting the peasant rearguard of the revolution, negotiations began between the old landowning power and the bourgeoisie, attempts to bargain and come to an agreement to suppress the revolution. These attempts were clothed in the form of parliamentary comedy; they constantly ended in failure due to the irreconcilability of the feudal reactionaries. Puppet parliaments were convened and rudely dispersed one after another. The bourgeoisie, tired of the storms of revolution, frightened by the independence and energy of the proletariat's initial actions, constantly moved to the right. The peasantry, largely revolutionary in mood, slowly assimilated political experience and illuminated its path to higher forms of struggle with the flames of countless arson attacks. The old government, along with the bloody suppression of the peasantry, tried to bribe a portion of it by selling land plots, but conducted the whole affair on such a paltry scale and so ineptly that nothing came of it. Insurgent uprisings of individual partisans and groups became more frequent every day. An unprecedented double terror reigned in the country, unseen anywhere in the world – from above and from below. The country was clearly moving towards new decisive battles. But this path was so long and full of hesitation that many became tired and even despaired. On the part of the radical intelligentsia, which participated in the struggle mainly with its sympathy, desertion was almost universal. There was nothing to regret about this, of course. But even among some of my former comrades, despondency and hopelessness had managed to take root. From this fact, I could judge how heavy and exhausting the revolutionary life had been over the past time. I myself, a fresh person, remembering the pre-revolutionary time and the beginning of the struggle, but not having experienced the full oppression of subsequent defeats, clearly saw the senselessness of burying the revolution; I saw how much had changed over these years, how many new elements had been added for the struggle, how impossible it was to stop at the existing illusory equilibrium. A new wave of revolution was inevitable and not far off. However, it was necessary to wait. I understood how agonizingly difficult the work of my comrades was in this situation. But I myself did not rush to go there, independently even of Werner's opinion. I thought it better to conserve strength so that it would be sufficient when it was needed in full. During long walks in the grove, Vladimir and I discussed the chances and conditions of the upcoming struggle. I was deeply moved by his naively heroic plans and dreams; he seemed to me a noble, sweet child destined for the same simple, unpretentious, beautiful death of a fighter as his young life. The revolution marks glorious sacrifices for itself and paints its proletarian banner with good blood! But Vladimir was not the only one who seemed like a child to me. Much that was naive and childish, which I had not seemed to notice or feel before, I found in Werner, an old revolutionary worker, and in other comrades I remembered. All the people I knew on Earth seemed to me like half-children, adolescents, vaguely perceiving life in themselves and around themselves, half-consciously surrendering to internal and external elemental forces. In this feeling, there was not a drop of condescension or contempt, but a deep sympathy and brotherly interest in people-embryos, children of young humanity.

4. THE ENVELOPE

The hot summer sun seemed to have melted the ice that enveloped the life of the country. It was awakening, and the lightning flashes of a new storm were already flashing on the horizon, and muffled thunder began to be heard from below again. And this sun and this awakening warmed my soul and lifted my strength, and I felt that soon I would be as healthy as I had never been in my life. In this vaguely cheerful state, I did not want to think about the past and it was pleasant to realize that I was forgotten by the whole world, forgotten by everyone... I expected to be resurrected for my comrades at a time when no one would even think to ask me about the years of my absence, – when everyone would be too busy, and my past would sink for a long time in the stormy waves of a new tide. And if I happened to notice facts that cast doubt on the reliability of these calculations, anxiety and disquiet arose in me, and an indefinite hostility towards everyone who might still remember me. One summer morning, Werner, returning from his rounds of patients at the clinic, did not go to the garden to rest, as he usually did, because these rounds were terribly tiring for him, but came to me and began to ask me in great detail about my well-being. It seemed to me that he was memorizing my answers. All this was not entirely usual, and at first I thought that he had somehow accidentally penetrated the secret of my little conspiracy. But from the conversation, I soon saw that he suspected nothing. Then he left – again, not to the garden, but to his office, and only after half an hour did I see him walking in his favorite dark alley through the window. I could not help but think about these trifles, because there was nothing larger around me in general. After various conjectures, I settled on the most plausible assumption: that Werner wanted to write someone – apparently at a special request – a detailed report on my health. Mail was always brought to him in the morning in his office at the clinic; this time he must have received a letter with an inquiry about me. It was necessary to find out from whom the letter was and why, and immediately, for my peace of mind. It was useless to ask Werner – he, for some reason, obviously did not find it possible to tell me, otherwise he would have said so himself, without any questions. Did Vladimir know anything? No, it turned out he knew nothing. I began to devise a way to get to the truth. Vladimir was ready to help me in any way. He considered my curiosity perfectly legitimate, and Werner's secrecy – unfounded. Without hesitation, he conducted a thorough search of Werner's rooms and his medical office, but found nothing interesting. "One must assume," said Vladimir, "that he either carries this letter with him or has torn it up and thrown it away." "And where does he usually throw torn letters and papers?" I asked. "In the basket that is in his office under the desk," Vladimir replied. "All right, in that case, bring me all the scraps you find in that basket." Vladimir left and soon returned. "There are no scraps there," he reported, "but here is what I found there, the envelope of a letter received today, judging by the postmark." I took the envelope and looked at the address. The ground swam under my feet, and the walls began to fall on me... The handwriting of Netty!

5. CONCLUSIONS

Among the chaos of memories and thoughts that rose in my soul when I saw that Netty had been on Earth and did not want to meet me, only the final conclusion was clear to me at first. It arose as if by itself, without any noticeable logical process, and was beyond any doubt. But I could not limit myself to simply carrying it out as quickly as possible. I wanted to motivate it sufficiently and clearly for myself and for others. Especially I could not reconcile myself to the fact that Netty would not understand me and would take for a simple impulse of feeling what was a logical necessity, what inevitably followed from my entire history. Therefore, I had to first consistently tell my story, tell it for my comrades, for myself, for Netty... Such is the origin of this manuscript of mine. Werner, who will read it first – the day after Vladimir and I disappear – will take care to have it printed, of course, with all the necessary changes for security. This is my only will to him. I deeply regret that I will not have the opportunity to shake his hand in farewell. As I wrote these memories, the past became clear to me, chaos gave way to certainty, my role and my position became clearly defined before my consciousness. In sound mind and firm memory, I can now draw all the conclusions... It is absolutely indisputable that the task entrusted to me proved to be beyond my strength. What was the reason for the failure? And how to explain the mistake of the insightful, deep psychologist Manni, who made such an unfortunate choice? I recall my conversation with Manni about this choice, a conversation that took place in that happy time for me when Netty's love inspired me with boundless faith in my own strength. "How," I asked, "did you, Manni, come to recognize me as the most suitable for the mission of Earth's representative from the mass of diverse people of our country whom you met in your search?" "The choice was not that broad," he replied. "Its scope had to be limited from the very beginning to representatives of scientific revolutionary socialism; all other worldviews are much further from our world." "Let it be so. But among the proletarians who form the basis and main force of our direction, could you not have found what you needed most easily among them?" "Yes, it would have been most reliable to search there. But... they usually lack one condition that I considered necessary: a broad, multifaceted education, at the height of your culture. This diverted the line of my search in another direction." Thus spoke Manni. His calculations did not justify themselves. Did this mean that he had no one to take at all, that the difference between the two cultures constituted a necessary abyss for an individual and only society could overcome it? To think so would perhaps be comforting for me personally, but I have serious doubts. I believe that Manni should have re-examined his last consideration – the one concerning the worker comrades. On what exactly did I fail? The first time it happened was that the mass of impressions of an alien life, its grandiose richness, flooded my consciousness and washed away the lines of its shores. With Netty's help, I survived the crisis and coped with it, but was not the crisis itself exacerbated and exaggerated by that heightened sensitivity, that refinement of perception, which is characteristic of people of socio-intellectual labor? Perhaps for a nature that was somewhat more primitive, somewhat less complex, but organically more stable and strong, everything would have gone more easily, the transition would have been less painful? Perhaps for an uneducated proletarian, entering a new, higher existence would not have been so difficult, because although he would have had to learn more anew, he would have had to unlearn much less, and it is precisely this that is the hardest... I think so, and I think that Manni made a miscalculation here, giving the level of culture more importance than the cultural force of development. The second time, what my spiritual forces broke against was the very character of the culture into which I tried to enter with my whole being: I was crushed by its height, the depth of its social connection, the purity and transparency of its relations between people. Sterny's speech, crudely expressing the entire incommensurability of the two types of life, was only a pretext, only the last push that threw me into that dark abyss to which the contradiction between my inner life and the entire social environment, at the factory, in the family, in communication with friends, was then spontaneously and irresistibly leading me. And again, would not this contradiction have been much stronger and sharper precisely for me, a revolutionary intellectual, who always performed nine-tenths of his work either simply alone, or in conditions of one-sided inequality with his fellow collaborators, as their teacher and leader, – in an environment of isolation of my personality among others? Could not the contradiction have been weaker and milder for a person who experiences nine-tenths of his working life, albeit in a primitive and undeveloped, but still in a comradely environment, with its perhaps somewhat rough, but real equality of collaborators? I think so; and I believe that Manni should have renewed his attempt, but in a new direction... And then for me there remains what was between the two collapses, what gave me energy and courage for a long struggle, what now allows me to draw its conclusions without a sense of humiliation. This is Netty's love. Undoubtedly, Netty's love was a misunderstanding, a mistake of her noble and ardent imagination. But such a mistake turned out to be possible, no one can take that away, and nothing will change it. In this, for me, is a guarantee of the real closeness of the two worlds, of their future fusion into one unseen, beautiful, and harmonious. And as for myself... but there are no conclusions here. A new life is inaccessible to me, and I no longer want the old one: I no longer belong to it with my thought or my feeling. The way out is clear. It's time to finish. My accomplice is waiting for me in the garden; here is his signal. Tomorrow we will both be far from here, on the way to where life boils and overflows, where it is so easy to erase the boundary between the past and the future, hateful to me. Farewell, Werner, old, good comrade. Long live the new, better life, and greetings to you, its bright ghost, my Netty!

Alexander Alexandrovich Bogdanov (1873–1928) – a Russian writer, economist, philosopher, and natural scientist. In 1908, he completed and published his best science fiction work – the novel "Red Star," which can be considered a precursor to Soviet science fiction. At the same time, he conducted active revolutionary work in close contact with V. I. Lenin. In 1913–1917, he created the two-volume work "Universal Organizational Science," in which he put forward a number of ideas that were later developed in cybernetics: principles of feedback, modeling, systemic analysis of the subject of study, etc. After the October Revolution, A. Bogdanov dedicated himself to work in biology and medicine. In 1926, he headed the world's first Blood Transfusion Institute and died after an unsuccessful experiment on himself in 1928. A. Bogdanov's utopian novel "Red Star" was first published by the St. Petersburg publishing house "Partnership of Art Printers" in 1908. It was then reissued in 1918 and 1929.
