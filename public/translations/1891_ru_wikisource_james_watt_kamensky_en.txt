James Watt. His Life and Scientific-Practical Activity
A Biographical Sketch by A. V. Kamensky
With a portrait of James Watt, engraved in Leipzig by Gedan

Chapter I. Origin and Childhood

He to whom the steam engine, in its current form, owes its existence and introduction into everyday practical life, was born and lived in Scotland in the second half of the eighteenth century. The honor of being the birthplace of such a remarkable person as James Watt belongs to a small but active town at the mouth of the River Clyde – Greenock, in whose town hall there is a monument to him with the inscription: "Born in Greenock in 1736, died in 1819," and where a public library bearing his name exists.

Thus, the entire active and mature period of Watt's life fell in the second half of the 18th century, a remarkable era of flourishing science and industry. Discoveries were being made in all branches of knowledge, and inventions appeared in whole dozens; the genius of practical progress, as if dormant until then, suddenly awoke and could barely keep pace with the countless tasks life presented to it. This century gave birth to a whole series of gifted, active, and honest fighters for truth, enlightenment, and public good, and James Watt, among them, holds one of the foremost places in Scotland and England.

Of James Watt's ancestors, it is known that his great-grandfather lived earlier in another part of Scotland, in Aberdeenshire, and was engaged in agriculture. However, the nearly continuous civil wars of Montrose, which raged in Scotland at the beginning of the 17th century, cost him his life: as a defender of the Covenant, he was killed in one of the bloody skirmishes, and his land, home, and property were confiscated. Fortunately, his son, Thomas, had relatives; they took in the orphan, gave him the opportunity to learn something, and thus we read that in the middle of the 17th century, this Thomas Watt settled in the small coastal town of Cartdike, or Crawfurddike, near Greenock, and began to teach mathematics and navigation.

It is difficult to say whom and how he could teach these sciences in the Cartdike and Greenock of that time, which consisted of a few dozen houses (in the latter, there were 3800 inhabitants in 1755); it is only certain that on his tombstone he was named a professor of mathematics and that during his lifetime he enjoyed great honor in Greenock, holding positions as chief district judge, chairman of the church council, etc. Likely, as a mathematician, he rebuilt the local church, expanded the bridge, verified the city's weights and measures, and as a judge and guardian of his city's morality, he expelled comedians from Greenock, or, as we would call them – skomorokhs, fined skippers for sailing on Sundays, revellers – for sitting in taverns on Friday and Saturday after the nine o'clock bell, and so on. Clearly, he was an influential, firm, and, for his time, very educated man.

This Thomas Watt died in 1734, i.e., only two years before the birth of his famous grandson. He left two sons: John and James. The former, while his father was still alive, was appointed to the crown service, as a baronet's clerk, but for some reason soon left it and engaged in leveling the mouth of the River Clyde. It seems none of the Watts possessed the qualities of a "ink-stained soul," and all of them, to a greater or lesser extent, were drawn to science and useful knowledge. The materials related to this leveling remained in an unprocessed state after his premature death and later served his brother and nephews for the compilation and publication of the first map of the River Clyde.

Thomas's younger son, James, the inventor's father, although he did not possess the scholarly talents of his father and brother, nevertheless combined in himself and in his home such a blend of education and practicality that it could not have been better suited for the birth and development of his son's inventive genius. Modest and active by nature, he could not confine himself to any single craft: contrary to the prevailing medieval customs of the time, which prohibited free choice even in crafts, he built ships, kept a stock of anchors, masts, ropes, blocks, and similar necessary maritime supplies, engaged in trade, and even owned his own vessels. His house backed onto the riverbank, making it very convenient for this type of activity. The first crane made by James Watt (father) stood at the Greenock pier, but he also made an organ for one of the neighboring churches by order, as musical instruments were considered mathematical at the time. In his workshop, alongside carpentry benches and blacksmith forges, stood entire collections of marine telescopes, quadrants, and other navigational instruments, which he not only sold but also repaired with his own hands. He apparently did not aspire to high office, as he refused the position of chief judge and spent most of his time in his workshop or on the riverbank. At the same time, in his house, next to portraits of close relatives, hung images of the luminaries of mathematical science – Isaac Newton and Napier, the inventor of logarithms.

Regarding his wife and the mother of the younger Watt, who, it seems, had a great influence on her son, the most flattering reviews have been preserved. Descended from one of the wealthiest and most ancient local families, she received a very good education for her time. Her son may have owed his remarkable orderliness and neatness at home to her remarkable accuracy and cleanliness in all his later works; she also taught him to read. Both his father and mother apparently treated their son very reasonably. Once, when a quarrel broke out among children playing with the boy, and they turned to James's father for a verdict with noise and shouting, he calmly said: "Let James tell us what the matter is, I am accustomed to hearing the truth from him." The boy was given almost complete freedom in choosing his activities, and he was never forced to study at school. It is likely that this, along with the infectious example of his always busy father and mother, explains the tireless activity and energy that later became the most outstanding traits of Watt's character.

It was among such people that the gifted boy grew up. From his earliest years, he suffered from weak health and already experienced headaches that caused him so much trouble later in his adult life. He was not strong enough to chase after his peers in their athletic games; therefore, his childhood was not spent in noisy games with street companions, nor in mischievous journeys along rivers, fields, and mountains: he did not feel at home in any of these and, apparently, even shunned boisterous company. His only pastime was fishing behind his father's house, where he spent entire long summer days in silence and solitude. For those familiar with the structure of English and Scottish life, this alone serves as a full attestation to the boy's character. The choice of a favorite pastime among them is, as is known, a matter of great importance, and once chosen, one must faithfully and unswervingly adhere to it, like one's craft or beloved. Inconstancy in either regard is met with almost equal disdain. To be a "master of all trades" is almost synonymous with being a good-for-nothing person. Therefore, it is understandable that a favorite pastime is chosen thoroughly and must correspond to the person's character: fishing is a quiet occupation for focused people who avoid noise and bustle. Such was James Watt. On rainy and cold days, he would spend hours sitting by his working mother with a pencil or a penknife in his hands, drawing or carving. He also attended primary school, but not very regularly, which, as we have already said, his parents did not worry much about and even encouraged him to stay home and do what he liked.

It is told that once, when James was six years old, a gentleman visited his father. Seeing the boy in the room during school hours, not engaged in anything in particular, the guest turned to his father with a question:
"Why don't you send your son to school instead of letting him loaf around at home doing nothing?"
"Here you are condemning him," replied the father, clearly taking his son's side, "but look at what he is doing."
History tells that the guest approached the six-year-old boy and saw him drawing various geometric figures and solving some problem. It is customary to tell such anecdotes about great people and their early childhood, and although the historical authenticity of this case is vouched for by a contemporary account, other testimonies, also from contemporaries, of a somewhat different nature, are perhaps even more plausible. His primary schoolmates claim that Watt not only made no particular progress in school subjects but was even considered dull. This is more likely because, with his weak health, talented nature, and parental permission to do as he pleased, he was not particularly diligent in memorizing lessons. On the other hand, he could not distinguish himself by dexterity or particular success in games with his peers due to illness, nor did he strive for it. But this did not prevent him from being interested in more serious books: at about 12 years old, he had already begun to read everything he could get his hands on about natural phenomena, and was often so engrossed in thinking about what he had read that he didn't notice what was happening around him.

His aunt recounts that one day he sat at her tea table and silently examined the teapot for a whole hour: he would open and close the lid, then place a saucer or a spoon to the spout and observe the falling drops of water, until finally she remarked to him:
"What is wrong with you, James? I have never seen such an idle boy as you! Take a book and do something, sir. You have been playing with the teapot lid for almost an hour and have not said a word. Aren't you ashamed to waste time on such trifles?"

Although absent-mindedness and the ability to completely lose oneself in one's thoughts remained a trait of his character forever, this incident is explained very simply: at that very time, the boy came across one of the very few books on physics and mechanics by S. Gravesande: "Elements of Natural Philosophy." It contained something about steam, mechanics, and machines, and during this incident, his mind was completely absorbed in repeating the experiments he had just read about. The people around him clearly did not encourage him to share his thoughts with them, and he himself, by nature, was more inclined to silent contemplation by himself, at least in his youth, than to conversations with others about what greatly occupied him.

However, on the other hand, there are several facts preserved about the same boy that prove that when he was healthy and in good spirits, he was not only talkative but also capable of captivating adults with his stories. His aunt, for example, tells that once, when he was still twelve years old, his mother brought him and his brother John to Glasgow and left them to visit friends there for some time. When she returned two weeks later, the host met her with a half-joking, half-serious request to take James with them, who, with his wonderful fantastical stories, left their imaginations restless, deprived them of sleep, and made them stay up far past midnight, contrary to their usual custom of going to bed at 10 o'clock. James's brother, John, explained that his brother's teeth ached at night, and to avoid being alone, he would start entertaining the company around 10 o'clock with some semi-fictional story with fantastic adventures and captivate them all so much that they forgot the time. He also excelled in this talent later among the so-called Birmingham philosophers, or the Lunar Society, of which he was a member and the soul...

Conversely, when headaches began to torment him, he became silent, irritable, dissatisfied with everyone, and would go somewhere into the field, into the mountains. Even in his very early youth, his father gifted him a set of carpentry tools and gave him a place in his workshop, where he spent most of his free time, first reassembling and rebuilding his toys, and then making models of the things his father manufactured; even the famous Greenock crane and organ made by his father did not escape this fate and, as we shall see below, influenced the young mechanic, accustoming him from an early age to the most diverse work and techniques. All these activities, however, went hand in hand with school studies and reading books and were done not out of obligation, not by compulsion, but out of his own volition and in his free time from school and home lessons.

Systematic education for young Watt did not end with primary school, from which he hardly gained much. After that, he entered the gymnasium, where he even studied Latin and mastered it to such an extent that in his old age he liked to insert Latin proverbs and expressions into his letters.[*] At thirteen, he was transferred to the mathematics class, and from then on, his abilities began to sharply manifest not only at home, in solitude, but also at school. He began to correctly grasp geometry and made extraordinarily rapid progress in it. His reading was now no longer limited to "Elements of Natural Philosophy." He pondered everything, inquired about everything, drew, calculated, and naturally began to take an interest in and be fascinated by astronomy. His father's collection of marine telescopes, quadrants, and similar items, as well as the entire workshop, served him greatly in this regard; he could freely use them and did so with enthusiasm. If he had had toy tools at his disposal, the boy might never have found the path of precise scientific thought that later enabled him to conceive of what inventors who felt their way forward could never have imagined.

[*] – A rather amusing anecdote has been preserved about the headmaster of the Greenock gymnasium, where Watt studied, Wilson, which well characterizes the narrow sectarian morals of Scotland at that time. This Wilson was a writer and poet by calling and greatly loved his muse. But when he was proposed to the local magnates as headmaster, they directly stated that they could accept him only on one condition – "that he give up his useless and blasphemous pursuit of versification once and for all." Circumstances forced the poor poet to submit to this condition, but then he certainly gave them hell in his correspondence with acquaintances and friends, these guardians of sectarian morality.

Serious interest and a serious mind require serious means for their development, not toys, no matter how great the natural talent. And the conditions in which the young inventor grew up were entirely extraordinary. Everyone has some talents, inclinations towards something, but far from everyone becomes a gifted person. How many people are there who, in their entire lives, never find themselves, never find an occupation in which they can reveal their talents: in childhood, youth, and adulthood, they live as circumstances dictate, doing not what they are inclined to do, but what is required – and finally go to the grave without even knowing what that sacred fire of passion and creativity is, which, under better circumstances, should have illuminated the path of life for everyone.

Not far from the city and his father's house, in view of the river, on a hill, grew a small but very beautiful grove of old elms and birches. It was here that the young philosopher would retreat to think his thoughts alone with nature; for hours he would lie on his back and, through the branches of the trees, observe the movement of the clouds, gaze into the endless and silent mystery of the starry sky. Perhaps it was in this grove, more than anywhere else, that an insatiable love for nature and a thirst to penetrate all its secrets grew in his soul. His youthful dreams had full scope here. Everything read in books, learned at school, and verified by personal experience was complemented here by a fervent imagination and merged into one with living, moving, and breathing nature. Personal experience taught him precision of thought, and living nature broadened and generalized it. To stop halfway, having made such a beginning, was impossible; his creativity found fertile ground and took firm root in it.

At this time, when he was about 14 years old, illness and headaches apparently greatly hindered the boy's systematic education and took him away from regular schoolwork. During such painful periods, he especially often visited his uncle Muirhead in Glasgow (professor of ancient languages at the local university) and studied a wide variety of subjects there, especially chemistry and physics; here he conducted experiments, built new devices, and verified the observations of others that he had read about in books. At that time, he also crafted some kind of "electric machine," likely something similar to a Leyden jar, which had just been invented at the time – and all this independently, without any external help. The experimental verification of what he read or learned was his most common activity and accustomed him to not accepting anything on faith, but to seeking proof for everything, and it is not surprising that with such predispositions, he later became a remarkable scientist and inventor. Nothing can serve as such a firm foundation for our knowledge and education as experience; our intellectual building is constructed from it just as the tissues of our body are from food. Especially if this experience is undertaken and executed out of one's own volition.

But even this was not enough for Watt; at the same time, he studied anatomy and medicine – and very seriously, as far as his means and the science of the time allowed. It is enough to say that once, to the great horror of his relatives, he was caught with the head of a dead child under his arm, which he had obtained somewhere for anatomical dissection. Medicine interested him so much that he often said that if he hadn't been a mechanic and had better health, he would have definitely become a doctor. Until old age, he always treated himself and his family.

There, in his uncle's house in Glasgow, he met the most educated young people of that time in the city; here began the friendly relationships with many of them that lasted until the end of his life. Among them all, young Watt soon earned an honorable place with his clear mind, erudition, and masterful ability to conduct general conversation. On the other hand, his remarkable simplicity and modesty towards others, despite his passionate intolerance and even sarcasm towards stupidity and self-satisfaction, won him the hearts of all.

In the summer, his uncle's family often lived on Loch Lomond, in a remarkably picturesque historical area, where young Watt also often visited. Here he found completely new sustenance for his versatile mind, which he could not find either in Greenock or Glasgow. To wander through the mountains and admire them, to follow the course of a stream to its source, and to collect interesting minerals and plants along the way was not a simple pastime or way to pass the time for him, but a serious study of nature: it was here that he learned to love it. That ability, which his friend and biographer, Professor Robison, later mentioned many times – the ability to make a subject of new serious study out of anything – likely manifested itself already in the fourteen-year-old boy. Without any systematic guidance or help, he studied nature in all its forms and much more thoroughly and intelligently than many do today, with cabinets, laboratories, and collections at their disposal.

But even this was not enough: it was not only external nature that interested him; passing by the settlements of the Scottish Highlanders, he often visited them, observed their customs, inquired, made acquaintances, listened eagerly for hours to their poetic legends about the glorious past – and returned home, filled with grandiose images of the past, transported to a completely different world and a different setting.

Watt generally read a great deal, read everything that came to hand, and later often said that he had never yet read a book from which he had not gained something useful or interesting. And it's no wonder: with such a rich store of knowledge verified by experience in his youth, with the ability to draw even complex impressions, such as poetic images of the past, from life itself, any book was within his reach and did not slide over his sensitive mind but took root in it and enriched it. But when did he find time to do all this? – the reader will naturally ask. His aunt answers this in her memoirs: "He rarely got up early, but during a few hours of study, he managed to do more than ordinary people do in several days." He never hurried and always found time for his friends, for poetry, for novels, and even for newspapers. And it is not for nothing that Watt was born and lived in the age of encyclopedism. What did he not know? Both in youth and in adulthood, his versatility and thorough familiarity with literally all branches of knowledge astonished his contemporaries.

But it would be a mistake to think that, as he grew older and began to take an interest in scientific and literary matters, young Watt abandoned or neglected his work in his father's workshop. On the contrary, the more he learned, the more meaning he found in what was done there. Only now he no longer played with his toys, did not launch miniature ships, did not learn to handle tools, but worked conscientiously and helped his father. The latter, however, never intended to turn him into a simple craftsman in his business; he gave his son tools, built him a special small forge, and granted him the freedom to do as he pleased. The very presence of the boy in such a large workshop, where carpenters, joiners, blacksmiths, locksmiths, and, finally, instrument makers worked, would have been beneficial to him, even if he himself did not participate in their work. And he worked – crafting models of his father's machines, then tools, and once even undertook to cast a silver coin, which he later kept as a memento of that time. Thus, he imperceptibly learned the properties of materials, accustomed himself to managing his resources, realizing his ideas, and above all, developed that practical skill and resourcefulness without which he probably would never have gifted the world his "giant with one idea," as a certain poet called the steam engine. And it is strange to say, however diverse the work he participated in or assisted with, all of it later proved useful to him: by crafting models of his father's machines, he prepared himself for modeling his steam engine; by working with navigational and surveying instruments, he prepared himself, on the one hand, to become a master of mathematical instruments, and on the other, a civil engineer and surveyor; the latter was also greatly aided by his participation in the compilation of the map of the River Clyde after his uncle's death. Even participation in the construction of the organ was not in vain.

To all this, it must be added that his tireless head was constantly working; even during hours of visible dejection, absent-mindedness, and apathy, which accompanied his headaches, it did not rest but continued its work, as if preparing new material for brighter moments when the mind would be able to use it and thus move forward faster.

Chapter II. Watt Studies in London

This continued until James turned 18. At this time, it became clear that his father's business did not promise long-term prosperity and that both sons, James and John, would have to rely on their own strength. In view of this, it was decided that the second son would be trained in commerce[*], and James would become an optician or, even better, a master of mathematical instruments.

The question arises: why was this profession chosen, and not another? Was it because it promised greater material benefits, or because it better suited the youth's inclinations? It would be unfair to Watt's father to doubt the answer: firstly, as we will soon see, such a craft, strictly speaking, did not yet exist in Scotland, and in any case, neither James nor his father could probably count on large incomes from this craft; secondly, the entire preceding childhood of the boy proves that he always enjoyed such freedom in his activities that children do not enjoy from all parents even in our time; thirdly, finally, it is easy to see that work on mathematical instruments at that time encompassed everything to which James had natural inclinations. To successfully manufacture them, it was not enough to be a craftsman; one had to understand the science on which they were based and for which they served. The very name of this craft meant almost the same as "learned mechanic," who had to understand, if not know, every mechanism. In other words, it was one of those occupations that were not yet strictly defined at the time and represented a combination of science and manual labor that could not have been better suited to all of young Watt's habits and tastes.

[*] – Drowned in the Atlantic Ocean in 1762 while sailing on his father's ship.

Be that as it may, in the summer of 1754, James Watt went to study his craft in Glasgow. However, it soon turned out that there were no true masters of this trade there and that they had to be sought in London. Almost a whole year passed in these searches, bringing Watt only the benefit of having made several very important acquaintances at the local university, which later proved to be extremely useful to him. He naturally settled in Glasgow with his uncle Muirhead, who was then a professor of Eastern languages and Latin. His uncle introduced his nephew to other professors and, among others, to Dr. Dick, a professor of natural sciences, who particularly liked Watt and began to patronize him from the very first steps of his career. Dick had connections in London and provided the young man with the necessary letters of recommendation.

But the trip there did not take place until the summer of the following year, 1758. The steam engine did not yet exist, and therefore the future inventor of it had to travel from Glasgow to London (600 versts) not by train, but on horseback, and not for 9-10 hours, as is now the case with express trains, but for 12 days with a two-day stop halfway. Upon arrival in London, it turned out that even there it was not easy to find a teacher for the trade Watt had chosen: there were only six true masters of this trade, but they were unwilling to accept an apprentice except with all the medieval formalities and rules of apprenticeship, meaning for at least seven years with the payment of large sums of money. Finally, through the patronage of Dr. Dick's acquaintances, Watt managed to arrange with a certain Morgan for a year with the payment of 20 guineas.

All these troubles and associated expenses (considerable for those times) caused the young man much anguish: he knew that his father's affairs were not prosperous, and that these emergency expenses were a very heavy burden on him. Being very delicate by nature and unwilling to cause others to suffer for his sake, he could not come to terms with the sacrifices his education cost his father. The more diligently Watt worked and studied under Morgan, the more economical he was in his expenses. He lived with Morgan, but for the sake of economy, he had his own food and spent no more than 8 shillings a week on it, "and if one spends less than that," he wrote to his relatives, "then one's stomach would be completely undermined," meaning one would have to starve. Moreover, working for his master until 9 PM, he managed to take on side work and earn something early in the morning. It is not surprising that after such work, he had to go to bed, in his own words, with a broken body and trembling hands.

He entered apprenticeship in August; at first, he was made to do copper rulers and graduated calipers and dividers; after two months, he had already made a quadrant better than another student who had been with Morgan for two years; in April, he was already entrusted with a sector, a theodolite, and several other instruments, and by the end of his apprenticeship year, a sector with a French hinge came from his hands, the work of which "could compete in the market with any other."

For a Scotsman and a provincial like Watt, London, as a large and populous city, must have presented many interesting sights; however, during his entire apprenticeship, he went out for a walk no more than twice. To the lack of time and dislike for urban noise and bustle was added the danger of being forcibly conscripted into the navy. The fact is that at that time the East India Company was recruiting soldiers and sailors and had the right to seize anyone on the streets of London who did not belong to the medieval guilds of the City or was not a "disciple" in the full and legal sense of the word. Young Watt could easily have met this fate because, as a provincial, he had no right to live and work in London, not only as a free apprentice but also as a simple laborer. Fortunately for him and for us, this did not happen; but the future inventor's health suffered greatly from the strenuous labor and lack of fresh air, so that upon the expiration of his one-year apprenticeship, he had to flee as quickly as possible back to Scotland. And so he did. Ahead of him awaited a life of labor to support not only himself but also his father, whose affairs were becoming worse and worse. He had to establish his own trade in Scotland, on the success of which he now counted. Before leaving, he had to purchase the most necessary tools and books in London, and Watt acquired them for a full 20 pounds, which constituted almost his entire capital.

Chapter III. Watt's First Works in Glasgow

Upon arriving in Glasgow, Watt intended to spend only a few days there and then go on to Greenock to visit his relatives, but things turned out differently. Acquaintances with professors proved useful from the very first. The Glasgow University received as a gift several very valuable astronomical instruments from a certain wealthy patron, Macfarlane, who owned vast lands and his own observatory in Java and bequeathed it to the university after his death. These instruments had just been received and required disassembly, cleaning, and installation, which was entrusted to the young protégé of Dr. Dick. This was his first independent earning. And one can imagine how glad he was of such good fortune and with what ardor he undertook the work on which his entire future depended.

Watt encountered serious difficulties with his first attempt to establish a workshop in Glasgow. Medieval routine, on the one hand, and narrow self-interest, on the other, rose against the newcomer and blocked his way: the Glasgow guilds, or craft unions, objected that he was not a native or citizen of Glasgow and did not allow him to set up a workshop. It was then that the university came to his aid. In connection with the installation of Macfarlane's instruments, he was appointed master of scientific instruments at the university and was allocated a special room for his workshop within the building itself. No one could interfere with this, as the university itself was considered an independent scientific guild. Thus, for Watt, perhaps the most interesting period of his life was about to begin: he entered a new environment of people who had long attracted his attention; he made many new acquaintances and friends who were all interested in the same things as he was; he was respected by everyone, and many loved him; he was at home here.

"When I first saw James Watt," writes Professor Robison (then still a student), "I approached him expecting to meet no more than a technician, and, to my surprise, found a scholar, although as young as myself, but always ready to share his knowledge. I had pretensions to consider myself a specialist in mechanics, but was soon completely disappointed, realizing that Watt had far surpassed me... All the young people at the university who were at all interested in science knew Watt, his room soon became a regular meeting place where everyone came with all sorts of questions and doubts, not only about mechanics: linguistics, antiquities, all natural sciences, even poetry, literature, and criticism – all were discussed here with equal interest and ardor. And Watt took everything seriously, leaving no question unanswered until he was convinced either of its triviality – and then he would drop it, or of its importance – and then he would make a series of new scientific investigations out of it... In his hands, everything became the beginning of scientific work, everything turned into science... With his generally recognized intellectual superiority over his peers, there was a surprising naive simplicity and openness in Watt's character that made attachments to him very strong. His superiority was softened by impartiality and constant readiness to recognize the merits and virtues of everyone."

His financial affairs did not go so successfully. The university, which gave him shelter, could not provide him with enough work to support himself and the workshop, and the demand for mathematical instruments in Glasgow was too low; moreover, the workshop's location away from the city center was not at all conducive to the rapid development of a new business; fame, or a name, in a new place, not only in those difficult times but also now, is not acquired quickly. Thus, from the summer of 1757 to the end of 1759, that is, for more than two years, the orders received by the workshop barely covered expenses, and poor Watt often had to ask his father for help himself. That is why it was a very difficult period in his life, and he repeatedly considered leaving Glasgow and moving to some port city where there was a greater demand for navigational instruments.

However, at the end of 1759, he managed to enter into a partnership with a certain Craig, who understood almost nothing about the business himself, but contributed a sum of money and undertook to keep the books. The entire capital of the partnership now consisted of labor and ready-to-sell instruments worth a thousand rubles, plus a thousand in cash, meaning a total of two thousand rubles. From this time on, Watt's affairs took a completely different turn: the demand increased so much that soon, besides himself, one permanent and three temporary hired workers were employed in the workshop, with Watt himself receiving a regular salary of 360 rubles a year. Five years later, the work was enough for 16 people, and the workshop's gross income reached six thousand rubles.

It should be said, however, that at this time, i.e., around 1763, Watt married the daughter of a Glasgow citizen and, having thus gained the right to open his workshop in the city center, moved from the university premises to a more populated and commercial part of Glasgow. This modest success did not come easily to him. He had to undertake all sorts of tasks besides his mathematical instruments! He was commissioned to do leveling, estimates, and plans for city structures; he was approached for repairs or even orders for instruments and mechanisms he had never made. If medicine at that time was considered a trade alongside barbers and dentists were not distinguished from ordinary blacksmiths, then it is not surprising that an optician was often approached for musical instruments. And is it not strange that in several places in Scotland, violins and even organs made by the inventor of the steam engine are still preserved to this day? But most interesting is that he made them, relying more on intuition and theory, as he was so devoid of any musical ear that he was never able to distinguish two musical tones from each other. There are blind people who learn to read, or deaf-mutes who can speak with signs; but for a person who cannot distinguish two notes to be able to make and build musical instruments himself, and with such success that even strict critics marveled at their accuracy – this was hardly heard of before Watt.

But even this was not enough: while building instruments and studying the theory of sound, he made a scientific discovery that would have done credit to a specialist. Here is how, according to Robison, Watt made his first organ. "The Masonic Lodge in Glasgow needed an organ. The organizers were our acquaintances. We all knew that Watt was incapable of distinguishing two tones from each other, but we were still sure he could do anything, and we suggested he take on this job. He agreed, but before starting to make the actual organ, he made a large model, incorporating a thousand different small devices and improvements, indicators, regulators, and the like. Then, starting with the instrument itself, he first studied the theory of music, mastered the theory of imperfect consonances, and learned to tune the organ to any tone with its help, without knowing music at all, doing so in a way that amazed our best performers. Incidentally, he invented a real monochord with a constant tone and, with its help, made observations that fully confirmed Blunchley's theory of string vibrations."

All this, of course, proves Watt's extraordinary gift, but it by no means testifies to the ease with which he achieved success in life; he was not one of those people who easily undertake what they do not know. And who can say what would have become of this immense talent if oppressive petty need had continued to weigh him down at the very time he was drawn to work on his ideas in mechanics. Fortunately, this did not happen, but something else entirely did.

In 1764, a professor of natural sciences at Glasgow University commissioned Watt to put in order and repair a small working model of the then-current fire engine (Newcomen's), which for some reason did not always work equally well, and sometimes refused to function at all. It had been sent to London to local craftsmen, but from there it returned with the same defects it had before; evidently, the metropolitan experts could not figure it out. Now, as Watt began to gain a reputation as a very skillful and resourceful mechanic, the university administration decided to entrust him with inspecting, and if possible, repairing the model.

At first, Watt approached this task as any minor repair in a mechanism. But, intrigued by the difficulties encountered, he undertook to solve the problem with his usual persistence, made a serious scientific investigation of it, and found that the model's defects belonged not to itself, but to the imperfect principle on which the then-current steam (or fire) engine of Savary and Newcomen was generally based, and after two years of labor, he discovered the new principle of the modern steam engine.

This, in a few words, is the entire history of the discovery that led to colossal advances in mechanics in our century and brought worldwide fame to James Watt. What exactly this new principle consisted of, in contrast to the old one, the reader will see below. Now, justice demands a caveat that it is only in this sense that Watt can be called the inventor of the steam engine. In reality, the steam engine (or fire engine, as it was called then) existed long before Watt and was even used in factory practice, especially in most coal mines and various ore mines for pumping water from shallow depths. Nevertheless, it was a clumsy, very imperfect mechanism, and steam itself remained a mysterious force that man could use in some cases, but never control. Watt was the first to explain the action of steam in a steam engine with the then-newly discovered theory of latent heat and built a mechanism that made it possible to use the natural properties of steam in the most optimal and economical way for practical human purposes. In other words, he finally subjugated the power of steam to man, and the steam engine in his hands transformed from a clever mechanical toy into a scientifically precise mechanism that did exactly what man intended it to do. But to clearly understand how exactly Watt achieved this, it is necessary to briefly trace the historical development of this mechanism before him.

Chapter IV. Watt's Predecessors

People had been familiar with the power of steam for a very long time. Even in ancient books, they wrote about it. The Alexandrian scholar Heron, 120 years before our era, described the so-called Aeolus's sphere, which spun on its horizontal axis when heated by the force of steam escaping from it. This, of course, was merely a toy without practical significance, but nevertheless, the power of steam, on which it was based, undoubtedly attracted people's attention even then. And from then until our day, in all centuries and in all countries, constant attempts were made to utilize this force, to make it perform useful work for man. These attempts gradually became more complex, more successful, until finally they took the form of that fire engine that immediately preceded Watt and his time.

To whom the honor of inventing such a mechanism belongs first remains disputed to this day. The English, with great justification, attribute it to the Marquis of Worcester (1663), then Captain Savary (1702), and blacksmith Newcomen (1704); the French, not without reason, claim a significant share of this honor for their compatriots: Salomon de Caus (1630) and Denis Papin (1674); the Germans, however, can justly demand recognition of participation in the invention of the steam engine for the famous Magdeburg burgomaster Otto von Guericke (1672), who immortalized his name with the Magdeburg hemispheres. But in reality, like all such disputes over the honor of invention, this dispute can never be resolved. Accidentally and often completely independently of each other stumbling upon different aspects of the same complex phenomenon, all these inventors and scientists, and even more so their compatriots, claim exclusive honor for discovering the entire phenomenon, which they did not discover. Only narrow national self-love and vanity can compel many biographers to this day to close their eyes to the simple consideration that none of their famous compatriots understood the operation of the steam engine as a whole – otherwise, Watt would not have needed to invent it; but, on the other hand, if they had not made their private contribution to the science of steam and to the practice of steam mechanisms, neither Watt nor his contemporaries would have had to invent and explain the principle of the modern steam engine. If this is kept in mind, the dispute is completely useless.

However, what was done by each of Watt's predecessors?

In 1663, the famous "Century of Inventions" by the Marquis of Worcester was published in London. In this original book, in the most enthusiastic and boastful terms, a certain "water-managing" machine is described, which the author considers nothing less than the eighth wonder of the world. Stumbling by chance upon a method of raising water to a certain height by heating a vessel fitted with a vertical tube, he, like all enthusiastic utopians and dreamers, imagined that he had already found the secret of the perpetuum mobile and solved all problems of motion and mechanical work. But in reality, having spent forty years working on the model and all his fortune on it, he left posterity not even a decent drawing of what he invented. His descriptions are so vague and convoluted that it is impossible not only to build any machine from them but even to clearly understand what its invention consisted of.

Salomon de Caus, a Frenchman who lived even earlier than the Marquis of Worcester, sometimes in England, sometimes in Germany, sometimes in France, published in 1613 the book "Les Raisons des Forces Mouvantes" (The Reasons of Moving Forces), in which he describes, again, a device that is somewhat more similar to a steam engine than Aeolus's sphere.

The work of another famous French doctor, the Huguenot Denis Papin, was of much greater significance for the history of the steam engine. He built an apparatus, the so-called Papin's steam cylinder, or simply Papin's boiler, which was a truly important step in the theory of the steam engine, but in practice, it apparently had no influence on its improvement. The idea for this steam cylinder was borrowed from the air pump, already known to the scientific world at the time. Everyone knew that if you take a cylinder closed at one end, insert a tight piston into it, and then somehow rarefy the air under the piston, this piston will rush into the created vacuum with such force that it can lift weights, move loads, and so on. The whole question was how to create a vacuum most conveniently and easily (a complete vacuum, of course, could not be achieved then).

To achieve this goal, Papin devised the following device. It is a hollow metal cylinder (two and a half inches in diameter and 4 feet long), closed at one end. A piston with a square rod enters it; the latter passes through a square opening in the cylinder lid. A little water is poured onto the bottom of such a cylinder, under the piston, and the piston is lowered until it touches the water. Then the cylinder is heated from below. As steam forms, the piston rises by the force of its elasticity, and the load placed on it rises with the piston. In Papin's device, this load was equal to 60 pounds. Thus, a load of 60 pounds was lifted by some force hidden inside a small tube, which weighed no more than 5 ounces. Such an effect, of course, had to make a strong impression on the minds of those times. Then, to cool the steam and create a vacuum under the piston, Papin proposed simply removing the heat. Such a lack of resourcefulness in the simplest practical techniques, of course, proves not only the impracticality of the inventor himself but also that his apparatus was never developed and applied to any practical purpose.

But however doomed to failure Papin's idea was, it undoubtedly marked a big step forward, and since an attempt was made to combine in one mechanism the ability of steam to create a vacuum with the properties of an air pump, it became much easier to transition from this combination to the force of steam and make it move a piston. This was the task of subsequent English inventors.

The first in their ranks is Captain Savary. His machine consisted of a separate steam boiler and an egg-shaped receiver connected by a tube with a valve. Steam was admitted into this receiver from the boiler, cooled, and created a vacuum. To do this, cold water was poured onto it. Furthermore, the same egg-shaped receiver was connected, by means of a tube equipped with a valve, to the mine's water-lifting pipe. As soon as a vacuum formed in the receiver, the valve separating the receiver from the water-lifting pipe opened, and the water, rushing there, filled it almost to the top. Now this valve was closed, and communication with the steam boiler was opened. Steam, under high pressure in the boiler, rushed into the receiver and pushed out the water, which had nowhere to go but up through the continuation of the water pipe into the cistern.

The demand for mechanisms for pumping water out of mines and pits was so great at that time that several similar systems existed, and the "fire" engine was considered the best. Savary's machine was improved by the blacksmith Newcomen. Later, various improvements were made to Newcomen's machine, such as spraying cold water under the piston and automatic opening and closing of valves with the movement of the beam. But despite all these changes and improvements, Newcomen's machine still remained, strictly speaking, not a steam engine, but an air engine, and worked extremely unevenly with terrible hissing and noise, making no more than 8 strokes per minute: sometimes it suddenly accelerated without any reason, hissed, wheezed, and shook the workshop, then stopped completely, quieted down, and, like a stubborn horse, refused to move for anything, while consuming a terrible amount of coal. In coal mines, this was not paid attention to, but in ore mines and other places, this made the machine completely unusable.

Despite all this, it probably would have continued to be used for another thirty years, and Watt's improvements would have remained a voice crying in the wilderness, if circumstances had not changed in the mining industry itself. Just at that time, shallow mines began to be depleted, and it became necessary to go deeper into the earth. This meant that water would fill them with doubled force, and if no intensified means of pumping it out were found, then most industrialists would face the risk of ruin, and thousands of workers – unemployment.

Such was the state of the steam engine by Watt's time.

Chapter V. The Invention of the Steam Engine

According to Watt himself, from the time the University of Glasgow commissioned him to repair the steam engine model, which happened in the winter of 1763, he had almost no dealings with the application of steam. True, in 1759, Robison, then still a student, drew his attention to the idea of applying steam to move carriages, but nothing came of it – the idea had not yet "matured." In 1762, he himself conducted some experiments with Papin's boiler and pump, from which he made something like a model of Savary's steam engine; this model could lift a load of up to 15 pounds with a nozzle diameter of only one-third of an inch. But he soon abandoned these experiments, seeing that a machine built on a similar principle would have the same, and even greater, defects as Savary's machine, i.e., it would burst boilers and damage pipes.

This was the extent of all his knowledge (apart from bookish knowledge, of course, which was also very little at the time), his entire experience. And if, having undertaken the famous model, he achieved such brilliant results, it was only thanks to his habit and ability – as his friend Robison put it – to draw serious scientific conclusions from any issue, to turn everything he touched into science.

This is how the inventor himself describes the repair of this small model. "At first, I began to repair it simply as a mechanism, and when it was done, I made it work. But then I was struck by the fact that the model's steam boiler could not supply enough steam, although by its size, it was obviously larger than required. By increasing the fire, one could make the model perform a few strokes, but at the same time, it required an enormous amount of water to be injected, although the load lifted by the small machine was small. Still, it was soon found that with a decrease in load, the model's boiler could supply steam to the cylinder, and it could work quite correctly, even with a moderate amount of injected water." In other words, it became clear that most of the steam was wasted without any action, and the machine's performance did not correspond at all to the boiler's dimensions. It was necessary to find the cause of this steam loss, and consequently, of heat.

"Pondering this phenomenon, I concluded that its cause must be sought in the law discovered not long before: that in a vacuum, water boils not at 80° Réaumur, but at a much lower temperature, even at 30°, and that, consequently, at temperatures above 30°, water in a hot cylinder must turn into steam on its own and interfere with the formation of a complete vacuum, and therefore counteract the atmospheric pressure on the piston from the outside." To definitively confirm the correctness of his conclusion, Watt conducted a series of experiments on the boiling of water and the elasticity of water vapor at various temperatures and found that the former increases much faster than the latter[*]. Expressing the obtained numbers in the form of a mathematical curve and applying it to the case of absolute vacuum in his machine, he saw that to obtain a sufficiently perfect vacuum, an enormous amount of water must be injected into the cylinder, which would almost completely cool it, and that to heat it in the next stroke of the piston would require as much steam as the boiler could not provide. From this, it became clear to him that the old engineers were quite right in being content with a load of no more than 6-7 pounds per square inch of piston in their "fire engines." In other words, these machines, by their very design, could not withstand a greater load.

[*] – When temperatures increase in an arithmetic progression, vapor pressures increase in a geometric progression.

Thus, Watt had solved the riddle of Newcomen's model. Now it remained to find a way to avoid the disadvantages of the old principle, that is, to eliminate the enormous loss of steam, heat, and, consequently, fuel. But Watt was not only a capable mechanic but also a scientist; he could not seek a new practical principle without understanding what requirements this principle must meet and where to find it. Therefore, first of all, it was necessary to study the laws of steam formation, it was necessary to find out how much heat is required to form such an amount of steam and how much steam is necessary to produce such work. From the previous considerations, it was clear to him that the numbers given in Desanglier's book were incorrect, and they needed to be corrected, and Watt undertook two series of experiments, which he himself did not consider scientifically precise, but which fully satisfied him, especially since he clearly saw the magnitude of possible error.

The aim of the first experiment was to determine how many times a water mass of the same weight occupies a larger volume as steam than as liquid. The conclusion was that in the form of steam, water occupies at least 1800 times more space than in the form of liquid. These figures gave him the opportunity to determine how large the steam boiler should be and how much water it should evaporate to fill a cylinder of a certain size with steam. Indeed, he immediately constructed a boiler that allowed him to see how much water evaporated in it with each stroke of the piston. Practice in this case differed significantly from theory: the boiler had to produce several times more steam than required to fill it according to theory.

The second series of experiments had a different goal: by injecting water into a cylinder filled with steam, he noticed with surprise that a relatively small amount of water, used to form this steam, could heat a very large amount of injected water to boiling. The aim of the second series of experiments was to determine if this was indeed the case, and what the numerical ratio was. As a result, it turned out that water, turned into steam, can heat six times the amount of water to boiling. "Amazed by this phenomenon," says Watt, "I mentioned it to my friend Black (professor of chemistry and natural sciences at Glasgow University), who then explained to me his theory of latent heat, which he had proposed not long before, and with which I was completely unfamiliar." This theory fully explained Watt's last observation, that when steam condenses into water, latent heat is released, which heats the injected water.

"I spent a lot of time and much more money than my circumstances allowed on such experiments," continues the inventor, "and yet the new principle was not found. However, the entire problem so occupied my mind, and my circumstances so strongly urged me to recover the time and resources spent, that finally, after many attempts to solve it, I arrived at what was like an axiom: in order to make a perfect steam engine, it is necessary that the cylinder be always as hot as the steam entering it; but, on the other hand, the condensation of steam to form a vacuum must occur at a temperature not higher than 30° Réaumur." It would seem that from this axiom to the practical conclusion that the condensation of steam should not occur in the cylinder but in some other vessel, there was only a small step; but it was precisely this step that the inventor could not take, and much time passed before his thought finally resolved into a new truth.

But remarkable is the form in which the new truth first appeared to him. It was not a simple logical deduction, such as we have just made, by combining two parts of his own axiom; no, it appeared to him in the form of a hypothesis, similar to the one that led Copernicus to a true understanding of the solar system. One day, in early 1765, after Sunday lunch, when Scottish hypocrisy prescribed idleness, but when Watt's restless head was nevertheless working intensely, he went for a walk in the park. Suddenly, in the middle of the field, with astonishing clarity, a question formulated itself: what if communication were suddenly opened between the cylinder containing steam and another vessel in which a vacuum had already formed, what would happen! The answer was immediately found: steam would rush into the vacuum until equilibrium was established in both vessels. And if cold water were injected into the second vessel? The steam would condense in it, and a vacuum would again form, into which the remaining steam from the cylinder would rush again until all the steam condensed and a vacuum formed in both vessels. This entire series of questions and answers flashed through his head with the speed of lightning and, in the words of his biographer, "filled him with delight." The great discovery that began a new era in mechanics, industry, and our civilization had been made. This happened at the end of April 1765.

Now it remained to reconcile all the details with it, test everything as a whole on a model, build a new steam engine according to this pattern, and put it into operation. But let's not get ahead of ourselves. The discovery had indeed been made, but before it entered life, Watt had to spend no more and no less than ten years of the most persistent labor, the most arduous struggle with countless obstacles and difficulties. Financial difficulties, of course, were at the forefront. Watt's own funds were utterly negligible, and it was not easy to find people who would grasp the true meaning of his discovery, believe in its potential success, and invest the money it required. And when such people were found, they expected too rapid success, miscalculated their strengths, and inadvertently delayed the further development of the business for several years. But there were other obstacles. The inaccuracy of mechanical work at the time and the lack of good craftsmen almost doomed the new machine to premature death. Had Watt not been his own first and best mechanic, this could easily have happened. But that's for later.

His mind continued to work. "When a vacuum, or almost a vacuum, had formed in both vessels, where should the injected and condensed water and air that entered with the steam and water be placed? Two answers seemed possible to me: either to equip the second vessel with a tube 34 feet high, through which the water could drain independently of atmospheric pressure [*], or to replace this tube with water and air pumps, which would automatically pump out both air and water from the second vessel as soon as the steam condensed. Furthermore, in Newcomen's machine, a layer of water was poured onto the piston to prevent steam and air from passing through. With the new principle, this became impossible, because any drop of water accidentally getting under the piston would turn into steam in a vacuum and thus destroy the vacuum. Moreover, when the piston was lowered inside the open cylinder, cold air would also enter it and cool its walls, thereby violating the basic requirement of temperature in the cylinder. To eliminate both of these inconveniences, the assumption arose – to close the upper end of the cylinder with a hermetic lid (with a hole and an additional box for the passage of the piston rod) and to conduct steam not only into the space under the piston but also above the piston, completely eliminating the effect of atmospheric pressure on the piston in the new machine."

[*] – A 34-foot column of water, as is known, balances atmospheric pressure.

Now the steam engine, for the first time, became truly a steam engine, and not a steam-air engine, as before. Finally, to eliminate the last possible cause of heat loss – through cooling of the outer cylinder walls by ambient air – it was proposed to surround it with another (outer) cylinder made of wood or some other poorly heat-conducting material, and to fill the space between the two cylinders with steam. Thus, all the main details were resolved easily and simply, and full scope was opened for the new principle.

"Once this idea of condensing steam in a separate vessel appeared," writes Watt himself, "all other improvements quickly followed as necessary conclusions; within two or three days, the invention was ready in my mind, and I immediately set about its practical implementation." First, he built a small model, with the help of which he tested his principle. It is remarkable that, according to the inventor, none of his later, much more complex and better-made machines worked as well or embodied his idea as perfectly as this hastily made small model.

Immediately after this test, he proceeded to build a large model, and the experiments, which left no doubt about the invention's benefit, were successfully repeated. "In 1768, I submitted," says Watt, "a petition for the issuance of a patent for methods of reducing steam consumption, and consequently – fuel consumption, in fire engines, which (the patent) was issued to me in January 1769." This patent includes only those parts of the invention that had been developed by that time. But many of them were made later and included in later patents or remained unpatented and, as he himself says in another note, were detrimental to his invention by improving the old Newcomen machine.

Thus, the main part of the invention was made, the principle was found and implemented in a model; even the exclusive right to use this principle was obtained. But could practical benefit be derived from it? No less than thirteen years of experiments, labor, doubts, trials, tests, struggles with physical, financial, and commercial difficulties, and obstacles passed before James Watt's steam engines came into general use and began to reward the inventor and builder not only with rosy hopes but also with material benefits.

Chapter VI. Watt the Businessman

In our time, as is known, the inventor, unless he is an Edison, gets a very unenviable share: he is pampered only until he is taken into their hands, i.e., burdened with monetary debt and forced to sell all his works for next to nothing; and then, having used his secrets and put them to use, they gradually begin to get rid of him, so as not to give him a share in the profits from his own invention. A hundred years ago, circumstances were not quite like that, and Watt probably would not have had to reap the fruits of his machine. With his credulity, aversion to any commercial dealings, with the extreme delicacy and modesty of this man, one could deceive him without much artifice or even effort. Fortunately, he had friends who closely monitored his affairs and took care of their arrangement. And it must be said truthfully – he was very fortunate in his financial partners.

Once the invention of the steam engine with a separate condenser was made (in the spring of 1765) – it was necessary to think about its practical application, and for this, funds were first needed for the development of the mechanism's details. Where to get them? Here, Watt's university friends helped him again. Professor Black had a good friend, a former doctor of medicine, and at that time a chemical and iron factory owner – Roebuck, who, it seemed, was a perfect fit for Watt. This personality in itself well characterized that time of new enterprises, unexpected discoveries, and rapid enrichments.

After completing his studies at Edinburgh University around 1747 and traveling abroad for further improvement, he began practicing in Birmingham and had enormous success. But his capable, active, and enterprising nature could not be satisfied with medical practice alone. And so, alongside it, he conducted a series of scientific research, mainly in applied chemistry. The research was as successful as his medical practice: instead of a small laboratory, a large one soon appeared, in which experiments continued on a grand scale, and then Dr. Roebuck became a major chemical factory owner. The fact is that he found a new method for the fabrication of sulfuric acid: before, it was prepared in glass retorts and was quite expensive, but Roebuck, starting to prepare it in lead chambers, thus reduced the cost of production by at least four times and increased the consumption of this extremely important technical product many times over in the shortest possible time. The financial results of such an innovation for the inventor were the most brilliant.

But rapid success, as often happens with capable but unstable natures, blinded him, made him believe blindly in his star. No new endeavor seemed too extensive and risky to him now; a thirst for extraordinary, unprecedented success appeared; everything ordinary, not requiring feverish activity, became repugnant to him and was quickly abandoned. Meanwhile, at this time, promising prospects were opening up in iron-related businesses – and Roebuck, without hesitation, bought the vast Carron mines near Glasgow. For roasting the ore, it was decided to use not charcoal, as was done before, but coal, which was supposed to bring enormous profits. Under such circumstances, it was natural to buy vast coal fields nearby. Unfortunately, it soon turned out that in both the mines and the pits, the surface layers were already depleted, and it was necessary to go deeper into the earth, and water hindered this. But what of it, means to pump out the water would be found – should one stop the construction of the exemplary Carron factory, already begun by the first engineer in England – Smeaton?

Finally, the perfectly arranged factory was ready; the first blast furnace was lit with triumph precisely on January 1, 1760. The owner himself lived nearby in the magnificent ancient castle "Kinneil," in a picturesque historical area. His name soon became known throughout Scotland and even England; his talents began to be marveled at, but cautious people hardly believed in the solidity of his enterprises. Meanwhile, water was doing its work and hindering operations at the factory. This led to Roebuck's first serious financial difficulties. Under such circumstances, Professor Black, a long-time friend of Roebuck, drew his attention to Watt's promising invention and introduced them to each other. The doctor immediately appreciated the practicality of the steam engine and clung to it as an anchor of salvation for his faltering affairs. A bold, enterprising, and generous man with means when his business interested him, such a partner was best suited to Watt's needs to develop, patent, and launch his invention.

There were so many flooded and even abandoned mines and pits in the country, so many unfinished productions, just waiting for a cheap and powerful engine, that there could be no doubt about the success of such an enterprise. But all this was in the future; for now, there were only models and a head full of prototypes of various levers, valves, pumps, which still required refinement and time – and, on top of all this, a thousand rubles, already spent by him on the invention. All this could have frightened a cautious businessman, but not Roebuck: however great the expenses for the "real business," he did not ponder them. A partnership was formed between him and Watt: he paid Watt's debt, undertook to provide all the necessary means and materials for work on the steam engine and obtaining a patent for it, and Watt – to invent and develop his inventions. For this, Roebuck had two-thirds, and Watt – one-third of the profits. It was understood, of course, that Watt would move his models from Glasgow to the vicinity of the Carron factory, where a cozy and quiet place was allocated for his workshop behind the "Kinneil" palace. The iron factory itself was given great importance: it was intended to make all parts of the machines with which the Carron factory would supply the whole world.

But in reality, something else happened. After his first model, Watt began working on a second, somewhat larger one, in which many important improvements were made, allowing it to lift a load not of 7, but of 10 pounds per square inch of piston surface; improvements followed improvements: the piston fitted more and more snugly to the cylinder walls, the condenser was further perfected, but the machine was still not ready; something was missing, something needed to be improved. Nevertheless, by the end of 1765, the factory received drawings of the parts of the new machine of actual dimensions (cylinder 24 inches in diameter and 7 feet long) from Watt. But before this order could be fulfilled, Roebuck's financial difficulties made further work by Watt impossible. Not receiving funds from Roebuck to conduct the business, Watt was forced to use only the time remaining from his other work for further experiments.

Now began again one of the most difficult periods of his life. His family at this time had already grown so much that the instrument workshop could not support it, and from Roebuck, under the circumstances, he could expect nothing. Nevertheless, as late as the end of 1766, in a letter to one of his friends, Watt expressed hope that everything spent on the machine would soon be repaid with interest. Obviously, it did not occur to him that another nine years would pass before the steam engine would start to pay for itself. Roebuck, who saw the machine primarily as an immediate means to improve his situation, was not at all prepared to wait so long for success. He was a man of the moment, interested only in what yielded immediate results. Moreover, he continued to believe in his star, which had only temporarily dimmed. Thus, the business stalled even before the first patent was obtained, which, of course, could not but trouble the inventor, who loved his machine as a child. And if all those clever people who later tried in various ways to appropriate his invention knew that this child was now lying almost a whole year unattended behind the "Kinneil" palace, they would surely not have missed the opportunity to break into the abandoned workshop and plunder Watt's treasure. Fortunately, this did not happen. For almost a whole year, he had to visit "Kinneil" only by chance; all his time was consumed by earning his daily bread, surveying, and drawing up the project for a Scottish canal. For the same reason, he had to travel to London to defend the advantages of his canal before Parliament over another. Parliament decided the case not in his favor, and it ended there.

But, on the other hand, this trip to London brought another, indirect benefit to Watt: on his way back, he visited for the first time the place where his cherished dreams were destined to be realized in 9 years; passing through Birmingham, he visited the famous "Soho" factory, where one of the most capable English merchants of that time, Matthew Boulton, produced all sorts of gold, silver, and other items and decorations. It was, in its own way, the largest and best factory in England. Its owner, an artist at heart and a remarkable businessman, spared no expense for his exemplary establishment; no later than 1765, he spent 9,000 rubles on new workshops and machines. This time, when Watt visited "Soho," Boulton was not at home, and he was shown the factory by Fothergill, a partner in one of the subsidiary businesses, and a friend of Boulton, the scholar and philosopher Dr. Small. Watt's experienced eye immediately appreciated the well-equipped factory, and it would have been strange if, under his circumstances at the time, the thought had not occurred to him: "Why is my machine lying somewhere in Scotland, in a half-forgotten workshop, and not here, in this vortex of bustling activity, where the best workers are working with the best machines, and seemingly under the best management in all of industrial Great Britain!" The interesting stories and the appearance of Small only confirmed him in these conclusions.

There is no doubt that Watt mentioned his invention in his conversation with Small and even let him understand its situation, because in Small's very first letter to him in January 1768, it is stated that both he and Boulton would not mind entering into business relations with him if he agreed to settle in "Soho." A hint was also made there about another desirable condition: that Watt should part with Roebuck. Thus, Watt was placed in a very interesting position, which any practical person would consider approximately in the following way: his beloved child, his machine, his entire capital stands and rusts at Roebuck's factory because this overly ardent businessman has overextended himself in his affairs and cannot fulfill his obligations towards him. On the other hand, he has an excellent opportunity to enter into a partnership with very active and wealthy people, in whose hands the business will surely go uphill. Do I have the right, by both divine and human law, to leave the doctor aside now?

And how does Watt act? It does not occur to him to part with Roebuck and demand compensation from him for the stoppage of work on the invention. On the contrary, he worries and is burdened by the thought that he has "dragged" the doctor into this business and made him spend a thousand rubles. He considers himself entirely indebted to Roebuck and barely dares to advise him, if the latter finds it possible, to enter into a partnership with Boulton. The first thing he did upon arriving in Scotland was to resume his experiments with the machine, made changes to the condenser, and finally achieved that the machine could perform up to twenty strokes per minute and lift a load of up to 14 pounds per square inch of piston [*]. This result was already so satisfactory, even according to Watt himself, that he decided, after informing Roebuck, to apply for a patent. The preparatory period of his work was clearly ending, and he was preparing to take an important step towards realizing his hopes.

But even here there were no stops and difficulties. Roebuck did not have the means even to obtain the patent, so Watt had to again resort to the help of his perennial friend and patron – Professor Black, who supplied him with the necessary money, despite his reputation as a miser even among the Scots.

[*] – Newcomen's machine, in its best days, never made more than 8 strokes per minute with a 7-pound load per square inch of piston, not to mention the incomparably greater coal consumption.

Finally, at the end of 1768, Watt went to London on this matter and, passing through, visited "Soho," where he met Boulton himself. They immediately liked each other very much, but could not reach any decisive agreement on the business because Watt refused to enter into even conditional negotiations without the consent of his partner. Nevertheless, a few days after his return to Scotland, he wrote to Boulton a letter – obviously with Roebuck's consent – in which he made a formal offer to enter into a partnership with himself and Roebuck for one-third of the profits. The doctor himself was to write to Boulton about the same a few days later. Obviously, Watt at that time still did not lose hope for his partner's financial recovery and did not intend to part with him. But Boulton had too good a business acumen to agree to such a half-measure. His reply to Watt can serve as a good example of the business foresight and broad perspective of this poet of industry, a contemporary of Adam Smith.

"I thank both you and Doctor Roebuck for the offer to participate in your company, but, unfortunately, the plan you propose is completely far from what I had in mind when I spoke with you. In hinting at my readiness to enter into business relations with you, I had two motives: the first – personal disposition towards you, and the second – a fondness for skillful and profitable undertakings. If I correctly understand your business, you will need money, very precise work, and extensive correspondence for the machine, if you want to achieve profit. In my opinion, to establish a solid reputation for the machine, it should not be touched by the unskilled hands of ordinary mechanics, who, due to their lack of skill, inexperience, and lack of necessary conveniences, will inevitably make it both poorly and inaccurately. To avoid this, I proposed to establish its production near my factory, on the bank of our canal, where we would build all sorts of equipment necessary for full production, and from where we could supply your machines to the whole world. With your help, we would be able to train real masters here, who (using tools that it would be unprofitable for anyone to acquire for just one machine) could produce your invention at 20% less cost than it would be under other conditions and with the same difference in precision of work as exists between the products of a blacksmith and an optician. Doctor Roebuck offers me to undertake the manufacture of the machine for three counties of England – I don't think it's worth doing that; but it is worth my while to make them for the whole world... What particularly prompted me to promise you my assistance – is the thought that you need a midwife who would help you give birth and present your offspring to the world; if I had known that you already had one, I certainly would not have offered you my services. I have decided once and for all not to undertake a task that is not under my personal supervision, and since my work here does not allow me to monitor the progress of the business in Scotland, and the doctor for the same reason cannot be present here, I have to conclude... no, I will do without it; whatever it may be, nothing will change my decision to always be ready to help you in any way I can – and although there are circumstances at present that prevent us from working together, I will hope that you or I will come up with a plan that..." – and so on. The ending is understandable. This is a true business letter written by an advanced and intelligent man of the 18th century. Did Watt himself understand his commercial interests as clearly and intelligently as they are expressed in this letter? Of course not. On what does the author base his calculation? On success, on patents, announcements, speculation, cheap materials, low wages, monopoly? Not at all; in his century, he had not even dreamed that all these springs of the modern market could have any serious significance. Truly good work, truly good and expensive tools and instruments, and real knowledge and love for the work in the entrepreneurs themselves – these are the only conditions without which, in his opinion, it was not worth undertaking the business...

Further, we will see how these people were able to realize their ideals and how true both his distrust of patents as a means of protection from predators and his view on competition turned out to be. At this point, negotiations between "Kinneil" and "Soho" stalled.

In early 1769, a cylinder was received, and by summer, the other parts of the machine ordered from the Carron factory were ready. But one can imagine the inventor's disappointment when he found that the cylinder, the most important part, upon which the success of the entire machine depended, was so inaccurate that at one end it was not even round, but oval. And this was the best work that the famous Carron factory could produce. What was Watt to do? He took a calibrated cylindrical wooden blank and began to straighten his iron cylinder with a hammer on it.

In 1770, a commercial crisis broke out in England; Roebuck's ruined affairs could not withstand it, and although they dragged on until 1773, there was no thought of continuing experiments or producing steam engines at "Kinneil." During all these four years, Watt's delicacy did not allow him to directly insist on removing Roebuck from the company, and its affairs were doomed to complete stagnation. Instead, in all his letters to Small, he repeated the same complaints in various ways: that "Roebuck's debt poisons his life," that "lost is the man whose entire fate hangs by a thread," that he would rather go bankrupt than always carry the thought that others suffer from his projects, and so on. But even more characteristic is the following remark in one of his letters: "Although," he says in 1772, "I myself have spent much more money on these experiments than my share in the machine is worth, I do not consider them as the share I have contributed to the capital, but merely as expenses for my invention." Or in another letter: "I fear I have drawn you too far into this matter, and even very indelicate; I understand that you may have some reason to think worse of me than I deserve. I assure you, everything I wrote to you about the price (of their share in the company) and otherwise, was said only in the interests of my friend the doctor, who should not..."

</document>

"...in the present circumstances, to risk nothing and who is too deeply interested in this matter. As for myself, I never thought of demanding remuneration for my part in the invention and am willing to accept, perhaps, a much smaller share than you would wish to offer me..." Indeed, it can be said: were it not for Bolton and Small being such conscientious men, Watt could easily have met the fate of all inventors, that is, complete destitution as a reward for gifting the world with the greatest invention. And what did he involve his future Birmingham partners and friends in? Perhaps they incurred some capital expenditures for him that were not later recovered, or perhaps they assumed some serious responsibility that he did not live up to? Not at all. Sympathizing with him as a person and respecting him as a gifted inventor, who, however, lacked any ability to defend himself in life's arena, they helped him in drafting the first and most important patent of 1769 for the "Method of reducing the consumption of steam and fuel in fire engines"—that is all. In fact, when Watt, at the request of Bolton and Small, showed them his own draft of the patent he intended to submit to the patent office, they were horrified: it provided, down to the smallest details in the drawings, all the information necessary to build and operate his machines. To publish such a patent would have meant helping others circumvent the inventor and use the fruits of his labor for free. Instead, as practical men, they suggested that he not submit any drawings at all, and modify the description of the machine in such a way as to avoid rivalry and piracy as much as possible. With minor corrections, both points in their text were accepted by Watt, and the patent was obtained. Whether Bolton and Small had their own benefit in this case, that is, to eventually benefit from the material fruits of this invention themselves, is difficult to judge. But even if we assume their complete disinterestedness, Watt had no reason to be burdened by their services if he himself were not so delicate with others. He considered himself indebted to everyone, and owed no one anything. Such was the tone of his relationships with friends and in all his affairs. It is not surprising that he always fulfilled his obligations in work and enterprises in the most scrupulous manner: he worked for three, spared no effort for his health, did not shy away from any menial labor (which, perhaps, was the secret of the success of his inventions), he looked after everything himself and performed twice as good work for half the cost as others. He was not only a brilliant inventor but also an exemplary worker. We will hardly be mistaken if we say that, were it not absolutely necessary for his existence, he probably would not have patented his inventions at all. He did so with many parts of his machine until bitter experience taught him that others were taking these very inventions of his to improve the old Newcomen engine with their help or directly patenting them as their own. Chapter VII. Watt the Engineer In the circumstances Watt found himself between 1768 and 1773, any occupation that provided a livelihood for him and his family was suitable. His engine stood idle, although all his thoughts were directed towards it, and the internal struggle he had to endure, agreeing to take on a matter completely foreign to him, is very instructive. Indeed, this entire period in the inventor's life, when he had to do what he did not want to do, illuminates his seemingly unsteady and unstable character from a completely new angle. It is one thing to see a person as they are, in the very niche for which nature adapted them—or in an artificial environment that has nothing to do with their inclinations, when one needs to live not as one wants, but as one can, not by inspiration, but by duty; one then feels oneself not a master, but a serf of one's life, and other people appear not as comrades in a common cause, but as enemies with whom one must struggle. The falsity of one's own situation illuminates everything around in a false light, and one sometimes loses sight of one's own guiding thread, flounders, and despairs, not knowing what to decide. It is precisely in such a situation that we see Watt in the following years. "Thanks to the disposition of the Glasgow authorities towards me," he writes to Small in 1770, "I now have to choose between continuing experiments with the engine with complete uncertainty about the future or accepting a position that is honorable for me and perhaps even profitable, the position of builder of a canal I designed myself... Despite my decision that I must dedicate all my efforts to my engine, I think that by refusing this offer, I will miss a favorable opportunity that will hardly present itself again. Meanwhile, I have a family, and I, despite my gray hairs, have not yet secured any reliable way to provide for them... All these and some other considerations led me to accept the offer..." The matter consisted of digging the so-called Monkland Canal, 9 miles long, for which there was a capital of 100 thousand rubles. Watt, as chief engineer and manager of the enterprise, was paid two thousand rubles a year—a quite good remuneration for that time. "Nothing can be more repugnant to me," he writes to the same Small on another occasion, "than this struggle and haggling with people, and yet my whole life is now devoted to this. I am ready to do any engineering work: make surveys, choose the direction of the canal, measure earth cubes, draw up cost estimates, give instructions and advice to the manager on how to carry them out—but not to deal with the workers myself, not to hire and pay them—that is not my business, and I refuse to be a manager... Nothing can be more shameful for a person than to take up a task that is not theirs. And the only quality that recommends me for this position is honesty, and even that reproaches me for occupying it for so long." At this time, 150 workers were employed on the canal, and Watt had only one foreman to supervise them, and he did everything else himself. "Despite the desperate weather," he writes in December of the same year, "I spend all my time at the canal, and I must admit, my current life is full of anxiety, not to mention physical fatigue, hunger, cold, wet feet, and the like. Despite all this, my health seems even better now than it was in the summer; although the headaches do not decrease in quantity or quality, I feel stronger, more decisive, and more energetic. Truly—get hired to plow land, and melancholy will be driven away quickly!" Finally, giving his friend an account of the work already done, he again says that for 100 thousand rubles he has already dug 7 miles of the canal. "This is on the one hand," Watt continues, "but on the other—I am extremely apathetic, my workers are not fulfilling their duties, clerks and foremen are cheating me, and I have the misfortune to see and understand this... And what angers me most is that at the same time others would have been able to do more work and perhaps with less trouble for themselves. I would rather face a loaded cannon head-on than conclude business deals and settle accounts. In short, as soon as I have to do anything with people, I feel out of place; it is enough for an engineer to fight with nature alone and see how it overcomes him at every step." It is hardly necessary to explain that he was indeed unfit to be a supervisor of works, although he worked for three and fulfilled his duties with pedantic accuracy, and that working under his supervision must have been quite unbearable. The falsity of his own situation was felt by him in the form of dissatisfaction with himself and others, and extreme demandingness and strictness towards himself turned into unbearable intolerance towards others. And he clearly felt all this because he was not created for authority. Meanwhile, circumstances forced him to engage in such work: the Monkland Canal occupied him from the summer of 1770 to the end of 1772, and only allowed him to pay off his personal debts incurred in recent years. Meanwhile, life had to go on. Rebák's affairs were not improving at all, but rather deteriorating. Following this canal came surveys and projects for several others. Then he was also commissioned to deepen the river Clyde and the harbor in Ayr, design docks in Glasgow and Greenock, build a bridge across the river Clyde, and so on, not to mention a multitude of small private jobs and consultations, which he was increasingly sought out for by people of various professions and positions. In short, Watt the engineer was beginning to gain as much fame as Watt the inventor later did. Some of his secondary inventions also date back to this period. Thus, at one time Small wrote, half-jokingly, half-seriously, that it would be good to apply his steam engine to move barges along canals. In response, Watt asks: "Have you ever thought about a spiral oar for this purpose, or are you for two paddle wheels?" To explain his idea, he attached a rough drawing of a steamship propeller and even with the same spiral pitch that is now considered most advantageous. This idea remained unapplied at the time, partly, perhaps, because Watt had many more brilliant ideas than hours in a day, especially since the steam engine itself was not yet ready. In the same year, 1770, he invented a micrometer screw for dividing a linear inch into 400, and on glass—even into 1000 equal parts. At the same time, he devised a micrometer for measuring distances, which, in principle, as it later turned out, had already been invented in France in 1667 for astronomical purposes. Watt came up with this idea completely independently of the French invention and often used this instrument in his surveys. Finally, in early 1773, Watt invented a new reflecting quadrant, also for surveying purposes—for measuring angles. In short, whatever this man took up, he found science in everything and clothed it in serious inventions. This period of Watt's life ended as sadly as it began. One day, while he was surveying for a canal in the north of Scotland, news suddenly arrived from home, urgently calling him back. His wife was dangerously ill, and when, after several days and nights of mad galloping through rain, cold, and mud, he finally arrived home, she was no longer alive—she had died in childbirth of their fourth child. The loss of this quiet companion, who had always calmed and encouraged him, deeply struck Watt: for almost a year he was in a kind of stupor and could not engage in anything serious. Of the four children left to him by her, two died in childhood, one daughter got married but also died soon, and only the eldest son, James, outlived all his relatives, remaining until 1848 the sole and honorable representative of the Watt family. Chapter VIII. The Success of the Steam Engine The death of his wife was, as it were, the last drop of bitterness in the chalice of trials of this period for Watt. His persistent complaints in recent years about constant headaches, "laziness," and "inactivity" were clearly the result of physical and moral exhaustion, reaching a point where he spoke of memory loss, gradual dullness, and the like. And then, from his ailing breast, a cry escaped: "I cannot bear to remain in Scotland any longer; I must either move to England or find some lucrative position abroad!" Thus he wrote to Small almost a year after the tragic event. This time, both his desires turned out to be possible. Rebák's affairs became completely entangled, and he was declared an insolvent debtor. Now it was useless for Watt to support him at the cost of his own success: the steam engine, along with the rest of the doctor's property, passed into the hands of the creditors' bankruptcy administration, which, as Watt himself wrote, did not give a farthing for his invention. Therefore, Bolton and Small could now easily acquire not only one-third of it but also both thirds belonging to Rebák for the debt that lay on it, i.e., 10 thousand rubles paid for Watt, and another 6 thousand rubles of Rebák's personal debt to Bolton. This was done, and the engine, along with its inventor, came into the possession of his Birmingham friend: in the summer of 1773, it was transported from "Kinneil" to "Soho" after a three-year wait for its turn. However, the true owner, its living genius, was only able to begin the interrupted series of experiments with it in the spring of the following year, 1774, and it was only by the autumn of the same year that these experiments led him to significant results; at that time, he wrote to his father from Birmingham: "My new fire engine is working well, serving its purpose better than all previous ones, and I expect the invention to be very beneficial to me." Such a result was all the more gratifying because it was achieved with the same parts that, in addition to the boiler supplied by Bolton, were made at the Carron plant. Now it was possible to consider replacing the oval cylinder, which Watt had repaired with hammers on a wooden block, with a new one. It was at this very time that, completely independently of the needs of the steam engine, a new boring machine was introduced at a mechanical plant in Chester, which made it possible to manufacture mathematically round cylinders. It was to this plant that Williamson turned to order a new cylinder 18 inches in diameter, with walls one inch thick, which was soon received. Now it was possible to assemble a real machine with a suitable cylinder and finally resolve the question—"to be or not to be?" This case with the Carron cylinder and all the delays and difficulties that arose from it with the steam engine serve as a good example of how closely all aspects of human life are interconnected and how a change in one of them inevitably requires and leads to changes in all others: however clear and useful the idea of Watt's engine was in itself, it could not have been realized at the low level of mechanical art that existed at the time of its first appearance. When Watt showed his first almost finished large engine to the then-famous engineer Smeaton, he approved of the principle and the craftsmanship, but decisively declared that it could not become generally practical because it required such skilled work as did not exist in England and would not exist for a long time. And the opinion of this engineer carried great weight, and if Watt himself had not been an excellent worker, and Bolton had not been such a master at choosing people and organizing them, and if they had not succeeded in carrying out the plan that Bolton had outlined when they had neither an engine, nor a company, nor people, that is, to create a new school of mechanics—Watt's invention would have had to wait a long time for its turn. It is not surprising that under these conditions Bolton began to doubt whether it was worth starting the production of steam engines for sale without first securing an extension of the patent. The fact is that out of the 14 years of exclusive right to use it, six had already expired by the beginning of 1775; the engine had not yet been properly tested and would certainly require many more experiments before it could gain widespread use and begin to pay for itself. Meanwhile, only 8 years remained for all this. It was decided to petition for a parliamentary act granting the inventor an exclusive right to use the steam engine for another 25 years. It was a troublesome matter, especially since strong opposition to such an act arose in Parliament, and especially among miners and factory owners. Nevertheless, there was no alternative but to use all connections and face the storm head-on. Talk began about the harm of a monopoly on the most necessary machine for national industry, about the ruin of the mining industry due to the high cost of the new engine, and so on. The most eloquent representative of such opinions in Parliament was Burke. But despite all this, in May 1775, the patent was obtained, and the future of the steam engine (and with it, its manufacturers) was secured. At the same time, Watt wrote to his father from London: "This matter has cost me much anxiety and great expense, and without the help of influential and numerous friends, I would never have managed to win it. I will stay here a few more days, and then I will return to Birmingham and begin to fulfill some already ordered machines." The last sentence shows that the new invention had by then ceased to be a private secret (to which, of course, the parliamentary process contributed significantly). In fact, the demand for a more powerful and perfect engine was so great at that time that work in many factories and mines had stopped pending the resolution of the same question: "to be or not to be" for Watt's invention. The old fire engines were too unsatisfactory, and the lack of a new engine meant ruin for many factory owners, especially copper ones, in Cornwall. That is why, long before the patent extension matter was resolved in Parliament, Bolton and Watt began to be inundated with inquiries and questions about their engine, and recently even with orders for it. However, it would be a mistake to think that from that day on, gold mountains began to pour into their pockets, and their machines set the whole world in motion. Work at "Soho" indeed began to boil; 2-3 months after the act was passed, the first new machine was ready and performed two thousand strokes per hundredweight of coal, and six months later Williamson (in Chester) received an order from "Soho" for 12 cylinders from 12 to 50 inches in diameter, and Bolton wrote to Watt that he had decided to achieve an annual output of 12-15 beam and up to 50 rotary engines from "Soho." When a famous visitor arrived at the factory in those same days, Bolton said to him, pointing to the steam engine department: "And here, sir, I produce what the whole world is looking for—power." Negotiations were even conducted to obtain a patent from the French government for the exclusive production of steam engines in France, but they only resulted in some French manufacturers visiting "Soho," getting drawings, and starting to make their own machines according to them, but unsuccessfully. At least, so claims Watt's relative and biographer, Muirhead. And despite all this, the financial side of the matter was far from brilliant: from the very beginning, new foundries and other workshops were needed, and two years after obtaining the act, Bolton estimated the commercial value of Watt's entire one-third share in the engine at only 20-30 thousand rubles, and as for working capital, the owner of "Soho" had to make incredible efforts for a long time to manage the countless expenses of his complex production and receive only hopes for the future in return. More than once Bolton was on the brink, and if he did not go bankrupt, it was only thanks to his remarkable tact and composure. There was another reason that almost changed the history of the steam engine. This was Watt's offer to move to Russia for several years. The fact is that his friend, Professor Robison, accepted an offer and went there to head the Naval School in 1770. In 1773, he invited Watt to come to St. Petersburg and undertake the construction of a steam engine to fill the docks with water, but Watt then declined the offer. Two years later, when he was formally asked, through the Russian envoy, to accept an appointment from the Russian government with a salary of 10 thousand rubles a year, his circumstances had changed so much that he hesitated for a long time, but finally, after the insistent urging of Bolton and other friends, he refused to go to the land of bears and brazen "Cossacks," for whom the law is not written [Another friend of his, the poet Darwin, wrote to him: "God, how frightened I was to hear that the Russian bear had laid its huge paw on you and was dragging you to Russia. Please, do not go there if there is any possibility. Russia is a dragon's lair: we see the tracks of many beasts that went there, but very few returning from it. I hope your fire engine will keep you here." Another argument against the trip was the story of the English engineer, Captain Perry, who, as the English say, was invited to Russia by Peter the Great and, after serving there for many years, had to seek refuge in the British ambassador's house and then return home without receiving a penny for his service. Watt was warned that the same fate might befall him]. In 1775, when Watt went to London on business regarding the patent extension, his close friend and assistant, William Small, died in Birmingham. The influence of this scholar and very good man on Watt's fate was very great. In general, if Bolton can be called the practical implementer and organizer of Watt's ideas, then Small was their guardian and protector. His death was a heavy loss for Watt, as well as for the entire close circle of friends surrounding Small. But, on the other hand, as far as Watt was concerned, burdened by a large family and important business responsibilities, he seemed to have rewarded himself for the loss of a friend, and in the following year, 1776, he remarried. His second wife, also the daughter of a Glasgow merchant, Mangregor, may not have had such charming qualities and was not bound to Watt by such close ties of love as his first wife, but in any case, she enjoyed great respect from both her husband and his friends for her good sense and strength of character. She was a thrifty Scotswoman and an exemplary housekeeper; such cleanliness reigned in her house that even a pet dog dared not cross the threshold of a room without wiping its paws on a mat specially prepared for this purpose at each door. Unfortunately, such immutable laws applied not only to dogs, children, and servants, as one might easily imagine, but also to the head of the household himself, who within the house completely renounced his supreme rights in favor of his wife. There were cases, for example, when, having talked too long with a friend, he would not notice that the scheduled bedtime had passed; then his dear half would come herself or send a servant to put out the fire without any ceremony. "It's time to go," he would say to the guest with a humble expression in such cases. This was how things were in his home, at least in his old age; whether they were so in the beginning is difficult to judge. From this wife, he had two more children, a son Gregory and a daughter Jessie—and both died as adults. Chapter IX. Watt the Mechanic The Cornish copper miners tried in vain to combat the rising water with old fire engines. Despite many important improvements made to them in recent years by Smeaton and others, they proved completely incapable of raising water to the required height. Meanwhile, these efforts came at great cost. There were many companies that invested ten, fifteen, or even twenty-eight and thirty-five thousand pounds in this struggle with water, all in vain—the water kept rising and rising. Capitalists, who had not yet gone bankrupt, faced the fatal necessity of either ceasing their operations, having written off all the money spent, or resorting to the assistance of Watt's steam engine. Most of them did the latter. Following the release of two or three first successful machines from "Soho" in 1775, the Cornish copper mine owners launched a real attack against Bolton and Watt, and they had to choose Cornwall as the main field of their activity for several years. This is why, in the years following 1775, Watt spent most of his time not in the vicinity of "Soho," in his modest house "Harper's Hill," where he had then moved his family from Scotland and where he had intended to lead a quiet and peaceful life as an inventor, thinker, and philosopher—but in inhospitable and distant Cornwall, where he had neither the time, nor peace, nor setting for a quiet life, but where, nevertheless, he accomplished a great deal. Once he had taken on the task, Watt no longer considered it possible to refuse his share of the responsibilities. His partner, in addition to steam engines, had many other matters on his hands, while Watt felt responsible only for this one matter, but he took on the most difficult part of it. And on the other hand, it could hardly have been done otherwise: Bolton's presence at the engine plant was incomparably more important than Watt's presence; meanwhile, on-site, in the mines, it was extremely important to have a person who possessed the secrets of the new mechanism perfectly and could show its work in its "true" light from the very first. In short, whether you like it or not, the machine had to be presented and demonstrated; only then could one count on the necessary number of orders. And Watt could not refuse to do all this if he sought commercial success for his invention. No one else could do it, because the remarkable assistants and craftsmen who later developed at "Soho" did not yet exist. The poor inventor had to again harness himself to the hated business harness and carry it as long as his strength lasted. Meanwhile, his physical strength did not at all meet the demands of his position. His duties were too numerous and varied. He had to rush from one mine to another, from the bottom of deep shafts to the surface of the earth and back; from account books and the desk—to meetings of directors and shareholders of copper companies. In one place a new engine was being built for him, in another—an old one was being modified; he was called there because the steam worked too fast and noisily, and here—because it was quiet. "Sometimes," he wrote, "it seems to me that my body will soon be torn to pieces and sent to all the 12 tribes of Israel." There were neither experienced mechanic workers nor reliable clerks, but ignorant people full of all sorts of prejudices all around. One had to fight everyone, be ready to repel at any moment, without, at the same time, spoiling the business. Such a life was beyond Watt's strength. Sometimes, driven to exhaustion and despair, he wrote to Bolton: "Come here yourself and settle everything! Peace of mind and deliverance from Cornwall—that is my constant prayer." This usually happened when he had to conclude some important new business contracts or insist on the fulfillment of old ones from which the Cornish wanted to get rid of. Watt would get hot, indignant, constantly rude, and did not achieve his goal. Then the always cheerful, smiling, and polite Bolton would appear and, in two or three days, settle everything—bring the owners to submission, appease the miners, calm Watt himself, and persuade him to stay at his post for a few more months, and then hastily leave for Birmingham, London, the continent, or elsewhere on extremely important and urgent business. It is hard to imagine a more unattractive, inhospitable place than the surroundings of Redruth in Cornwall, where Watt spent several consecutive years: for miles around, the land was dug up by seekers of tin and copper; all above-ground structures, scattered in disarray among ugly heaps of debris, resembled temporary huts after an earthquake more than human dwellings. No greenery in the entire area, as if everything had died out or burned down by some demonic whim, and an eternal curse weighed over the entire country; "as if the devil himself," according to the expression of a novelist describing this part of England, "had walked through this place with his fiery hooves and raked his tracks with giant rakes." To top it all off, the population was very inhospitable and rude (according to Watt's reviews, at least, which, of course, could have been personal in nature), capable, as he put it, of "eating the lard intended for the engine..." It is not surprising that in such an environment, the inventor's state of mind was far from brilliant. Needless to say, few people understood the true merits of the engine in Cornwall. "Now," Watt writes after the installation of the first engine, "everyone is happy with the speed, size, and terrible noise of the engine. Once I wanted to cut off the steam so that it wouldn't knock so much with every stroke, but the master apparently cannot sleep peacefully without this infernal roar, so I left it to the machinist's discretion. Ignoramuses are inspired by noise with the idea of power, and modesty in an engine is as little understood by them as in people." But it was even more difficult to combat the prejudices of the workers. At first, they stubbornly claimed that such an engine could not pump out so much water from such a depth. But the water receded hour by hour, and finally the mine was dry, and work resumed. Then dark rumors appeared, unrest among the minds against the new engine itself and its inventor; they began to say that it would soon take away everyone's work and leave them destitute. Petitions were even drawn up against Watt, thousands of signatures were collected and sent to Parliament (of course, they had no effect)... What nonsense, the reader will note, what ignorance! Yes, Watt thought so too and, of course, suffered. Meanwhile, to us, looking at the matter 100 years later, it is clear that the instinct of the working man was not as nonsensical as it seems at first glance. Of course, regarding the immediate causes and consequences of the introduction of the steam pump in Cornwall, all these rumors and fears had no basis: Watt's engine, by its appearance, saved not one thousand miners from unemployment, prevented the closure of more than a dozen mines flooded with water. But what about later? Whatever the consequences of introducing steam into our industry in other respects—and there are many very useful ones among them—it is undeniable that steam ushered in the era of large-scale factory production, the era of the victory of machine labor over manual labor, the era of the subjugation of living human labor to the tool, the plant, the factory, and, consequently, to the factory owner. A new force appeared—steam, and the subjugated person felt that new sacrifices would be required of him. Is he to blame that the instinct of self-preservation, the awakened consciousness (still dull) of his human dignity rebelled against the new violent sacrifice and made him hostile... to the machine? But even that was not enough. As soon as it became clear that, despite all these obstacles, the new engine would prevail, a real hunt began for it: dozens of inventors and mechanics appeared who all undertook to build "their own" machines for the factory owners at half price, in reality, however, they merely tried to imitate Watt clumsily. Almost anyone who had ever worked at "Soho" and knew anything about the engine undertook to build it. On the other hand, among the wealthy people, there were many who were ready to seize the opportunity and make profits without sharing with the inventor; some even sought opportunities to penetrate the secrets of "Soho," sending their people to nearby taverns to find out from Bolton's workers, over a pint of beer, what and how things were done there, and then used the obtained information and built their own engines by domestic means. At first, both Watt and Bolton tried not to pay attention to these predatory attempts, assuming they would die a natural death, but soon they had to think. In Cornwall itself, next to their own machines, others appeared, built by unknown people and working extremely poorly: due to incorrect dimensions, they either suddenly accelerated, or stopped completely, or worked only with a very negligible pressure, burning incredible amounts of coal. Finally, there were many who burst their boilers and broke their mechanisms to pieces. It is clear that such proximity spoiled the reputation of the steam engine throughout the district and, moreover, gave their clients, the mine owners, an excuse to refuse to pay premiums for the use of the patent. This could no longer be ignored; they had to sue, investigate, and prove, which, of course, was not only completely hateful to the poor idealist Watt, but also cost a lot of money: within only four years, up to 60 thousand rubles were spent on lawsuits and lawyers. There were many such lawsuits; Watt, of course, emerged victorious from all of them, but at what cost? To all this, one must add constant financial difficulties. The fact is that as early as 1780, Watt writes that according to their books, "Cornwall eats up all their profits, all that was received from other places, and even a part of their own money." And even in 1783, he complains: "We have remade all the machines in Cornwall, except one, and also quite a few in other parts of England, but riches still do not flow to us as one might think from the outside. The expenses of running the business are inevitably huge and have so far absorbed almost all our profits, but we hope to soon improve our situation with constant attention and an increase in the number of our machines." The conditions under which they installed their machines in the mines were that in addition to the cost of the machine itself, the mine owners had to pay them one-third or twice a year, as a premium for the use of their patent, one-third of the cost of the coal that the new machine saved them compared to the old one. This saving was calculated empirically and amounted to a fixed sum for each day and hour of the machine's operation, even for each stroke of the piston. Therefore, for correct calculation with each factory owner, it was necessary to keep an accurate count of the number of strokes made by the machine—it was because of this accounting that disagreements always arose, because the owners could not reconcile themselves with the premium and resorted to all sorts of tricks and even deception to avoid payments. Because of this, Watt often had to resort to court. Finally, he devised a special automatic counter that accurately showed the number of strokes made and installed it on each machine. Despite all this, according to the inventor, they lost a considerable amount of money on the owners. Be that as it may, by 1783, things were going much better, even according to Watt's gloomy reports; now not "all" but "almost all" profits were absorbed by expenses, and they "could already hope," and so on. It is clear that the period of financial difficulties was ending, and the period of harvest was approaching, which continued throughout the last 15 years of the 18th century, when the steam engine not only repaid all their costs and losses to Bolton and Watt, but also provided a very decent net income. In 1800, the patent for the steam engine expired, and with it, their partnership ceased. Having embarked on this venture as relatively young men, both were now old and decided to hand over the factory affairs to their children: Matthew Bolton and the young Watts, James and Gregory, between whom a partnership was formed, and the old men, still too vigorous to retire completely, devoted the rest of their lives to what interested them: Bolton—to the mint, and Watt—to copying statues, busts, and the like with his new machine, as well as to travel. Gregory Watt, who showed so much promise, to his father's great sorrow, died in 1804, and the company of the young Watt and Bolton continued to exist until the death of the former in 1848. It should be noted that after the patent expired, the production of steam engines at "Soho" began to yield not less, but much greater profits. Of course, many new mechanical factories appeared in the country, and the price of machines decreased, but Bolton's old predictions came true with remarkable accuracy: the exemplary "Soho" factory with its selected staff of relatively well-paid workers and the best machines long found no worthy rival and, with free competition, could derive greater profits from the production of steam engines than under the exclusive protection of the law. Production, of course, greatly expanded. Naturally, in 1800, the steam engine was no longer the same mechanism as it was patented in 1769. Since then, improvements followed improvements, the mechanism changed in many ways, and its applications expanded. Especially many additions to it were made by Watt between 1775 and 1785. In this decade, his talent, along with his fortieth year, reached full maturity and manifested itself with remarkable energy and perseverance as never before. Despite the fact that this was the most labor-intensive period of his life in Cornwall, inventions, one more important and original than the other, were born in his mind as if in defiance of all surrounding circumstances and external conditions. During this time, he took out five different patents and made several important inventions, which he often did not consider necessary to patent at all. Of these five patents, some are completely new, and others are for previously made inventions to avoid their appropriation by someone else. "Then," Watt wrote, "came times when people could apparently read the inventor's thoughts in his head, which he had not yet even managed to express." 1) From the very beginning of his work on the steam engine, he paid attention to the fact that in the future it should serve as a source not only of linear but also of circular motion. The first thought of building a rotary engine or steam wheel occurred to him back in 1766, at the very beginning of his experiments at "Kinneil." However, his experiments at the time led to nothing and perhaps even slowed down the success of his work on the linear engine—his attention was divided. One such mechanism was even included in the patent of 1769. But when the linear engine was finally created at "Soho" and began to be used in mines, collieries, breweries, and other factories, there arose a demand for the application of steam to other types of motion in other industries, for example, milling, which had long needed an engine that would not depend on changes in weather, season, animal power, and other variable conditions. After several attempts to develop a steam wheel, that is, a special rotary steam engine without a condenser, Watt concluded that linear motion is much easier to convert into circular motion than to build a separate mechanism for the latter. The result of this thought was five different ways of converting linear motion into circular motion, described in his second patent of 1781, relating to the steam engine. 2) In 1782, a third patent followed under the general title—"Some improvements in steam or fire engines for raising water and other mechanical purposes." In fact, this patent includes five completely independent improvements made at different times since Watt began working on the steam engine. These were, firstly, the use of steam with expansion, which was cut off before filling the entire cylinder. The first idea of this occurred to Watt in 1767. He also found at that time that when steam is cut off, i.e., when the valve of the steam pipe from the boiler to the cylinder is closed after a quarter of the piston stroke, the action is equal to more than double the amount of steam if it fills a cylinder twice as small. Such is the elasticity of steam; as it expands and rarefies, it continues to press on the piston and move it. Watt found that by allowing steam to expand in this way, fuel could be significantly saved. Secondly, the same patent describes a double-acting engine with two steam inlets on both sides of the piston, or with a double cylinder, as the author himself called it. This is the very principle that came to his mind in the first days after inventing the steam engine with a separate condenser. Thanks to this principle, his engine ceased to be half-air-powered and became fully steam-powered. Steam acts on it not on one, but on both sides of the piston and thus eliminates the effect of atmospheric pressure on it. With the same mechanism, the same expenses, and in the same amount of time, such an engine performs twice the work of a single-acting one, without depending on fuel savings due to steam cut-off. Thirdly, a double or compound engine, consisting of two or more separate engines, whose cylinders, as well as condensers, are connected in such a way that the steam used to press the piston of one can act, expanding, on the piston of another, and so on, with the pistons of both engines acting either jointly or alternately. 3) In 1777, the factory owner Wilkinson approached Watt with a question as to whether he could apply his steam engine to operate a hammer weighing 15 hundredweight, which would deliver 30-40 blows per minute. Experiments were started at the same time, initially with a hammer of 60 pounds, but since Watt had to spend almost all his time in Cornwall at that time, this matter was abandoned and not resumed until the end of 1782. However, in that year, remarkable results were obtained. Watt himself, dissatisfied with everything, wrote to Bolton: "Well, I don't think anyone has ever been able to do such a thing!" After long experiments, he managed to set up a hammer weighing 7.5-10 hundredweight, delivering up to 300 blows per minute (for practical purposes, 100 blows are quite sufficient). This was the first steam hammer, patented in 1784. In terms of its practical importance, it is second only to the very first patent of 1769. The fact is that, in addition to the main invention—the hammer—it contains a whole series of other minor ones, each of which would do credit to any mechanic. The most important of these is, of course, Watt's parallelogram, which serves to provide the piston rods with always linear and vertical motion without the help of chains and toothed sectors at the ends of the beam, as was done before. Many scholars worked on explaining the operation of this mechanism, and in practice, it still forms an obligatory part of the beam—where the latter is used. Watt himself wrote to his son later: "Although I do not seek glory, I am more proud of the parallelogram than of any other mechanical invention." Among other secondary innovations related to the steam hammer, we will mention only the most important ones; it would be completely impossible to describe them all. The valve, which allows the engine to regulate the uniform inflow of steam itself, and thus achieve uniform speed. It is connected to the centrifugal governor, which remains almost in the same form as it was in Watt's hands 100 years ago. The accuracy of this mechanism's operation is shown by the following curiosity: in a spinning mill in Manchester a few years ago, the main engine that drove the entire huge factory drove a wall clock instead of a spring or a weight, and the time of this clock perfectly coincided with other ordinary clocks with a pendulum—so uniform was the motion. A manometer showing the steam pressure in the boiler. A manometer for measuring pressure in the condenser. A manometer for indicating the steam pressure in the cylinder at various moments of the piston stroke. Here also described is the counter for counting the number of piston strokes, which was mentioned earlier. 4) In 1785, Watt proposed an innovation in factory practice that, unfortunately, still awaits its full realization. This is a smokeless furnace for industrial purposes. The smoke, before exiting into the chimney, was passed through a layer of incandescent, already burned-out, coke. In this process, the smallest particles of coal forming the smoke were heated and burned, thus saving a portion of the fuel. 5) To this decade, and even to its beginning, belongs his invention, which had nothing to do with the steam engine, but nevertheless proved to be extremely useful in commercial practice. This is an office copying press: for letters, manuscripts, and the like, without which no commercial office can do without today. These are the main inventions of James Watt, with which his name will forever be associated. But in addition, he also created many useful mechanisms and devices at various times, which were also significant in their time, although now completely forgotten. Such, for example, is the arithmetic calculating machine, on which the inventor seems to have spent a considerable amount of time and which nevertheless never had any practical application. Finally, the last invention that occupied him most in his old age was a machine for copying sculptural works, or, as he called it, an eidograph. Not being an artist and not claiming to ever learn any art, he devised a mechanical device that allowed him to copy the most intricate shapes, bas-reliefs, medallions, statues, busts, vessels, and many other things—with mathematical precision. This machine was started at the end of the last century, but was finally completed and improved by him only a few years before his death. The workshop in his house near Birmingham, where he lived in his last years, was full of copies of various famous original busts, statuettes, and the like. The old man took special pleasure in giving these beautiful little things made of marble and other materials to his friends and acquaintances, saying: "And take this as a memento from a young artist." Subsequently, this machine found numerous industrial applications, not always of such a noble character. Looking back at this entire period, encompassed by the main part of these inventions, one might think that their author did nothing but ponder, try, develop, and build all these devices, mechanisms, and machines. In reality, nothing could be more mistaken than such an assumption: all these new ideas and improvements were thought out and developed in passing, incidentally. The majority of them fell within the decade of 1775-1785, when Watt was literally overwhelmed with mandatory work on installing and modifying steam pumps in the Cornish mines. The mountains of business correspondence that he had to conduct could alone have consumed all the time of this very active man. And yet, he continued to invent more persistently than ever, using any minute to compose descriptions, patents, and drawings, which, incidentally, he always made himself and with the utmost care—it was difficult to find a more meticulous and accurate draftsman at that time. Moreover, the creative process in his brain was evidently accompanied by real labor pains. Never in his entire life did he suffer so much from headaches as during this time; never did he invent with such great suffering, and hardly ever did he succumb to such complete despair as in those days. Again and again he vowed not to invent anything more, not to undertake new projects, not to conduct new experiments, and, according to his son, he would sit at his desk for hours, resting his head on his hands and not uttering a single word. But a few days would pass, and he would again forget himself in the creative flow of his thoughts, work in the same direction, and as a result, give the world important new discoveries. His creativity proceeded not only unconsciously but also against his will. How little he was aware of what was happening within him is shown by his persistent and undoubtedly completely sincere complaints about the loss of the creative abilities he once possessed, about dullness, depression, decline of strength, and approaching old age, when he was not yet fifty years old. And all this during the period of the most intense creative activity and the triumph of his inner strength over unfavorable external conditions. Chapter X. Watt the Scientist What is particularly striking about Watt is his independence in everything he knew and did. He was, in the fullest sense of the word, self-taught. It cannot be said that there were no schools or universities where one could study natural philosophy (as experimental sciences are called in England, as if there were another philosophy—not natural). But Watt did not attend them. Bound by necessity to the lathe and workshop, he never had the leisure or means for it. Even the theory of latent heat, which was of such great importance for his inventions, he learned from Dr. Black himself, not from his lectures. Before, he had never gone through any complete systematic course in any school. Still living in his father's house in Greenock, he began to be interested in astronomy and observed the starry sky himself in a grove behind the house. Later, he began to read books in other branches of natural science—physics, chemistry, anatomy, medicine, mechanics—and verified everything he read with experiments, but without any external guidance. Chemistry was given preference over all other sciences. Thus, he continued his self-education throughout his life: he read and observed everything that came to hand, and from everything, he chose only what was important and necessary for him. This ability for quick and accurate selection, as if by instinct, was one of his remarkable talents. Another would have gotten tangled ten times in the gigantic heap of knowledge and facts acquired without any system and haphazardly, but for Watt, this only developed new abilities to sort knowledge and discard the unnecessary. From his notes on some untalented but voluminous work, the reader could always learn much more than from the author himself. And the mass of diverse information he collected was truly enormous, and not just in natural sciences—chemistry or mechanics, in which, of course, he was a specialist—but literally in all branches of knowledge, including even such areas as antiquities, metaphysics, literature, poetry, and the like. He knew European languages and was well acquainted with foreign literatures. He learned German specifically to read a then-famous book on mechanics ("Theatrum Machinarum"—"Theater of Machines" (Lat.)) when he still lived at the University of Glasgow. In the same way, he mastered Italian. Despite all this, his knowledge was remarkably precise and clear; all one had to do was give him a topic, indicate a subject on which it was desirable to converse, and one could hear from Watt a mass of remarkably interesting, well-thought-out information and facts, which he presented as easily and freely as if he had devoted his entire life to that subject. "He," as Walter Scott, his contemporary, put it, "embraced every question with his talent," and to every listener, it seemed that he was dealing with a person in whose hands everything was so clear, simple, and understandable. As we have repeatedly said, Watt was as little a scientist by profession as by education—but he created scientific works that astonished scientists. His goal was not to enrich science, however much he loved it, but to "conquer nature," and for this, "it was necessary only to find its weak spots." Science served him only as a means to achieve this. It should be noted, however, that science at that time differed greatly from what is called by that name now. Strict boundaries did not exist: heat and light were considered elementary substances, just like water, hydrogen, iron, sulfur, and the like. No later than 1781, scientists were trying to answer the question of whether heat has weight, and they answered: "Yes, but very little." Chemistry itself was just beginning to emerge from infancy, freeing itself from the prejudiced concepts of alchemists. Only in the 1780s did Lavoisier, and later other chemists, begin to use scales in their experiments, thus placing their work on the foundation of precise scientific research. Only in 1773 did Lavoisier himself contrast his rational theory of oxidation and reduction (only suspecting the existence of oxygen, which was discovered as a separate gas by Priestley in 1774) with the outdated but still dominant phlogiston theory of Stahl. Water and air were considered simple substances that could not be decomposed. Chemistry was a collection of fragmented observations of various simple and complex substances, mostly inherited from alchemists and not connected by any common unifying principles. At the end of the 18th century, these common principles were just beginning to be established. In this regard, the clarification of the transition of bodies from one state to another, i.e., from solid to liquid, from liquid to gaseous, and vice versa, was of great importance. Black's discovery of latent heat was the first important step, thanks to which the truth began to be clarified. Heat began to be seen as a latent component of all simple bodies that separate or absorb it, depending on the circumstances. It is very natural that Watt, who had to work most of his life on saving and consuming heat, on the formation of steam from water and the conversion of this steam into useful work, constantly encountered the question of the transition of bodies from one state to another, and with his ability to tackle the task and "make science out of everything," he could not help but arrive at new scientific truths. And so it was. Knowing that water, to turn into steam, must, as they then expressed it, "combine" with latent heat, and on the other hand, that the more visible heat of steam, the less its latent heat—Watt, as early as 1782, in a letter to Bolton, expressed the following hypothesis: with strong heating of water vapor, it will, in all probability, completely change its nature and turn into some kind of gas or air, as gases were then called. Approximately at the same time, many scientists, conducting experiments with the ignition of hydrogen, always noticed the appearance of moisture. Priestley was the first to notice that even when exploding a detonating mixture of hydrogen and oxygen in a closed glass vessel with an electric spark, dew appeared on its walls after cooling, very similar to water. These experiments were repeated by many English and French scientists at the time, but the appearance of dew was always explained by the precipitation of moisture from the air and the gases taken, rather than by the combination of the gases themselves into water. Finally, it was concluded that water is nothing more than a combination of one volume part of oxygen and two parts of hydrogen; that it is formed from their combination with an explosion and release of heat and decomposes into them when water vapor is heated very strongly or when an electric current is passed through water. The question is, who first came to these conclusions? All chemistry textbooks state that the honor of discovering the composition of water belongs to the English scientist Cavendish. This is confirmed by a reference to his work "Experiments on Air," dated January 1783. But this is not entirely true: this year is incorrect and was changed publicly at one time to 1784, when the work was presented to the Royal Society and published by it. On the other hand, in early 1783, James Watt's work entitled "Thoughts on the Constituent Parts of Water and on Oxygen" was presented to the same Royal Society, in which the propositions were clearly established that... "water, light, and heat are the sole products of the violent combination of hydrogen with oxygen in a closed vessel during Priestley's experiments and that, consequently, water consists of oxygen and hydrogen, deprived of part of their latent or elementary heat and—further—that pure or dephlogisticated air consists of water, deprived of its hydrogen and combined with elementary, or latent, heat and light." But, unfortunately, this manuscript lay in the Royal Society for a whole year and was read only in April 1784. An fierce dispute arose between the supporters of one and the other over the priority of discovery, with Cavendish and his party asserting that the works on which he based his conclusion about the composition of water were done back in 1781, when Priestley first noticed moisture on the walls of a closed vessel during the explosion of the detonating mixture. Meanwhile, Watt's friends insisted that in 1781 Cavendish had only repeated Priestley's experiments, but that no conclusions about the composition of water had ever been made by him, but that he learned about them from a member of the Royal Society, Blagden, from Watt's manuscript, which lay in the society for a whole year and was open to all members. Such a consideration is allegedly confirmed by Blagden's intentional alteration of the numbers, firstly, of Cavendish's aforementioned work, and secondly, of an important letter from Watt that proved the priority of his discovery. Both could have been done by Blagden because these documents were printed by the Royal Society when he was secretary of the society—for which he allegedly received 150 thousand rubles after Cavendish's death. "Who is guilty, who is right—it is not for us to judge," and it seems much better to repeat with Watt what he once said in old age: "It doesn't matter who first discovered the composition of water; what matters is that it has been discovered." But, in any case, it is important for us to know that Watt arrived at the discovery of the composition of water completely independently. It cannot, of course, be overlooked that at that time the minds of many people were occupied with this question, that this was a barrier that contemporary science and scientists had reached, and many simultaneously tried to overcome this barrier, but unsuccessfully. Watt managed to do it, perhaps because his experience and familiarity with the transition of bodies from one state to another were not bookish, nor even laboratory, but practical, life-based. Following the general course of his work, we can even outline the path his thought took, approaching the discovery of the composition of water. Constantly, for over ten years, encountering the condensation of steam into water and perfectly clearly understanding the cause of the heat release during this process, it was difficult for him not to find a similarity with this phenomenon in the explosion of the detonating mixture in Priestley's experiments: during the condensation of steam, a void remains, heat is released, and water is formed. During the explosion of gases in Priestley's experiments, there was also a void, heat was also released, and as a result, water should also have appeared. Such a conclusion must have naturally occurred to Watt, even if he had had no hints of the commonality of hydrogen and oxygen with water, which in reality he had plenty of. And he drew this conclusion. Just as it would be difficult to imagine Watt's personality in the first period of his life, the period of the invention of the steam engine without his connection with the University of Glasgow and his friends there, it is equally difficult to understand the course of his thoughts during his life in Birmingham without knowing about the "Lunar Society" and its members. By some strange coincidence, Birmingham in the seventies of the last century, i.e., during the era of general revival and awakening of thought, became the center of the chosen minds and talents of middle England. Philosophers, scientists and patrons, engineers, mechanics and inventors gathered here in a close group and called themselves the "Lunar Society," firstly, because they met once a month at one of their members' homes, in turn, around the full moon, and secondly, because these meetings, starting around two o'clock in the afternoon with dinner, ended after discussing all sorts of issues late in the evening, and members had to go home by moonlight. This was not a scholarly society in the strict sense; it would be more accurate to call it a society of free thinkers that existed not only in England. Its friendly gatherings were always dedicated to the liveliest and most heated debates on the scientific and philosophical issues of the day. Without any formalism, without deference to rank or ceremony, people of the most diverse professions and positions came together here to exchange living words about the news of science, philosophy, and everyday life that interested them and that stirred minds everywhere at the end of the 18th century. Many of the members were bound by close friendship, others were attracted by the light of intellect and knowledge of the core intimate group, which included, in addition to Watt, Small, and Bolton, the chemist Priestley, another chemist and great wit Car, the poet Darwin, the botanist Withering, the mechanic Edgeworth, the philanthropist and eccentric Day, and others. Priestley was an extremely remarkable person in himself, apart from any relation to Watt or the "Lunar Society," in which he undoubtedly played a prominent role. With enormous abilities and the most diverse knowledge in natural sciences, mechanics, metaphysics, and so on, he possessed incredible energy and perseverance in all his scholarly work; he became passionate about literally everything he took up, and he took up almost everything and suffered due to the lack of any system, his scatteredness. But energy and enthusiasm compensated for this weakness. Being a Presbyterian preacher and having a large family, he never worried about tomorrow and certainly lived in poverty. When friends once wanted to give him practical advice on how to profit from his scientific discoveries, he sharply replied that he had never derived, nor intended to derive, material benefits from science for himself. And indeed, as soon as this enthusiast found something new, even before he was himself sure of success, he would announce his discovery to all his acquaintances and friends, and it happened that some of them used his instructions and hints, developed them, and then passed them off as their own discoveries, while Priestley was left with nothing. Fortunately, in the "Lunar Society," the duties of friendship and humanity were understood differently than in scientific societies. At first, one-time subscriptions were raised for Priestley's benefit, and then all wealthy members, at the initiative of Bolton and Wedgwood, agreed to contribute a certain sum annually so that Priestley could continue his scholarly work without worrying about a piece of bread. This proved all the more feasible as his needs with his family were very modest. Needless to say, all this was done without Priestley's knowledge. Reading the friendly correspondence on this matter between Bolton, Wedgwood, and others, one doesn't know what to marvel at more in these 18th-century people: the fidelity in understanding their social duties, or the sincere sympathy, delicacy, and modesty with which they helped each other. In such a circle, any theoretical question had to be illuminated with special interest, and it is not surprising that the steam engine and various chemical issues could serve as themes for the poet Darwin's songs, and for the witty Car an inexhaustible source of his jokes, which enlivened even the driest and most abstract debates. But from some indications, it can be concluded that not all gatherings were dedicated to abstract science. In 1791, when events in France reached their highest tension, the conversations took on a completely different character. It would be an exaggeration to say that among the members of the "Lunar Society" themselves there were many revolutionaries or even sympathizers of the French Revolution, but their interest in events in France was undoubtedly very great. Not to mention that many had personal friends and acquaintances there, such as Lavoisier, Laplace, Berthollet, and so on, others, like Bolton, Watt, and Priestley, had children being educated there—they were all extremely interested in the outcome of this social crisis, the fate of the principles to which, in the depths of their souls, they could not help but feel some sympathy. It is not surprising, therefore, that despite the perfectly "well-intentioned" political views and the known religiosity of many of them, the entire "Lunar Society" in Birmingham was viewed as a gathering of Voltairians and free thinkers. Moreover, there were indeed black sheep among them: Priestley, without hesitation, expressed his sympathy for French affairs. A freethinker in church matters and a republican in politics, he had not previously enjoyed a reputation as "reliable" and orthodox, especially among the clergy. And now, when in 1791 his son arrived from Paris and enthusiastically announced to the whole society the triumph of new ideas, his excitement was transmitted to his ardent father. Now he began to thunder against the clergy and royal authority from the pulpit and openly entered into disputes with the clergy, and the Bordeaux "Society of Friends of Man" elected him, almost the only Englishman, as its honorary member. In those same days, news arrived, of course not without embellishments, that Watt's son, James, had wholeheartedly devoted himself to revolutionary affairs, became a member of the Jacobin Club, and even reconciled Robespierre with Danton as a second, and then, having quarreled with the head of the Convention, had to flee to Italy. All this was enough to outrage the guardians of antiquity in Birmingham. And so, when a company of local radicals planned a public dinner at one of the hotels, a crowd of people rushed into the house, broke the windows, smashed the furniture, and with shouts: "Down with the philosophers, long live the church and the king!"—rushed to destroy and set fire to the houses of Priestley and other Dissenters. He himself was warned in time, and he left the house with his family. Bolton and Watt at that time armed their workers at the factory and prepared to give a decisive rebuff if the crowd attacked them. However, this did not happen: most of the Dissenters lived in another part of the city, and the theater of military operations moved there. A few days later, Priestley was able to return to the city, but it was no longer possible for him to live in Birmingham, and he moved to America, where he died in the town of Northumberland in Pennsylvania in 1803. As great as the loss of the "Lunar Society" was with Priestley's departure, so much did he himself regret the loss of it: "All that I have managed to do for science," he wrote to his friends, "when I lived in Birmingham, belongs to you as much as to me." Around the same time, some other members of the "Lunar Society" also left Birmingham, and although it continued to exist until the beginning of the 19th century, it was never again as lively and active as in the period described. Among Watt's merits to science should also be included his proposal to adopt a decimal system of weights and measures common to all countries, which he addressed to prominent English and French scientists in 1783. Many met this proposal very sympathetically. The French decimal system, as is known, was developed by a special commission in 1789. Watt's system differed somewhat from the one later adopted in France, but undoubtedly expressed the needs of the time correctly. Unfortunately, the clumsy English system of weights and measures still flourishes to this day. But this is no fault of Watt's. Chapter XI. Watt in Retirement Contrary to the well-known Latin saying that "a healthy spirit in a healthy body," Watt's body throughout his life did not get along with his spirit. It ached all his life, refused to work and serve the spirit, which, like a proud owner of a ruined castle, continued to soar in ideological heights, paying no attention to the infirmities of its meager assistant. As a result, in old age, a kind of agreement was reached: the spirit reconciled itself with a calm life instead of soaring to the heights, and the frail body calmed down and began to bear its subordinate service acceptably. Indeed, with the completion of Watt's burdensome duties in the factory business, by the beginning of the 19th century, when he was already about 65 years old, his health improved, headaches and fits of melancholy ceased, and he revived; his stooped body with a forward-jutting head, sunken chest, and thin legs suddenly straightened up and began to assert its rights to existence. And his strong mind, which had not succumbed to the temptations of practical wisdom even in the most bleak moments of life, now acquired a clarity and serene calm that are rarely attained even by healthy people. And so it continued almost without interruption for the last 19 years of his life. Of course, he continued to invent; for this, he had his copying-sculpture machine, which he constantly improved. Punctually every day, after breakfast and completing his private correspondence, he would put on his leather apron and go to the workshop, where several unfinished busts, a dozen planned improvements to the machine, and a swarm of various memories associated with every old chisel, every model, and almost every speck of dust in that room awaited him. Here he continued to work, invent, read, and think about what had occupied him all his life. Evenings were devoted either to reading novels, of which he had read an amazing number in his lifetime, or to conversation with friends, of whom he had fewer and fewer left. By the way, this workshop was the only room where he was the complete master—here he could do what he wanted and when he wanted; in all other respects, his house was ruled by the immutable law of his dear half, the struggle with whom for independence was evidently as hateful to Watt's sensitive and delicate character as the war with people in the market for the right to exist. He preferred to meekly allow her to hide his snuffbox five times a day, extinguish the candles at the appointed hour when guests were present, and submit to all her regulations regarding meticulous cleanliness and tidiness, rather than stir up a storm in a teacup and poison his life. But the old man did not spend all his time at home; frequent travels, either to Scotland, or to Wales, where he had a small estate, or to London and even to the continent, gave him the opportunity to satisfy his still insatiable curiosity and observation. Arriving in a large city, for example, in London, he had to visit everything related to it: not just any novelties indiscriminately, as many lovers of sights do, but necessarily everything new in his specialty that had appeared since his last visit. At home and at his Welsh estate, he engaged in gardening, and the first thing he did upon arriving was to walk around every tree and every bush, like old acquaintances, examine them, and consult with the gardener whether anything needed to be done to make them grow, bloom, and bear fruit better. Watt was especially interesting and inimitable in society. There was literally no contemporary who personally knew him who did not marvel at his talent for captivating with his conversation and the richness of its content. He loved to talk a lot, especially in old age, and talked about literally everything with equal ease, knowledge, and interest. Nevertheless, he could not stand anything pretentious, bombastic, and boastful, and not only did he himself never play this role, but he did not hesitate to curb anyone who tried to do so in his presence. Upon meeting him in society for the first time, one might not notice him—he behaved so modestly and his figure was so inconspicuous. But as soon as he joined the general conversation (which, by the way, he never initiated himself), his remarkable talent for exposition, clarity of mind, richness, and variety of content captivated not only his peers but literally the entire society: adults surrounded him, and children crowded at his feet or sat on his knees; to some, he explained the difference in the construction of the bow, harp, and piano; with others, he argued about German poetry or philosophy; to third, he told about his travels abroad or gave advice on how to make the best and most durable paint for home use. All this was said not in the tone of a teacher or an overconfident oracle, whoever the listener might be, and was interspersed with jokes and anecdotes. His favorite state was good-naturedness, and his entire demeanor and manner of speaking expressed conscious calmness and mental strength. But modesty was still the predominant trait of his character. True, now that the goal of his life had, so to speak, been achieved, the life struggle had ceased, he rarely encountered reasons to indulge in self-abasement, to lament his weakness and inadequacy, but the obvious success of his life did not blind him in the slightest and did not change his opinion of himself. Of course, now he knew his own worth and his merits to his homeland and humanity, but this realization never made him equate his personal merits and talents with the virtues of human personality of other people. With his extremely humane and delicate intuition, he always understood that these are incomparable things: no matter how great the former might be, their moral value is completely insignificant compared to the eternal dignity of a person. That is why all Watt's biographers speak unanimously about the extraordinary delicacy of his character, about the fact that he not only always took into account the interests of others, but also their feelings, always avoided not only causing harm to anyone but also offending anyone's self-esteem. Boasting and bragging were the only exception—he could not stand them in anyone and struck them without mercy. Such delicacy in him is all the more valuable because it is generally a very rare quality among the English, and in him it should hardly be attributed to his Scottish origin. Twice in his old age he was forced to take on the position of sheriff of the county where he lived, and both times he had to use all his eloquence and connections to free himself from this official honor and obligation. It is interesting what he answered. "I am already almost 70 years old," he wrote, "my health requires me to spend most of my time at home. I have never been gifted with the decisiveness or firmness of character so necessary for public activity. I know from experience that the worries and anxieties associated with lawsuits make me completely unfit for any work; my mind and body are already worn out... I have worked hard for the benefit of society for most of my life and, I hope, not in vain: instrumenta artis nostrae are in everyone's hands. I have already served the state in the form for which nature intended me, and I hope I have earned that my homeland does not treat me so unfairly, forcing me into a position for which I am completely unsuitable, which will force me to reveal my weaknesses, and perhaps something worse." A few years before his death, the English government decided to honor Watt for all his services to his homeland with a baronetcy—an honor that in England is refused only by madmen and revolutionaries. Watt was neither the one nor the other, and yet, after some reflection, he delicately declined this offer, finding himself unfit for such a title. He never refused one kind of honor—that of membership in various learned societies—and was a member of the Royal Societies of London and Edinburgh, the Paris Academy, and many others. All young inventors considered it their right to besiege Watt with questions and requests. And what inventions were not discussed with him: there was, of course, a perpetuum mobile, and a refutation of Newton's law of gravity, and a giant iron pipe that was planned to be built on the shore, and then flooded across the sea strait to create a tunnel... One might expect, at least, that the old inventor would laugh at such foolishness. Not at all: in the kindest and calmest tone, he writes very detailed answers, in which he refutes one absurd proposal after another from young enthusiasts and finally advises not to take any measures or spend money without testing their invention on a working model. Likewise, he was always ready to help young people who showed any abilities with whatever he could. In memory of his school years in Greenock, he donated a considerable sum to the town for the purchase of scientific books and the founding of a public library, which now bears the name of its founder. Similarly, an annual prize was established at the University of Glasgow for the best scientific essay in mechanics, chemistry, and physics—in turn, in the order established by Watt himself. In this last period of his life, the old man had to lose two of his adult children—his beloved son Gregory and his married daughter Jessie. By his very nature, gentle and affectionate, he could not help but be a loving father and raise his children on the same free principles on which he himself was raised, i.e., on the principles of reasonable moral influence, advice, friendship, rather than fear, obedience, and parental authority. When Priestley first came to his family, Watt's relationship with his children charmed him, and he wrote: "Watt treats his children as friends, and they adore him as the best of fathers." With his eldest son, when he grew up, he was not particularly close, although their relationship was always very warm and familial. But with the younger one, he had a closer bond: in character and inclinations, he greatly resembled his father and promised to continue his scientific work; despite his young age, he had already completed two good chemistry papers, showed great intellectual abilities and oratorical talent. Unfortunately, he evidently inherited his father's poor health. From the age of 22, he showed signs of consumption, and no precautions could save him; life granted him only two more years, and in 1804, at about 24 years of age, he died in his father's arms. His daughter Jessie also died of the same illness, leaving several children. It is likely that these bitter losses prompted the old man to think about a new pneumatic method for treating lung diseases, i.e., introducing medications into the lungs in the form of gases. Experiments conducted in this direction seemed to yield brilliant results. And so, in Bristol, together with Dr. Beddoes, Watt founded a clinic, which greatly occupied him. However, the results did not justify the brilliant expectations. In such and similar pursuits, always active, with a host of thoughts and plans, Watt lived out his final years. The ranks of old friends around him began to thin. In 1794, his old partner and patron, Dr. Rebák, died; in 1799, his best and faithful friend Black, as well as his friend Withering, passed away; in 1802, the poet Darwin died; three years later, the devoted Professor Robison died; 1809 took away Bolton himself, and the always sickly, eternally suffering Watt lived on and on, and finally began to say that soon the world would become so empty for him that it would not hurt to part with it. And indeed, his death was the quietest and most peaceful; as if he himself came to the conclusion that he had done everything in his life that he wanted, and was parting with it without regret. Until the very last moment, he retained such freshness of mind that when he traveled to Scotland in 1817, his acquaintances there were literally charmed by him and assured that they had never seen him in such full bloom of all his intellectual abilities as on that occasion. Only a few weeks before his death did he feel unwell; but by then he surely knew that it was the approach of death, and met it on August 19, 1819, as any self-respecting person who respected life should, with full consciousness of fulfilled purpose and duty.
